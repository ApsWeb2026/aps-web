## Distinguishing Questions About Life, APS, and Biological Explanation

Contemporary biology explains living systems through many successful approaches. Mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental, ecological, physiological, informational, mathematical, organisational, cognitive, and systems approaches often address different questions and identify different explanatory dependencies.

APS does not treat this plurality as a contest in which one framework must replace all the others. Nor does the existence of different concepts or theoretical architectures by itself establish that one approach explains more than another.

Comparative explanation begins with a prior question:

> **What, exactly, is being explained?**

This matters especially on the APS website because three importantly different questions can otherwise appear to be versions of the same debate.

One concerns **Life**: what distinguishes living organisation, and whether a particular property, process, capacity, or description is sufficient to explain what Life is.

A second concerns **APS**: what kind of explanatory framework APS is, and whether familiar philosophical or theoretical labels accurately characterise it.

A third concerns **comparative explanation**: how APS relates to another explanatory approach when both address the same specified biological problem.

These questions are related, but they are not interchangeable.

> **Life is the biological organisation APS seeks to explain; APS is the explanatory framework. Claims about what Life is must therefore be distinguished from claims about what APS is and from comparisons between APS and other explanatory approaches.**

This article maps those distinctions and provides a route through the relevant APS articles.

## Why These Questions Must Be Distinguished

A statement such as *Life is not computation* makes a different kind of claim from *APS is not holism*.

The first concerns the explanandum. It asks whether computation is sufficient to constitute, identify, or explain living organisation as living organisation.

The second concerns the explanatory framework. It asks whether describing APS as holistic accurately characterises the commitments and structure of APS.

Neither question is equivalent to asking how APS compares with a developed explanatory approach such as autonomy theory, organisational biology, systems theory, or the Extended Evolutionary Synthesis.

That third question requires a comparator and a specified explanatory target.

The website therefore distinguishes three article families:

1. **Life Demarcation**
2. **APS Identity and Clarification**
3. **Comparative Explanatory Approaches**

These are functional distinctions. They do not imply a hierarchy among articles or explanatory approaches, and they do not generate automatic parallel articles.

The existence of *Why Life Is Not X* does not entail that the website also requires *Why APS Is Not X* or *APS and X*. Each article must be justified by a distinct explanatory question.

## 1. Life Demarcation

### What is being asked?

Life-demarcation articles ask whether some proposed property, capacity, process, model, or organisational description is sufficient by itself to identify or explain Life.

Within APS, Life is defined as:

> **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

The purpose of a *Why Life Is Not X* article is therefore not to dismiss X.

Information processing, computation, control, prediction, replication, intelligence, self-production, and other processes may be biologically important. The relevant question is narrower:

> **Does X, by itself, constitute or adequately characterise the organisation that distinguishes living systems as living systems?**

Articles in this family include:

- **[Why Life Is Not DNA's Way of Making More DNA](/articles/why-life-is-not-dnas-way-of-making-more-dna/)**
- **[Why Life Is Not Information Processing](/articles/why-life-is-not-information-processing/)**
- **[Why Life Is Not Control Theory](/articles/why-life-is-not-control-theory/)**
- **[Why Life Is Not Active Inference](/articles/why-life-is-not-active-inference/)**
- **[Why Life Is Not Autopoiesis](/articles/why-life-is-not-autopoiesis/)**
- **[Why Life Is Not Computation](/articles/why-life-is-not-computation-an-aps-clarification/)**
- **[Why Life Is Not Intelligence](/articles/why-life-is-not-intelligence-an-aps-clarification/)**
- **[Why Life Is Not a Machine](/articles/why-life-is-not-a-machine/)**

These articles concern **Life**, not whether APS defeats a competing theory.

Failure of X to constitute Life does not establish that APS has greater explanatory power than every framework employing X. That would require a separate comparative argument.

## 2. APS Identity and Clarification

### What is being asked?

A second group of articles concerns APS itself.

These articles ask whether APS is accurately described by familiar philosophical, methodological, or explanatory labels.

Examples include:

- **[Why APS Is Not Hierarchical](/articles/why-aps-is-not-hierarchical/)**
- **[Why APS Is Not Holism](/articles/why-aps-is-not-holism/)**
- **[Why APS Is Not Organicism](/articles/why-aps-is-not-organicism/)**
- **[Reductionism in Biology — An APS Clarification](/articles/reductionism-in-biology-an-aps-clarification/)**
- **[Emergence in Biology — An APS Clarification](/articles/emergence-in-biology-an-aps-clarification/)**

Their task is not to redefine Life.

They clarify the explanatory commitments of the Agency–Process–Scale framework.

Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed living organisation. They are not independent causes, components, or hierarchical levels.

Clarifying that architecture can establish what APS does and does not claim. It cannot, by itself, establish that APS explains a particular biological phenomenon better than another approach.

That distinction is fundamental to the comparative methodology of APS.

## 3. Comparative Explanatory Approaches

### What is being asked?

The third family concerns genuine comparison.

Here the question is not simply:

> *How is APS conceptually different from X?*

It is:

> **When APS and another explanatory approach address a sufficiently matched biological explanandum, what does each explain, through which dependencies, and with what explanatory gain or limitation?**

Current comparative objects include:

- **[APS and Autonomy Theory](/articles/aps-and-autonomy-theory/)**
- **[Why Life Is Not Enactivism](/articles/why-life-is-not-enactivism/)**
- **APS and Organisational Biology**
- **[APS and Systems Theory](/articles/aps-and-systems-theory/)**
- **[APS and the Extended Evolutionary Synthesis](/articles/aps-and-the-extended-evolutionary-synthesis/)**
- **[APS and the Free Energy Principle](/articles/aps-and-the-free-energy-principle/)**
- **[Gene-Centric Biology and APS](/articles/gene-centric-biology-and-aps/)**


Other comparisons belong here only when there is a genuine shared explanatory target.

The term **explanatory approaches** is intentionally broad. APS may be compared with theories, frameworks, research programmes, formalisms, explanatory strategies, philosophical positions, or research traditions. These should not be treated as though they were all theories of the same kind or competitors for precisely the same explanatory task.

A comparison must first establish that there is something sufficiently similar to compare.

## How Comparative Claims Are Assessed

Conceptual difference is easy to identify.

Explanatory gain is harder to establish.

Two approaches may use different vocabularies, organise concepts differently, emphasise different processes, or operate across different domains without genuinely competing as explanations.

APS therefore treats comparative assessment as explanandum-relative and comparator-relative.

A disciplined comparison should:

1. **specify the explanandum** — identify what biological phenomenon or dependency is actually being explained;
2. **identify the strongest relevant comparator** — compare APS with the best applicable alternative rather than a simplified representative;
3. **reconstruct both approaches fairly** — differences should not be manufactured by weakening the comparator;
4. **identify the relevant explanatory dependencies** — determine whether the approaches differ in what they claim the phenomenon depends upon;
5. **distinguish conceptual organisation from empirical warrant** — coherence or integration does not by itself establish biological explanation;
6. **identify material implementation where required** — organisational or causal claims must ultimately be biologically realised rather than inferred from framework architecture alone;
7. **permit more than one outcome** — comparison may reveal overlap, complementarity, independence, redundancy, tension, methodological gain, or explanatory gain favouring either approach.

APS architecture therefore does not determine the result of an APS comparison in advance.

For the detailed methodological account, see **[Comparative Explanatory Methodology in Theoretical Biology](/articles/comparative-explanatory-methodology-theoretical-biology/)**.

## Choose the Appropriate Pathway

The simplest way to navigate this part of the website is to begin with the question being asked.

### If the question is:

> **What is Life, and is X sufficient to account for it?**

Follow the **Life Demarcation** articles.

Begin with **[What Is Life? — A Biological Question Revisited](/articles/what-is-life-a-biological-question-revisited/)**, then use the relevant *Why Life Is Not X* article to examine a particular proposed characterisation.

### If the question is:

> **What is APS, and would describing it as X mischaracterise the framework?**

Follow **APS Identity and Clarification**.

Begin with **[What Is APS?](/orientation/what-is-aps/)** and the principal APS orientation articles, then consult the relevant clarification.

### If the question is:

> **How does APS compare with another explanatory approach?**

Follow **Comparative Explanatory Approaches**.

Begin with **[Comparative Explanatory Methodology in Theoretical Biology](/articles/comparative-explanatory-methodology-theoretical-biology/)** for the methodological controls, then proceed to the relevant *APS and X* comparison.

These pathways may intersect, but they should not be collapsed.

For example, showing that active inference is not by itself an account of Life would not settle whether the Free Energy Principle provides powerful explanations of particular biological phenomena. Likewise, showing that APS is not holistic would not establish that APS explains a matched biological target better than a holistic or systems-oriented account.

Different questions require different arguments.

## Where This Programme Fits Within APS

APS begins from living organisation, but it does not follow that every question about biological explanation is a question about the definition of Life.

The framework distinguishes its substantive account of living organisation from the methodological standards used to evaluate explanatory claims.

This distinction is especially important as APS is applied across biological domains.

The framework may propose that Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic perspectives on organised persistence. That architecture can guide investigation, identify candidate dependencies, and expose questions that might otherwise remain implicit.

But an explanatory proposal still has to earn its scientific role.

Where APS and another approach address different explananda, both may be appropriate without direct competition. Where they address the same explanandum, their explanatory contributions must be compared rather than inferred from differences in terminology or scope.

The purpose of this programme is therefore not to organise contemporary biology beneath APS.

It is to make clear **which question is being asked, what kind of comparison is legitimate, and what evidence would be required to support the resulting explanatory claim**.

That distinction allows APS to engage biological pluralism without either reducing all explanations to a single framework or treating all explanatory approaches as equivalent.

It also keeps three questions that biology needs to ask clearly apart:

> **What is Life?**  
> **What is APS?**  
> **How should APS and other explanatory approaches be compared?**

It is not a full presentation of the framework’s philosophical architecture—developed more extensively in *[APS as Philosophy — A Viability-Oriented Account of Biological Reality](/articles/aps-as-philosophy-biological-intelligibility/)*—nor a formal account of APS explanatory structure, developed in *[The Explanatory Geometry of Biology — How APS Organises Biological Explanation](/articles/explanatory-geometry-of-biology/)*. ## Introduction

Biology possesses extraordinarily powerful explanatory frameworks.

Mechanistic biology explains how systems operate through organised causal interactions. Evolutionary biology explains how populations change across generations. Genetics explains inheritance and variation. Information-theoretic approaches describe signalling, coding, and regulation. Systems biology analyses interaction networks across multiple scales.

These successes address different biological questions and need not share a
single explicit explanatory target.

APS begins from a particular question that cuts across those differences:

> What organisation is characteristic of living systems, and how is that
> organisation maintained despite change?

APS proposes life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and
organised persistence as the problem of how that organisation maintains and
re-establishes the conditions of its continuity through change.

This gives APS a potentially domain-wide explanatory problem without making
that problem obligatory for every biological inquiry. Particular phenomena may
be adequately explained through mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental,
ecological, mathematical, informational, or other approaches.

APS therefore does not reconstruct biological explanation generally. It
reframes questions about living organisation by making that organisation
explicit as an explanatory target and investigating how established biological
findings and explanations bear upon it.

## Organised Persistence

APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

Organised persistence identifies a central explanatory problem arising from
that organisation: how living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions
of their continuity despite continual material and organisational change.

Living systems are not merely collections of components. Their organisation
is dynamically maintained through activities that contribute to sustaining
conditions required for continued viability.

TThis persistence is active rather than passive, organisational rather than
merely structural, temporally extended rather than instantaneous, and
viability-oriented rather than mechanically indifferent. A living system
therefore exists only insofar as it continuously regenerates and modulates the
conditions of its own persistence.

The processes contributing to such continuity can include metabolic
maintenance, environmental regulation, adaptive response, developmental
organisation, behavioural activity, reproduction, and evolutionary
transformation. These processes occur in different biological domains and may
require different explanations. APS investigates their relationships where
those relationships contribute to explaining living organisation and organised
persistence.

## Why APS Reframes Biological Explanation

APS reframes biology by changing the explanatory question when living
organisation itself is the target.

Biological explanations legitimately focus on mechanisms, genes, information,
traits, selection, development, ecological relations, mathematical structure,
and many other phenomena. APS does not claim that these approaches become
adequate only when reconstructed through its framework.

Instead, APS asks:

> How do established biological findings and explanations bear upon the
> organisation through which living systems maintain and re-establish the
> conditions of their persistence?

This question permits APS to reconsider familiar biological concepts in
relation to living organisation. Within APS:

- mechanisms can be investigated for their contribution to organised
  persistence;
- informational relations can be examined within the organisation in which
  they acquire biological roles;
- function is analysed as present-tense viability-relative organisational
  contribution;
- evolution is investigated as historical transformation of living
  organisation;
- cognition is investigated in relation to the organisation of biological
  significance across time.

These are APS reframings. Their value does not follow merely from placing
familiar concepts within a broader organisational architecture.

APS reframes biology by reorganising questions about living systems around
viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and organised persistence,
and by investigating how established biological findings and explanations bear
upon those targets through its Agency–Process–Scale Explanatory Grammar and
wider comparative methodology.

Reframing does not by itself replace, invalidate, subsume, or improve upon
existing explanations. Whether a particular APS reframing provides explanatory
gain must be established through target-matched comparison.

## Agency, Process, and Scale

APS organises its general account of living organisation through three
complementary analytic projections:

- **Agency** — what living systems do;
- **Process** — how continuity is maintained despite change;
- **Scale** — where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal
  extents.

**Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections of one
viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

They are not independent components, causes, entities, domains, dimensions, or
hierarchical levels of biological reality. The distinctions are analytic; the
organisation and activities they disclose are biologically real.

**Agency** concerns viability-oriented organisational activity through which
living systems contribute to maintaining and re-establishing conditions of
their persistence. It does not require intention, consciousness, intelligence,
or mental representation.

**Process** concerns the dynamic organisation through which continuity is
maintained despite change. Living organisation persists through ongoing
activity and transformation rather than static existence.

**Scale** concerns the spatial and temporal extents across which persistence is
organised. Relations across those extents operate through materially
implemented interactions and constraints rather than abstract top-down,
bottom-up, or level-to-level causal propagation.

Together, Agency, Process, and Scale constitute the **APS Explanatory Grammar**:
the organised conceptual and explanatory relations through which APS structures
its account of living organisation and organised persistence.

They are not obligatory categories of biological explanation generally.
Their relevance follows from the explanatory target APS addresses, not from a
requirement that every biological inquiry be reconstructed through them.

## Explanatory Priority

APS distinguishes explanatory priority from ontological or causal priority.

Living systems remain physically constituted, and APS introduces no separate
causal substance or hierarchically deeper biological level.

Explanatory priority is instead relative to what is being explained. Where a
question concerns a particular molecular mechanism, developmental process,
evolutionary change, ecological interaction, or other bounded phenomenon, a
specialist explanation may be sufficient and preferable.

Where living organisation itself is the explanatory target, APS gives
organisational relations particular explanatory importance because its
question concerns how living systems maintain and re-establish conditions of
viability despite change.

TThis gives APS **potential domain-wide relevance**, not universal explanatory
authority. APS proposes an account potentially relevant across the living
domain because living organisation itself is its target. It does not follow
that organisation must possess explanatory priority in every biological
inquiry.

Nor does APS's organisational emphasis establish its superiority over
alternative general accounts of life. Whether APS provides the preferred
general account of living organisation depends upon demonstrated explanatory
gain over the strongest relevant alternatives addressing that same target.

## Evolution Within Organised Persistence

Evolutionary theory provides explanations of population change, adaptation,
diversification, inheritance, and historical transformation that retain their
own explanatory targets and evidential foundations.

APS addresses a different but related question. Living organisation must exist
and persist sufficiently for reproduction, inheritance, variation, and
selection to occur. APS therefore investigates evolution in relation to the
historical transformation of viability-oriented organisation across
generations.

This reframing does not subordinate evolutionary explanation to APS or make
organised persistence a replacement for natural selection and other
evolutionary processes. It asks how established evolutionary explanations bear
upon the continuity and transformation of living organisation when that is the
target under investigation.

Whether this organisational reframing adds explanatory value beyond established evolutionary accounts is a comparative question and cannot be inferred from the scope or structure of the APS architecture.

## Relating Explanations Across Biological Domains

Biological domains address different questions using different explanatory
resources. Their plurality need not constitute fragmentation, and their
successful explanations need not share a single explicit conceptual
foundation.

APS nevertheless asks whether explanatory contributions from different domains
can be related when living organisation or organised persistence is the target.

Mechanistic findings may clarify processes through which persistence is
maintained. Evolutionary explanations may clarify historical transformations
of living organisation. Developmental and physiological explanations may
clarify continuity through change within organisms. Ecological explanations
may clarify materially implemented organism–environment relations. Cognitive
explanations may bear upon increasingly temporally extended organisation of
biological significance.

These relations are not established merely because APS can represent them
within one conceptual architecture. Nor does their integration automatically
produce a better explanation.

APS therefore provides a comparative and integrative framework for
investigating **whether and how** explanatory contributions can be related
around a shared target while preserving the independence of specialist
explanations where appropriate.

Its contribution is potentially synthetic, but synthesis has no automatic
explanatory priority over analysis or specialist explanation. Explanatory gain
must be demonstrated in relation to the question being addressed.

## Why This Matters

APS addresses a genuine problem of explanatory organisation without treating
the plurality of biology as a defect to be eliminated.

Mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental, physiological, ecological,
informational, mathematical, cognitive, and other approaches illuminate
different biological questions. APS asks what happens when living organisation
itself becomes the explanatory target and whether findings from these domains
can then be related in ways that improve understanding.

Its potential contribution is therefore both substantive and methodological.

Substantively, APS proposes life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed
organisation and organised persistence as the problem of maintaining and
re-establishing that organisation despite change.

Methodologically, APS provides a comparative explanatory methodology within
which its Agency–Process–Scale grammar helps organise questions concerning
living organisation and organised persistence. Relationships among biological
explanations can thereby be specified and assessed without assuming in advance
that integration will provide explanatory gain.

This makes APS potentially general without making it universally authoritative.

Its generality derives from its explanatory target: living organisation is
characteristic of the living domain. Its adequacy and comparative standing,
however, remain separate questions.

The absence of demonstrated APS superiority does not establish its inferiority.
Comparative preference in either direction requires target-matched assessment
against the strongest relevant alternatives.

The wider comparative programme develops this question through
*Comparative Explanatory Methodology in Theoretical Biology*, *APS and
Contemporary Theories*, and individual comparative applications.

## APS as a Reframing of Biological Intelligibility

APS is not primarily a theory of evolution, cognition, mechanism, or any other
specialist biological domain.

It is a **comparative explanatory methodology for biology organised around a
general account of living organisation and the problem of organised
persistence**.

Its Agency–Process–Scale grammar structures that inquiry by asking what living
systems do, how continuity is maintained despite change, and where persistence
is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

This permits APS to reconsider relationships among mechanism, function,
evolution, cognition, normativity, environment, and other biological concepts
where they bear upon living organisation.

That reconsideration is what **reframing** means.

It does not mean that APS has shown those concepts to possess a single
underlying explanation, that specialist explanations are incomplete without
APS, or that APS has already demonstrated superior explanatory performance.

APS reframes biology by making living organisation explicit as a general
explanatory target and providing a structured way to investigate how
biological explanations bear upon it.

That is why APS reframes biology.

## Systems Theory and the Study of Organisation

Systems theory encompasses a broad set of approaches that model phenomena in terms of interacting components, feedback loops, and dynamic behaviour. In biology, these approaches have been central to moving beyond strictly reductionist explanations.

Rather than analysing isolated parts, systems approaches examine how interactions among components produce organised behaviour over time. Concepts such as feedback, regulation, stability, and emergence are central to this perspective. APS interprets such phenomena through its own account of living organisation rather than treating emergence itself as a sufficient explanation. See *[Emergence in Biology — An APS Clarification](/articles/emergence-in-biology-an-aps-clarification/)*.

These approaches have proven extremely powerful for investigating and explaining biological systems, from metabolic networks to ecological dynamics. In this respect, systems theory captures an important aspect of living organisation: biological phenomena often depend upon relations among interacting processes rather than upon isolated components considered independently.

## Points of Comparison with APS

Systems approaches and APS overlap in their attention to interaction, regulation, feedback, dynamic organisation, and relations among processes unfolding across spatial and temporal extents.

This overlap does not establish that the approaches share the same explanatory targets or organising principles. Systems theory encompasses diverse research programmes, while APS specifically proposes viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation as its account of life and treats organised persistence as the explanatory problem of how living organisation maintains and re-establishes continuity through change.

Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections within the APS Explanatory Grammar rather than co-constitutive components or dimensions of biological reality. Systems concepts such as feedback, coupling, and dynamic stability may therefore provide important points of comparison with APS without being incorporated into APS or interpreted as expressions of it.

The relationship between systems approaches and APS must consequently be assessed in relation to the question being addressed. Similarity, difference, overlap, complementarity, independence, or explanatory gain are possible comparative outcomes rather than assumptions.

[[box:how-aps-relates-to-other-theoretical-frameworks]]

## Systems Description and the Question of Living Organisation

Systems theory encompasses approaches with different explanatory aims. Many formal systems methods can describe interaction, feedback, stability, and dynamic behaviour without themselves specifying what distinguishes living organisation from other organised systems. This is not necessarily a deficiency: such methods may be designed to address different explanatory targets.

Likewise, system boundaries may sometimes be specified for purposes of modelling and analysis. Other systems and organisational approaches treat biological boundaries more substantively. It would therefore be misleading to attribute a single account of boundaries or normativity to systems theory as a whole.

Many systems-theoretic tools can be applied across domains including:

- living organisms  
- mechanical devices  
- economic systems  
- climate dynamics  

Such formal tools can be applied across these cases without necessarily distinguishing them in terms of their mode of organisation. This also helps explain why similar functional patterns may be realised across very different material systems without thereby erasing the distinction between biological and non-biological organisation. See *[Multiple Realization and Biological Organisation](/articles/multiple-realization-and-biological-organisation/)*.

## APS and the Question of Biological Organisation

APS makes living organisation itself an explicit explanatory target by proposing viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation as its general account of living organisation.

The canonical APS formulation is:

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

This is the substantive biological account proposed by APS. Its canonical
status within APS does not by itself establish its comparative superiority
over alternative accounts of living organisation.

In APS, living systems are not characterised by complexity, feedback, or
dynamic behaviour alone. Their organisation is viability-oriented: activity
is organised in relation to maintaining and re-establishing the conditions
required for persistence.

This establishes a point of comparison with systems approaches that describe
interaction, feedback, stability, or dynamics without making viability-relative
living organisation their explicit explanatory target. Such approaches may
provide adequate explanations for the questions they address; APS asks the
different question of how organised activity bears upon the persistence of
the living system itself.

Within APS, **biological normativity is viability-relative asymmetry**: states, processes, and outcomes differ in their consequences for viable organisation. Biological Evaluation is the process through which agency generates significance in relation to viability-relevant differences.

Whether this APS account provides explanatory gain over systems approaches
when living organisation itself is the shared explanatory target remains a
matter for target-matched comparison. The difference in explanatory focus
does not establish that systems approaches are incomplete or that APS provides
the preferred explanation.

## System Boundaries and Organisational Closure

A central difference concerns how system boundaries are understood.

In some systems approaches, boundaries are specified for analytical or modelling purposes, so the system under investigation is partly determined by the explanatory task and modelling framework. Other systems and organisational approaches attribute greater biological significance to system boundaries.

APS proposes that biologically relevant boundaries should be investigated in
relation to constraint-closed organisation rather than treated solely as
observer-selected modelling boundaries. On this account, boundaries are
materially realised and maintained through organised biological activity.

This provides a substantive APS claim about living organisation. Whether it
improves upon alternative systems or organisational accounts of biological
boundaries requires comparative and biological assessment.

Within APS, biological boundaries are therefore investigated as materially realised features of living organisation whose maintenance may itself depend upon organised biological activity. This differs from cases in which a boundary is specified primarily for modelling or analytical purposes, without implying that systems approaches generally treat biological boundaries as externally imposed.

## Agency and the Distinction Between Living and Non-Living Systems

Formal systems descriptions can often be applied to both living and non-living
systems without requiring a criterion that distinguishes the two. APS makes
that distinction an explicit explanatory concern.

APS proposes biological agency as part of its account of this distinction. **Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity**: the activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained under conditions relevant to its viability.

This distinguishes the APS account of biological agency from cases in which:

- functions are externally assigned rather than generated through viability-relative organisation;
- organised dynamics occur without satisfying the conditions APS associates with viability-oriented, constraint-closed self-maintenance.

In APS terms, agency is not an additional feature layered onto living organisation. Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity: the present-tense activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained.

### Systems, Organisation, and the Appearance of Cognition

Because systems theory can describe complex, adaptive, and dynamically stable
behaviour, it can be used to model systems that appear intelligent or
cognitively organised, including artificial, computational, and social systems.

From an APS perspective, this descriptive power does not by itself establish
cognition. Cognition is not defined by behavioural complexity, feedback,
optimisation, or dynamical sophistication alone.

APS distinguishes biological organisation from adaptive systems in general
through its proposed account of viability-oriented, constraint-closed
organisation. Artificial, computational, cybernetic, and other non-biological
systems may exhibit feedback, regulation, optimisation, distributed
coordination, and complex adaptive behaviour without thereby satisfying the
conditions APS associates with biological agency or cognition.

APS therefore does not infer cognition from behavioural complexity, feedback,
optimisation, or dynamical sophistication alone. Within APS, cognition requires
a more specific organisation of biological significance across time. Whether
particular systems satisfy the relevant conditions must therefore be assessed
rather than inferred from systems complexity alone.

For further discussion, see *[Why AI Is Not Biological Agency](/articles/why-ai-is-not-biological-agency/)*
and *[Why Life Is Not Intelligence](/articles/why-life-is-not-intelligence-an-aps-clarification/)*.

## Comparing Systems Theory and APS

Systems approaches provide powerful resources for investigating interaction,
feedback, regulation, dynamical trajectories, network organisation, and other
features of biological systems.

APS addresses a particular explanatory target: living organisation and the
activity through which organised persistence is maintained despite change. It
proposes viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation as its general
account of that target.

These differences do not establish that systems theory is merely descriptive,
that APS supplies a missing explanatory layer, or that systems approaches must
be integrated into APS. Nor does the broader applicability of systems methods
establish their superiority over APS when living organisation itself is the
question being addressed.

The appropriate relationship depends upon the explanatory target. For some
questions, systems analysis may be sufficient and preferable. Where living
organisation itself is the target, APS may provide additional explanatory or
methodological resources. Whether it does so must be established through
target-matched comparison with the strongest relevant alternatives.

Systems theory and APS should therefore be compared rather than hierarchically
nested. Complementarity, integration, overlap, independence, redundancy, or
comparative explanatory gain remain possible outcomes of that comparison.

## Key Point

Systems approaches provide powerful explanations of interaction, feedback, regulation, dynamics, and network organisation. APS proposes viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation as its account of living organisation. Neither conceptual difference nor APS's explanatory architecture establishes comparative superiority. Where systems approaches and APS address the same target, explanatory preference requires target-matched comparison against the strongest relevant alternatives.

APS and autonomy theory occupy closely related positions within contemporary organisational biology. Both reject explanations of life that are exhausted by inventories of components considered in isolation and instead direct attention to the organisation through which living systems maintain themselves through time. Autonomy theory has made major contributions to this organisational perspective through accounts of autonomy, self-maintenance, regulation, constraint closure, and biological normativity. APS addresses closely related explanatory targets through its account of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and organised persistence.

Their relationship therefore involves substantial conceptual overlap as well as differences of terminology, explanatory architecture, emphasis, and scope. APS draws upon the organisational tradition in which autonomy theory is a major development, but intellectual continuity does not establish that APS is a theoretical extension, completion, or improvement of autonomy theory. Where the two frameworks address the same explanatory target, their relative adequacy must be assessed rather than assumed.

The comparison is especially important because both frameworks investigate how living systems maintain the conditions of their own continuation despite material turnover and changing circumstances. APS additionally organises its account through viability, organised persistence, Agency–Process–Scale, and explicit distinctions concerning definition, diagnosis, and comparative explanation. Whether those differences yield biological explanatory gain, methodological gain, complementarity, redundancy, or some other relation remains a target-dependent comparative question.

## The Autonomy Insight

Autonomy theory has been a major contributor to the organisational turn in theoretical biology in theoretical biology by grounding biological explanation in organisation rather than components. Earlier approaches often attempted to explain living systems primarily through their constituent parts or the mechanisms through which those parts interacted. Autonomy theorists argued that such explanations remained incomplete unless they also accounted for the organisational relations that make living systems possible in the first place.

The central insight was that living systems persist through networks of mutually dependent processes that collectively maintain the conditions required for their own continuation. Biological organisation is therefore not imposed from outside but continuously generated and sustained through the activity of the system itself. A living organism remains alive not because particular molecules persist unchanged, but because organisational relations are continually reproduced through ongoing activity.

This perspective transformed the theoretical landscape by treating biological organisation as an explanatory principle in its own right. Living systems came to be understood as self-maintaining unities whose persistence depends upon organised activity rather than passive material constitution. Constraint closure provided a formal account of this organisation and helped explain how living systems could maintain their own conditions of existence despite continual material turnover.

APS shares this organisational insight. Its concept of organised persistence likewise treats continuity as dependent upon the ongoing maintenance and re-establishment of organisational conditions rather than the persistence of unchanged material constituents. The resulting overlap is substantial. APS nevertheless organises this problem through its own explanatory grammar, including viability-oriented organisation and Agency–Process–Scale. Those differences identify matters for comparison; they do not by themselves establish an extension or explanatory advance over autonomy theory.

## Points of Convergence with APS

APS and autonomy theory share a number of foundational commitments that place them within a common organisational tradition. Both reject attempts to define life solely through constituent components, molecular inventories, or externally assigned functions and instead treat organisation as the primary object of biological explanation. Living systems are understood as entities whose continued existence depends upon ongoing self-maintaining activity rather than static structural properties.

Both frameworks further recognise that biological organisation is intrinsically normative. Living systems continuously distinguish between processes that contribute to persistence and processes that undermine it. This distinction does not arise from external observers or imposed goals but emerges from the organisational requirements of continued existence. Biological organisation therefore carries its own standards of success and failure.

Most importantly, both autonomy theory and APS recognise that living systems actively participate in their own persistence. Organisms do not simply undergo biological processes; they continuously contribute to the maintenance of the conditions that make those processes possible. Biological organisation is therefore inseparable from the activity through which that organisation is sustained.

These shared commitments place APS and autonomy theory within a closely related organisational explanatory field. APS therefore should not be defined by opposition to autonomy theory. At the same time, conceptual inheritance and agreement do not establish that APS is simply an extension of autonomy theory, nor that the two frameworks are necessarily complementary. Their relation must be specified according to the explanatory target under consideration.

## From Organisation to Agency

Contemporary organisational biology distinguishes among several closely related questions concerning organisation, regulation, and agency. Autonomy theory has contributed substantially to all three, including through later work on biological regulation. APS employs these concepts within its own account of viability-oriented organisation. The relevant comparative question is therefore not whether APS adds concepts missing from autonomy theory, but how the frameworks organise and relate these explanatory resources and whether those differences matter for a specified biological explanandum.

### Constraint Closure

The concept of constraint closure occupies a central position within autonomy theory. Living systems consist of networks of constraints that mutually contribute to one another's maintenance while collectively sustaining the organised processes upon which the system depends. Through this organisation, the activities of the system continuously regenerate the conditions that make those activities possible.

Constraint closure therefore provides an explanation for how living systems maintain themselves despite continual material turnover. Organisms persist not because particular molecules remain unchanged, but because organisational relations are continuously reproduced through ongoing activity. Closure identifies the organisational architecture that underlies biological persistence and provides one of the most influential accounts of biological organisation in contemporary theoretical biology.

APS likewise assigns constraint closure a central role within its account of living organisation. Within APS, life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. This creates substantial overlap with autonomy theory, but the shared use of closure concepts does not establish equivalence, incorporation, or explanatory priority. Their respective roles must be assessed within the explanatory architectures in which they occur.

### Regulation

Biological regulation concerns how living systems coordinate and modulate ongoing activity under changing conditions. Autonomy theory has developed substantial accounts of regulation alongside its treatment of organisational closure and self-maintenance.

Closure and regulation should not, however, be assigned predetermined explanatory roles in which closure explains organisational integrity while regulation supplies a separate capacity for adaptive control. They may participate together in explanations of particular biological phenomena, and their relationship depends upon the theoretical architecture and explanandum under consideration.

APS interprets regulation within viability-oriented organisation. Regulation may contribute to maintaining and re-establishing viable conditions under change, but its position within APS does not establish that it is explanatorily downstream from constraint closure or that autonomy-theoretic accounts must be reconstructed through APS.

### Agency

Within APS, biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity. Agency concerns what living systems do in maintaining and re-establishing the conditions of their persistence. Environmental engagement is an important expression of biological agency, but agency is not restricted to organism–environment interaction.

Movement, behaviour, physiological adjustment, regulation, environmental modification, growth, repair, and other forms of organised activity may therefore be expressions of agency insofar as they participate in viability-oriented organisation. The relevant distinction is not between an internally regulated system and an externally acting agent, but between different explanatory aspects of the organised activity through which living systems persist.

APS consequently does not treat agency as an additional property imposed upon an already complete organisation. Agency is an analytic perspective on living organisation in activity. In the canonical APS formulation, Agency concerns what living systems do; Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change; Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. The distinctions are analytic; the organisation and activities they disclose are biologically real.

### APS Integration

APS places constraint closure, regulation, agency, and viability within a common account of living organisation. Constraint closure concerns the mutually sustaining organisation of constraints; regulation concerns organised modulation of activity under changing conditions; and biological agency concerns viability-oriented organisational activity.

Within APS these relations are interpreted through the canonical claim that life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and through the problem of organised persistence: how that organisation maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its continuity despite change.

This provides APS with a distinctive explanatory architecture. It does not follow, however, that this architecture is richer, more comprehensive, or explanatorily superior to autonomy theory. Some of the relations APS makes explicit may already be represented within autonomy theory under different concepts or explanatory arrangements. Determining whether APS produces additional biological explanation therefore requires comparison at a matched explanatory target.

## The Scope of the Autonomy Framework

The close relationship between autonomy theory and APS makes differences in scope especially important to characterise carefully. APS explicitly organises questions concerning definition and diagnosis, Agency–Process–Scale, viability, organised persistence, and evolutionary transformation within a single explanatory grammar. Autonomy theory has its own developed treatments of organisation, regulation, agency, normativity, identity, and biological continuity.

The presence of additional distinctions within APS does not demonstrate that autonomy theory leaves corresponding biological phenomena unexplained. Some differences may concern terminology; others may concern explanatory emphasis, architecture, methodology, or target. Still others may prove substantively explanatory. These possibilities must be distinguished rather than collapsed into a general claim that APS extends autonomy theory.

The appropriate relationship is therefore one of comparative proximity without a predetermined verdict. APS is historically and conceptually continuous with important parts of the autonomy tradition while remaining a distinct explanatory framework. Where their targets overlap, comparative preference depends upon what is being explained and upon demonstrated explanatory performance.

## Definition and Diagnosis

APS explicitly distinguishes questions concerning what life is from questions concerning how living organisation is identified or investigated. This distinction is particularly useful when organisational concepts are applied to empirical investigation, life detection, artificial systems, or borderline biological cases. The distinction should be understood here as part of APS's explanatory architecture rather than as evidence that autonomy theory fails to distinguish these questions.

Definition concerns the nature of living organisation itself. Within APS, life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Organised persistence concerns how this organisation continually maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its continuity despite change. Definition therefore concerns what constitutes living organisation rather than the procedures through which it is detected.

Maintaining this distinction prevents conceptual confusion. Biological organisation should not be defined in terms of the methods used to detect it, just as the existence of a phenomenon should not be confused with the techniques employed to investigate it. APS therefore treats definition and diagnosis as complementary but distinct explanatory domains.

The distinction also allows APS to state explicitly how its organisational concepts relate to empirical investigation. This is a methodological feature of APS. Whether it provides an advantage over autonomy-theory approaches is a separate comparative question and is not established by the distinction itself.

## Scale and Organisation

Questions of biological organisation frequently extend across different spatial and temporal extents. Physiological regulation, development, ecological interaction, reproduction, and evolutionary transformation may involve processes whose relevant organisation is distributed differently in space and time.

APS addresses such variation through Scale. **Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.** It does not identify an additional component, cause, entity, domain, or hierarchical level of living organisation.

Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Agency concerns what living systems do; Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change; Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

This formulation does not require every biological explanandum to be explained across multiple scales, nor does it establish that explanations spanning more spatial or temporal extents are inherently superior. The relevant scale or combination of scales depends upon the explanatory target.

APS therefore does not convert explanatory priority into ontological, causal, temporal, or hierarchical priority. Material processes and organisational relations may enter biological explanations in different ways depending upon what is being explained. The explanatory relevance of organisation must be established for the particular explanandum rather than inferred from its position within APS architecture.

## Evolutionary Continuity and Historical Transformation

APS gives explicit attention to the relationship between present organisation and evolutionary transformation. It interprets living systems not only as presently organised entities but as participants in historically continuous processes of variation, inheritance, reconstruction, and evolutionary change. This emphasis distinguishes APS's explanatory architecture from some formulations centred more directly on autonomous self-maintenance, but the difference should not be interpreted as evidence that autonomy theory is confined to ahistorical or exclusively individual organisation.

Living systems do not merely persist in the present. They emerge from historically continuous lineages whose organisational characteristics have been maintained, modified, and transformed across generations. Understanding living organisation therefore requires understanding not only how organisms maintain themselves now but also how viable forms of organisation remain historically continuous through evolutionary time.

APS formulates this historical problem as the transformation of viability-oriented organisation across evolutionary time. Variation, inheritance, development, ecological interaction, and selection may all contribute to explanations of how forms of living organisation persist and change across generations.

This formulation places historical transformation explicitly within the APS explanatory architecture. It should not be interpreted as establishing a fixed division in which organisational approaches explain present self-maintenance while evolutionary theory explains historical transformation, nor as showing that autonomy theory lacks resources for addressing historical biological continuity.

The comparative issue is instead whether APS and autonomy-theoretic approaches identify different dependencies or provide different explanatory results when applied to the same historically extended biological explanandum. That question cannot be settled by APS's explicit inclusion of evolutionary transformation within its architecture.

APS therefore brings organisational persistence and historical transformation into an explicit common explanatory architecture. Autonomy theory provides powerful resources for explaining autonomous organisation, while APS formulates evolutionary transformation in terms of historically changing viability-oriented organisation. Whether these approaches are complementary, overlapping, differently targeted, or comparatively unequal cannot be decided from the difference in architecture alone.

## Normativity and Viability

Autonomy theory played a major role in reintroducing normativity into biological explanation. By showing that living systems maintain themselves through organised activity, it became possible to explain why certain processes contribute to persistence while others undermine it. Biological normativity no longer needed to be understood as externally imposed purpose or observer-relative evaluation. Instead, norms emerged from the organisational requirements of living systems themselves.

APS shares the organisational grounding of biological normativity while expressing that relation explicitly in viability-relative terms. Within APS, normativity consists in viability-relative asymmetry: conditions and activities differ biologically according to how they bear upon the maintenance or re-establishment of organised persistence. Constraint closure and viability therefore occupy related but distinguishable roles within the APS account. This formulation specifies APS's own explanatory position; it does not establish that autonomy theory lacks an equivalent normative relation under different terminology or architecture.

Within APS, viability should not be equated with simple short-term survival. Viability concerns the conditions within which living organisation can continue, and those conditions may extend across different biological activities and temporal extents.

Biological agency is **viability-oriented organisational activity**. Regulation, environmental interaction, physiological adjustment, development, repair, behaviour, and other activities may participate in agency where they contribute to the enactment and maintenance of living organisation.

Constraint closure, agency, viability, and normativity should not therefore be arranged as successive explanatory stages. Within APS they articulate relations within one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Biological normativity is **viability-relative asymmetry**: states, processes, and outcomes differ in their consequences for viable organisation.

This is APS's formulation of their relationship. It does not establish that autonomy theory lacks corresponding explanatory resources, nor that its accounts of normativity, regulation, or agency must be reconstructed through viability-oriented terminology. Any comparative difference must be assessed against a specified explanatory target.

## The APS Perspective

From an APS perspective, autonomy theory is one of the major contemporary expressions of organisational biology. Its accounts of autonomy, self-maintenance, constraint closure, regulation, function, and endogenous normativity overlap substantially with questions central to APS. APS should therefore neither define itself through opposition to autonomy theory nor present autonomy theory merely as an incomplete precursor.

APS nevertheless has its own explanatory architecture. It defines life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation; treats organised persistence as the problem of how that organisation maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its continuity despite change; distinguishes definition from diagnosis; and employs Agency, Process, and Scale as complementary analytic projections of one living organisation. It also explicitly relates present organised persistence to historical and evolutionary transformation.

These features establish the structure and scope of APS. They do not establish comparative superiority. Greater conceptual breadth, explicit integration, or potential foundational generality cannot substitute for target-matched comparison against the strongest autonomy-theory explanation of the same phenomenon.

The relationship between APS and autonomy theory should therefore remain open to several outcomes. Particular comparisons may reveal substantial overlap, complementarity, independence, redundancy, tension, methodological gain, or differential biological explanatory gain. APS's distinctive contribution can be established only where the relevant comparison shows what additional explanatory work its architecture actually performs.

## Summary

Autonomy theory has made a major contribution to organisational biological explanation by demonstrating the importance of constraint closure, self-maintenance, and endogenous normativity. It showed that living systems are not merely collections of components but organised unities whose persistence depends upon the continual regeneration of the conditions required for their own existence.

APS shares these organisational foundations while articulating its own account through viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and organised persistence. It explicitly relates closure, regulation, agency, viability, Agency–Process–Scale, definition and diagnosis, and historical transformation within a single explanatory grammar.

These differences establish APS as a distinct but closely related organisational framework; they do not establish that it extends or improves upon autonomy theory. Where the two address the same biological explanandum, their relationship must be established through target-matched comparison. Depending upon the case, the result may be overlap, complementarity, independence, redundancy, tension, or explanatory gain favouring either framework.

Autonomy theory and APS share substantial commitments to organisational explanation, self-maintenance, constraint closure, regulation, and endogenous biological normativity. APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation** and treats organised persistence as the problem of continuity through change. These architectural differences do not themselves establish explanatory gain; where the frameworks address the same target, comparative preference requires target-matched assessment.

This article clarifies the relationship between the Extended Evolutionary Synthesis (EES) and APS. The EES and APS address overlapping questions concerning organismal activity, development, environmental relations, continuity, and evolutionary transformation. Their conceptual and explanatory differences create a comparative problem; they do not establish that APS grounds or completes evolutionary explanation.

## The Extended Evolutionary Synthesis

The Extended Evolutionary Synthesis encompasses a set of approaches that expand evolutionary theory beyond the gene-centric framework of the Modern Synthesis. It incorporates processes such as developmental bias, phenotypic plasticity, niche construction, and ecological inheritance.

These approaches emphasise that organisms are not passive products of selection but active participants in shaping their evolutionary trajectories. Development influences variation, and organism–environment interaction modifies the conditions under which selection operates.

In this way, the EES broadens evolutionary explanation by situating genetic change within a wider set of developmental, ecological, and organism–environment processes.

## Points of Convergence with APS

APS and the EES share several important commitments. Both resist strictly gene-centric accounts of evolution and emphasise the significance of organism–environment interaction, while recognising that development, regulation, and ecological context contribute to understanding how evolutionary processes unfold. The EES emphasis on niche construction and phenotypic plasticity also provides an important point of contact with the APS treatment of organisms as active participants in processes affecting the conditions of their persistence.

These convergences make the EES a natural point of comparison for APS. They establish overlapping explanatory concerns, however, rather than theoretical identity or a predetermined relation between the two frameworks.

## Comparing Evolutionary and Organisational Explanatory Targets

APS and the EES overlap substantially in the biological phenomena they address, but they organise explanatory questions differently. The EES broadens evolutionary explanation by examining how development, inheritance, plasticity, niche construction, selection, and organism–environment relations contribute to evolutionary change.

APS investigates living organisation and organised persistence and asks how evolutionary processes bear upon the historical continuity and transformation of viability-oriented organisation.

This does not establish that APS asks a more fundamental question, nor that evolutionary theory merely presupposes conditions which APS alone explains. Claims that persistence, reproduction, inheritance, variation, or selection depend upon particular organisational relations should instead be treated as Dependency Hypotheses whose evidential and explanatory significance must be assessed.

The relevant comparative question is therefore whether APS and an evolutionary explanation address the same explanandum and, if so, whether the APS formulation changes explanatory discrimination, dependency disclosure, scope, evidence, or warranted understanding. Difference in conceptual architecture alone does not establish such gain.

## APS: Organised Persistence and Evolutionary Transformation

APS proposes that life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and treats organised persistence as the problem of how that organisation maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its continuity despite change.

Applied to evolution, APS investigates possible dependencies among organised persistence, reproduction, inheritance, variation, environmental relations, and historical transformation. It may therefore ask how forms of living organisation are maintained, reconstructed, and transformed across generations.

Within APS, biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity. Agency may include regulation, environmental modification, development, behaviour, reproduction, and other activities where these contribute to the maintenance or transformation of living organisation.

APS accordingly interprets evolution as the historical transformation of viability-oriented organisation. This is an APS explanatory framing, not a replacement for established evolutionary explanations of population change, selection, inheritance, adaptation, or diversification. Whether this reframing adds explanatory gain is a separate comparative question.

## Evolution, Adaptation, and Persistence

APS interprets adaptation and evolution in relation to living organisation, while evolutionary frameworks may characterise these phenomena through different explanatory units and processes. These formulations should be compared rather than hierarchically nested.

Within APS, adaptation may be investigated as the reorganisation of viability-oriented activity in relation to changing conditions, while evolution is interpreted as the historical transformation of viability-oriented organisation across generations. These formulations locate adaptation and evolution within the APS explanatory architecture; they do not replace established evolutionary definitions or establish that evolutionary explanations must be reconstructed through organised persistence.

Within APS, biological purpose refers to viability-oriented organisation without requiring externally imposed design or conscious intention.

APS may investigate proposed relations among inheritance, adaptation, fitness, selection, and lineage continuity as Dependency Hypotheses. Their exact form and explanatory contribution require biological and comparative assessment rather than following automatically from their position within APS architecture.

## The Scope of the EES

The EES addresses evolutionary questions through an expanded range of developmental, ecological, hereditary, and organismal processes. APS makes living organisation itself an explicit explanatory target and asks how evolutionary processes bear upon organised persistence and historical transformation.

These differences in scope do not establish that the EES is explanatorily incomplete, that APS supplies its missing foundation, or that the two frameworks are necessarily complementary. Where their targets differ, each may remain adequate for its own question. Where their targets overlap, their relative explanatory contribution requires target-matched comparison.

## Comparing the EES and APS

The EES and APS can be compared across several overlapping concerns: development, organismal activity, environmental modification, inheritance, adaptation, historical continuity, and evolutionary transformation.

APS brings these questions into relation with its account of viability-oriented organised persistence. The EES develops them within an explicitly evolutionary explanatory programme. Neither architecture is thereby contained within the other.

Conceptual inclusion within APS does not establish integration or explanatory superiority.

# Why APS Is Not Organicism

APS shares important concerns with historical organicism. Both approaches reject the idea that living systems can be adequately understood as mere aggregates of independently meaningful parts. Both emphasise that organisms are organised, dynamically integrated, and continuously self-maintaining systems whose components derive biological significance from their relations within the larger organisation of the living system.

For this reason APS may initially appear to belong within the organicist tradition of theoretical biology.

APS rejects strongly reductionistic conceptions of life, emphasises organisation and persistence, and interprets organisms as temporally extended systems sustained through continuous processes of regulation, repair, adaptation, and exchange. APS also shares the organicist conviction that biological explanation must address the organised dynamics of living systems rather than merely cataloguing isolated component interactions.

Yet APS is not simply a contemporary form of organicism.

Historical organicism recognised many important features of living systems, but often lacked explicit explanatory structure, operational criteria, and diagnostic precision. APS inherits several of the strongest organism-centred insights of organicist biology while reconstructing them within a more explicit continuity-oriented explanatory architecture centred on viability, organised persistence, perturbation-sensitive regulation, and scale-integrated explanation.

The result is not a rejection of organicism, but a reconstruction and operationalisation of its strongest explanatory intuitions within a more precise framework for biological organisation.

## What Was Organicism?

Organicism emerged during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as a response to two dominant approaches to life.

The first was mechanistic reductionism, which attempted to explain organisms entirely through machine-like interactions among isolated components. The second was vitalism, which proposed that living systems depended upon special non-physical forces irreducible to ordinary physical processes.

Organicism rejected both positions.

Against reductionistic mechanism, organicists argued that organisms could not be adequately understood as simple assemblies of independently functioning parts. Biological systems exhibited integration, regulation, development, persistence, and coordinated organisation that could not be fully captured through atomistic decomposition alone.

Against vitalism, most organicists insisted that living systems remained entirely natural systems. The distinctive features of life did not require mysterious vital forces, but instead emerged from the organised relations among components within the organism itself.

Although organicism took many forms, several recurring themes characterised the broader tradition:

- organisms are organised wholes rather than mere collections of parts,
- biological explanation requires attention to system-level organisation,
- parts derive their functions from their roles within the larger system,
- living systems actively maintain themselves across time,
- biological causation is distributed across multiple organisational scales,
- organisms exhibit continuity-preserving regulation and development.

These themes strongly influenced later developments in systems biology, developmental systems theory, autonomy theory, process philosophy, cybernetics, enactivism, and contemporary organisational approaches to biology.

## What Organicism Correctly Recognised

Many of the central intuitions of organicism remain fundamentally important.

Living systems are not static objects assembled from independently meaningful components. Organisms persist only through continuous processes of self-production, regulation, repair, exchange, adaptation, and environmental coupling. Biological intelligibility therefore depends upon understanding the organised relations through which living systems maintain themselves across time.

APS strongly agrees with this general orientation.

In APS, living systems are understood as viability-oriented organisations sustained through organised persistence. Biological explanation therefore cannot be reduced exclusively to the local behaviour of isolated components detached from the larger systems within which those components acquire biological significance.

An enzyme, membrane, signalling pathway, developmental process, or behavioural pattern matters biologically only insofar as it contributes to the continuity and viability of the organised system within which it participates.

APS also agrees with the organicist insight that biological explanation is inherently multiscale. Organisms are neither reducible to molecular events nor adequately explained through global descriptions detached from underlying processes. Biological intelligibility instead depends upon understanding how processes occurring across multiple organisational scales contribute to the persistence of the system as a whole.

Similarly, APS shares the organicist emphasis on temporality and persistence. Organisms are not fixed structures but continuously maintained organisational processes whose identities depend upon regulated continuity rather than static material sameness.

In these respects APS clearly belongs within the broader organism-centred and organisational traditions of theoretical biology.

## What Classical Organicism Could Not Fully Explain

Despite these important insights, historical organicism often remained insufficiently precise as an explanatory framework.

Many organicist accounts correctly recognised the importance of organisation while failing to specify exactly what forms of organisation distinguished living systems from other complex physical systems. Terms such as “integration”, “wholeness”, or “organismic order” were often invoked without explicit criteria capable of distinguishing biological organisation from non-living forms of physical organisation.

As a result, organicism sometimes drifted toward explanatory vagueness.

Classical organicism also frequently lacked operational diagnostics. Living systems were often characterised descriptively rather than through explicitly tractable organisational criteria capable of guiding empirical investigation.

APS attempts to address these limitations directly.

Rather than appealing generally to organismic wholeness, APS specifies organisational features associated with living systems, including:

- viability-oriented organisation,
- endogenous constraint maintenance,
- perturbation-sensitive self-maintenance,
- scale-integrated regulation,
- organisational persistence,
- continuity-preserving reorganisation,
- constraint closure.

These concepts provide explanatory and diagnostic structure absent from many earlier organicist approaches.

Historical organicism also sometimes adopted an overly anti-mechanistic orientation. APS rejects this opposition. Mechanistic explanations remain indispensable within biology. However, mechanisms themselves become biologically intelligible only within the organised systems whose persistence they contribute to maintaining.

## APS and Organised Persistence

APS does not merely claim that organisms are integrated wholes.

APS instead argues that living systems are intelligible because they sustain organised persistence through viability-oriented activity across time.

This is a stronger and more explicit explanatory claim than classical organicism typically provided.

In APS, organisms continuously regulate the conditions necessary for their own persistence. Biological organisation is therefore understood not as static structural arrangement but as dynamically maintained continuity architecture.

Living systems persist through:

- viability-oriented regulation,
- coordinated organisational activity,
- environmental coupling,
- repair and compensation,
- developmental continuity,
- adaptive reorganisation,
- perturbation-sensitive self-maintenance.

Biological explanation therefore concerns how organised systems maintain continuity under changing conditions.

Organisation matters because persistence depends upon it.

## Mechanism Within Organised Persistence

APS does not reject mechanistic explanation.

Mechanistic decomposition remains indispensable for biological investigation because living systems are experimentally tractable through perturbation, intervention, and analysis of organised component relations.

However, APS rejects the assumption that mechanisms are explanatorily self-sufficient.

Mechanisms are biologically meaningful only insofar as they participate in larger systems of organised persistence.

In APS, mechanisms are understood as locally stabilised organisational processes embedded within broader continuity architecture. Their biological significance derives from the ways they contribute to viability maintenance, persistence regulation, repair, adaptation, and organisational continuity.

Mechanistic explanation therefore remains valid and indispensable, but it is situated within a larger account of viability-oriented organisation.

APS thus preserves the empirical strengths of mechanistic biology while rejecting atomistic reductionism.

## Organisation, Function, and Endogenous Normativity

APS also differs from many historical forms of organicism through its explicit treatment of biological normativity.

In APS, normativity does not arise from external evaluative frameworks imposed upon living systems. Instead, normativity emerges endogenously from the viability conditions governing organised persistence itself.

Functions are therefore understood relationally in terms of their contributions to the maintenance of viability-oriented organisation.

Processes, structures, and behaviours become biologically functional because they contribute to the persistence of the organised system across time.

This allows APS to explain why biological systems exhibit purposive and self-regulating behaviour without invoking either vital forces or externally imposed teleology.

Purpose, function, malfunction, adaptation, and regulation become intelligible as features of systems organised around the preservation of viability and continuity.

## Why APS Is Not Holism

APS is also not simply a form of holism.

APS does not appeal to mysterious organismic forces, irreducible wholes, or anti-analytic metaphysics. Nor does it reject decomposition, experimentation, or mechanistic investigation.

Instead APS argues that biological explanation requires understanding the reciprocal relations between parts, processes, organisation, and persistence across multiple scales.

Wholes and parts are therefore understood relationally rather than oppositionally.

Decomposition remains scientifically indispensable, but the explanatory significance of decomposed components depends upon the organisational systems within which those components participate.

APS therefore integrates mechanistic analysis within a broader framework of continuity-oriented organisational explanation.

## APS as an Organisational Explanatory Framework

APS is therefore not merely a philosophical orientation or metaphysical interpretation of life.

APS aims to function as an explicit explanatory grammar for biological systems organised around:

- agency,
- process,
- scale,
- viability,
- persistence,
- continuity regulation,
- organisational maintenance.

Rather than treating organisation as a vague descriptive property, APS attempts to specify the organisational conditions under which living systems persist through time.

This gives APS a more operational and diagnostically tractable structure than many earlier forms of organicism.

APS therefore attempts to preserve the strongest insights of organism-centred biology while integrating them within a continuity-oriented explanatory architecture capable of supporting empirical investigation, mechanistic analysis, and multiscale biological explanation.

## Beyond Organicism

APS belongs historically within the broader organisational and organism-centred traditions of theoretical biology, but it moves beyond classical organicism in several important respects.

APS:

- operationalises organisational explanation,
- grounds normativity in viability conditions,
- integrates mechanism within organised persistence,
- treats continuity as explanatorily central,
- specifies diagnostic organisational criteria,
- reconstructs biological explanation around persistence and viability.

The explanatory importance of organisation therefore arises not from abstract appeals to wholeness alone, but from the observable dynamics through which living systems maintain themselves across time.

APS preserves the strongest explanatory insights of organicism while reconstructing them within a more explicit and biologically tractable framework centred on organised persistence and continuity-oriented explanation.

## Introduction

Biology is an empirical science, but its explanatory problems are not exhausted by empirical observation. Research into organisms, genes, evolution, physiology, ecology, and behaviour repeatedly raises questions about the concepts through which biological phenomena are identified and explained. What is life? What distinguishes living from non-living systems? What is biological function? What makes something an organism? Why do living systems appear purposive? What is adaptation? What is cognition? Why do agency, normativity, organisation, meaning, and teleology continually reappear within biological explanation? Questions of this kind have long made biology a central subject of philosophical inquiry (Hull 1974; Godfrey-Smith 2014).

They arise not because biological science has failed to produce successful empirical explanations, but because empirical success does not by itself settle what those explanations presuppose, what their central concepts mean, or how different explanatory approaches relate. Philosophy of biology examines these conceptual foundations, explanatory structures, and ontological commitments. It asks not only how biological processes occur, but what counts as an adequate biological explanation and what forms of organisation make living systems biologically intelligible.

A central reason these questions persist is that living systems maintain continuity despite continual change. Organisms replace material components, alter their structure, undergo development, respond to their surroundings, reproduce, and participate in evolutionary change while sustaining forms of organisation through which continued functioning remains possible. APS describes this continuity of living organisation through ongoing material and structural transformation as **organised persistence**. Organised persistence is therefore something biological explanation must account for; it is not itself the definition of life.

[[box:description-is-not-explanation]]

## What Is Philosophy of Biology?

Philosophy of biology is concerned with the conceptual, explanatory, and ontological foundations of biological science (Hull 1974; Godfrey-Smith 2014). Its questions range from the nature of life, function, adaptation, information, individuality, cognition, teleology, and biological organisation to broader issues concerning reduction, emergence, explanation, and the relation of biology to physics and chemistry.

Such questions are unusually persistent because biology studies systems that maintain and regulate themselves while undergoing continual change. Living systems develop, reproduce, adapt, evolve, and remain coupled to changing environments. Their material composition is not fixed, yet their organisation exhibits forms of continuity. Their activities can succeed or fail relative to conditions required for continued functioning, and biological explanation therefore repeatedly invokes distinctions such as functional and dysfunctional, adaptive and maladaptive, regulated and dysregulated.

These features do not make biology independent of physics or chemistry. They do, however, generate explanatory questions concerning organisation, history, function, and normativity that are characteristically biological (Mayr 1982; Dupré 1993; Godfrey-Smith 2014). Philosophy of biology investigates how these questions should be formulated and what kinds of explanatory resources are needed to address them.

The field therefore does more than comment on biological discoveries from outside biology. It examines concepts already operating within biological practice. When biologists describe a structure as functional, a response as adaptive, an organism as an individual, or a regulatory process as maintaining a viable condition, they are already relying upon distinctions whose meaning and explanatory role can be philosophically examined.

## Why Biology Generates Philosophical Problems

Biology occupies a distinctive explanatory position because living systems combine causal and dynamical processes with organisation oriented toward continued functioning. Physical systems exhibit causation, interaction, transformation, and temporal change. Living systems exhibit these as well, but their activities are also organised in ways that maintain boundaries, coordinate processes, repair disruption, regulate internal conditions, develop, reproduce, and respond adaptively to changing circumstances.

These features generate recurring questions about function, purposiveness, individuality, normativity, identity, and persistence. A heart can function well or badly. A regulatory process can maintain viability or fail to do so. A developmental trajectory can remain within viable bounds or depart from them. An organism can continue through substantial material turnover while retaining organisational continuity. Such distinctions are not adequately characterised merely by listing the components involved; they concern how those components and processes are organised in relation to the continued functioning of the living system.

Mechanistic explanation remains indispensable to understanding how biological activities are materially realised. The philosophical issue is not whether mechanisms matter, but whether every biologically relevant explanandum is exhausted by identifying mechanisms. For some questions, a mechanistic account may be sufficient. For others, an organisational analysis may identify dependencies or relations that the relevant mechanistic account does not make explicit. Whether that provides additional explanation cannot be assumed in advance; it must be determined relative to the explanandum and the established comparator.

This comparative restraint matters because philosophy of biology is concerned not merely with generating alternative descriptions, but with understanding explanatory contribution. An organisational vocabulary does not gain explanatory force simply because it redescribes mechanisms in different terms. The relevant question is whether it clarifies dependencies, conditions, or relations required for the phenomenon being explained.

Philosophy therefore returns within biology not because empirical biology is incomplete in some general sense, but because successful biological inquiry repeatedly generates questions about what its explanations explain, how different explanatory modes relate, and what organisational conditions biological concepts presuppose.

## Organised Persistence and Biological Explanation

APS proposes that **life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. This definition identifies living systems not by a particular substance, molecule, structure, or isolated process, but by an organisation through which the conditions required for continued functioning are maintained and re-established.

Constraint closure is central to this account because the constraints that regulate biological processes are themselves generated, maintained, or renewed through the organisation they help sustain (Moreno and Mossio 2015). Viability is distinct from constraint closure. It concerns the asymmetry between conditions that permit continued organisation and those that lead toward loss of functioning or breakdown. The two concepts therefore answer different questions: constraint closure concerns how the conditions of continued organisation are mutually maintained, whereas viability distinguishes the conditions under which that organisation can continue.

Living organisation is never materially static. Organisms maintain boundaries, coordinate metabolism, repair damage, respond to environmental change, reorganise during development, and reproduce while undergoing continuous material turnover. **Organised persistence** names the continuity of this organisation through such change. It is therefore neither an additional component of life nor a result produced by Agency, Process, and Scale. It is the persistence of living organisation across ongoing transformation.

Once living systems are understood in this way, biologically consequential asymmetries become unavoidable. Some conditions sustain organisation while others undermine it. Some activities contribute to continued functioning while others do not. Regulatory outcomes can therefore differ in relation to the viability of the system itself.

APS interprets these differences as the basis of biological normativity. Normativity does not require conscious judgement or external evaluation. It arises from viability-relative asymmetry within living organisation: conditions and activities differ in their consequences for the system's continued organisation. Organised persistence is what is sustained through these relations; it is not the independent source from which normativity emerges.

This distinction is important because it prevents several APS concepts from collapsing into one another. Life identifies the form of organisation under consideration. Viability identifies the relevant asymmetry among conditions. Constraint closure concerns how conditions of continued organisation are mutually maintained. Organised persistence identifies the continuity of that organisation through change. Each concept therefore performs a different explanatory function within the same biological organisation.

## A Brief Historical Overview

Questions concerning life, organisation, purpose, and biological explanation have a long history. Aristotle already treated living beings as forms of organised activity whose parts and processes could not be understood solely through material composition. Modern biology transformed these questions rather than eliminating them. Evolutionary theory, genetics, physiology, developmental biology, molecular biology, ecology, systems theory, and theoretical biology progressively altered what could be explained empirically while simultaneously creating new questions about the kinds of explanation appropriate to living systems (Mayr 1982).

Twentieth-century biology often placed strong emphasis on genes, molecular mechanisms, reduction, and informational explanation. These approaches generated major explanatory successes. At the same time, other traditions continued to emphasise process, development, organisation, autonomy, ecology, emergence, and systems dynamics (Dupré 1993; Nicholson and Dupré 2018; Moreno and Mossio 2015).

These traditions need not be arranged as mutually exclusive alternatives. Their explanatory contributions depend upon the phenomena and questions to which they are applied. Molecular mechanisms may explain the production of a particular cellular outcome, developmental accounts may explain how structures arise through time, evolutionary accounts may explain historical transformation, and organisational accounts may ask how processes and constraints are related to conditions required for continued functioning.

Philosophy of biology became increasingly important as the field in which such explanatory differences could be examined explicitly. Its task is not simply to choose among biological traditions, but to clarify their concepts, assumptions, explananda, and relations.

## Teleology, Teleonomy, and Biological Normativity

Few problems illustrate the persistence of philosophical questions within biology more clearly than purposiveness. Living systems routinely behave in ways that can be described as directed toward outcomes: wounds heal, organisms regulate temperature and metabolism, developmental processes converge on organised forms, and behaviour changes in response to conditions relevant to survival and reproduction. Such descriptions easily invite the language of goals and purposes.

Classical teleology interpreted purposive organisation through final causes or intrinsic ends. Modern biology largely rejected teleological explanations where they appeared to invoke external design, metaphysical purpose, or causation by future states. The problem, however, did not disappear when these explanations were rejected. Biology still required ways of describing the organised, goal-directed character of many living processes.

The concept of **teleonomy** emerged as one response. Pittendrigh, Mayr, Monod, and later commentators used teleonomic language to distinguish biologically organised goal-directedness from metaphysical or future-directed teleology, commonly relating such organisation to evolutionary history and inherited biological organisation (Pittendrigh 1958; Mayr 1982; Monod 1971; Corning 2019). Contemporary discussion continues to examine how far teleonomy can naturalise purposive biological language and how it relates to function, adaptation, organisation, and evolutionary explanation (Corning et al. 2023).

Evolutionary history is indispensable to explaining why many traits and capacities exist, but historical selection and present organisation answer different questions. Explaining how a regulatory system arose does not by itself settle how that system presently organises activity in relation to conditions required for continued functioning. APS addresses this present-tense question organisationally without treating historical explanation as dispensable.

Within APS, purpose does not require intention, foresight, external design, or future-directed causation. It refers to **viability-oriented organisation**: biological activity is organised in relation to conditions bearing upon continued functioning. This formulation does not compete with evolutionary explanations of how such organisation arose. It identifies a distinct present-tense relation within the living system.

Normativity follows from the same viability-relative structure. Because some conditions support continued organisation while others impair or terminate it, biological outcomes are not equivalent for the living system. Function and dysfunction, successful and failed regulation, and adaptive and maladaptive outcomes acquire significance through these viability-relative differences.

Organised persistence is consequently the continuity sustained through this organisation, not the source from which normativity or purposiveness independently arises. The philosophical importance of teleology and teleonomy lies precisely here: they reveal how biological explanation repeatedly encounters organised relations to viability even after metaphysical purpose has been excluded.

## Mechanism and Organisation

Mechanistic explanation is indispensable to biology. Molecular interactions, physiological processes, developmental pathways, and regulatory dynamics are often understood by identifying the entities, activities, and causal organisation through which particular biological outcomes are produced. Nothing in APS requires these explanatory achievements to be displaced.

The philosophical question concerns what a specified mechanism explains and whether the explanandum extends beyond the production of the immediate outcome. A mechanism may explain how a regulatory response occurs, for example, while a further question concerns how that response contributes to maintaining or re-establishing conditions required for continued functioning. The distinction is not between an inadequate mechanistic explanation and a superior organisational one. It is between explanatory questions that may require different relations to be made explicit.

APS therefore approaches mechanism and organisation comparatively. For a specified biological explanandum, it asks whether attention to the organisation of viability-relevant dependencies contributes explanatory information not already supplied by the established account. Sometimes it may. In other cases, the established mechanistic explanation may already capture the relevant dependencies, or organisational terminology may simply redescribe what is already known. Redescription alone does not constitute explanatory gain.

This comparative approach also avoids treating mechanism and organisation as different causal levels. Biological organisation is materially implemented through the processes and constraints that constitute living activity. Where processes occurring across different spatial or temporal extents influence local biological activity, that influence must itself be materially realised through constraints that alter local conditions. Wider-scale organisation does not supersede local causation.

Mechanistic and organisational explanations can therefore be complementary without being automatically complementary in every case. Their relation must be established by asking what each explains, which dependencies each identifies, and whether their explanatory contributions genuinely differ.

## Evolution and Historical Organisation

Evolution transformed biological explanation by establishing that present biological organisation has a history. Organisms cannot be understood solely through their present structure because traits, developmental systems, regulatory capacities, and organism–environment relations have themselves been transformed across generations (Mayr 1982).

APS accepts the explanatory importance of established evolutionary accounts. Population-genetic, phylogenetic, ecological, developmental, and other evolutionary approaches explain different aspects of biological change, and an organisational description does not replace those explanations simply by redescribing their outcomes.

APS instead asks whether evolutionary change can also be illuminated by examining the historical transformation of the organisation through which living systems persist. On this interpretation, evolution concerns not only changes in particular traits or their frequencies but changes in the forms and conditions under which living organisation is maintained across generations. Developmental organisation, ecological relations, adaptive capacities, and lineage continuity can thereby become relevant to understanding how organised persistence itself changes historically.

This is an APS interpretation of evolutionary organisation, not a replacement definition of evolution. Its explanatory value must be assessed relative to the phenomenon under investigation and to the evolutionary explanations already available. Where it identifies dependencies or continuities that those explanations leave unresolved, it may contribute additional understanding. Where it merely restates established evolutionary relations in APS terminology, it does not.

The philosophical significance of evolution is therefore broader than any single explanatory framework. Evolution brings history into the constitution of biological explanation and raises continuing questions about how historical transformation relates to present organisation. APS addresses one part of that problem by examining how viability-oriented organisation changes while biological continuity is maintained across generations.

## Cognition, Meaning, and Life

Biology increasingly intersects with philosophy of mind and cognitive science as questions concerning cognition, information, representation, meaning, intelligence, and consciousness are extended beyond their traditional human and animal contexts. These developments raise a fundamental biological question: what must living organisation be capable of before its activity should count as cognitive rather than merely regulatory?

APS approaches this problem organisationally. Living systems continually encounter conditions that differ in their consequences for viability. Biological agency is the viability-oriented organisational activity through which such systems maintain and re-establish conditions required for their persistence. Within this activity, biological evaluation generates significance as conditions acquire relevance in relation to continued functioning.

Cognition requires more than this basic evaluative organisation. APS therefore does not infer cognition from life itself, nor does it treat every instance of regulation, responsiveness, or adaptive activity as cognitive. The relevant question is how biological significance becomes organised in ways that extend beyond immediate or fixed regulation and support activity across temporally structured possibilities.

This places cognition within the investigation of living organisation without collapsing cognition into life. Meaning, semiosis, evaluation, and cognition can be investigated in relation to viability-oriented organisation and organised persistence while retaining the distinctions required to determine where cognition begins. Life does not require cognition; cognition presupposes life.

The philosophical importance of this relation is considerable. Questions traditionally associated with mind can then be approached through the biological organisation from which increasingly complex forms of evaluation and significance arise, without assuming that the explanation of biological organisation by itself resolves consciousness or subjective experience.

## Why Conceptual Clarification Matters

Philosophy of biology matters because biological science depends upon concepts as well as observations. Terms such as life, function, adaptation, agency, cognition, information, organisation, individuality, and purpose do not merely label independently given objects. They help determine what phenomena are distinguished, what questions are asked, and what counts as an explanation.

Conceptual ambiguity can therefore become explanatory ambiguity. If function is alternately treated as historical effect, present contribution, or observer-assigned usefulness, apparently competing explanations may be addressing different questions. If organisation means sometimes spatial arrangement, sometimes causal interaction, and sometimes the maintenance of viability-relevant dependencies, explanatory claims may appear to agree while referring to different relations. Similar difficulties arise when agency is equated with intention, normativity with conscious judgement, or persistence with mere continued existence.

Philosophical clarification helps expose these differences. It can distinguish explananda, identify assumptions, clarify the relations among explanatory approaches, and show where disagreement is substantive rather than terminological. It can also reveal when a proposed explanation adds no new dependency and therefore amounts primarily to redescription.

This does not place philosophy above empirical biology. Conceptual analysis cannot determine biological facts independently of evidence, and philosophical coherence cannot substitute for empirical adequacy. The relationship is reciprocal: biological findings constrain philosophical interpretation, while conceptual clarification helps determine what those findings are being asked to explain.

For APS, this relationship is especially important because its central concepts are intended to perform distinct explanatory functions. Life, viability, constraint closure, agency, process, scale, and organised persistence cannot be allowed to collapse into interchangeable descriptions. Their value depends upon whether the distinctions they introduce clarify biological organisation and contribute to explanation.

## APS Within Philosophy of Biology

APS belongs within the broader landscape of philosophy of biology and theoretical biology because it addresses longstanding questions concerning life, organisation, function, normativity, purpose, cognition, persistence, and explanation. It does not do so by proposing that established biological approaches should be replaced by a single alternative theory.

Its canonical starting point is that **life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. This organisation consists in the ongoing modulation of constraints through which a living system maintains and re-establishes conditions required for its own persistence. Organised persistence describes the continuity of this living organisation through material and structural change.

Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections of that one organisation rather than independent components, causes, levels, or interacting domains. **Agency** concerns what living systems do. **Process** concerns how continuity is maintained despite change. **Scale** concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. The distinctions are analytic; the organisation and activities they disclose are biologically real.

Adaptation, evolution, normativity, semiosis, and cognition should therefore not be placed alongside Agency, Process, and Scale as additional APS dimensions. They are biological phenomena whose organisation can be investigated through these analytic projections where doing so clarifies the explanandum.

APS is developed as a comparative explanatory methodology for biology, but comparative explanatory methodology must also be distinguished from the particular APS account subjected to it. The comparative method requires explanatory proposals to be assessed against specified explananda and relevant established comparators. APS cannot determine the outcome of that comparison merely by supplying its own vocabulary or ontology.

This imposes an important discipline on APS itself. An organisational formulation provides explanatory gain only when it identifies relevant dependencies, distinctions, or relations that contribute beyond what the comparator already explains. A reformulation that merely redescribes established findings in terms of viability, organisation, agency, process, or scale does not thereby improve the explanation.

APS can therefore be understood as an organisational proposal operating within a wider philosophical and methodological inquiry into biological explanation. Its contribution is not secured by explanatory priority but by comparative performance.

## Conclusion

Philosophy of biology matters because the explanatory success of biological science does not eliminate questions about the concepts, relations, and forms of organisation through which living phenomena become intelligible. Living systems undergo continual material, structural, developmental, ecological, and evolutionary change while maintaining and re-establishing conditions required for continued functioning. Their continuity through such transformation is organised persistence.

This feature of life generates recurring questions concerning function, normativity, purposiveness, agency, individuality, cognition, and meaning. Such questions cannot be settled simply by declaring biology irreducible to physics or by assuming that organisational explanation must supplement every mechanistic account. They require careful specification of what is being explained and of the relations upon which the explanation depends.

APS approaches these problems from the proposition that **life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. Agency, Process, and Scale are analytic projections of that one organisation: Agency concerns what living systems do, Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change, and Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. Organised persistence names the continuity of living organisation through transformation rather than an additional component or independent explanatory principle.

This organisational account does not acquire explanatory priority merely by being expressed in APS terms. Mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental, ecological, and other established explanations retain their explanatory achievements. APS must demonstrate any additional contribution relative to specified explananda and comparators, and redescription alone does not constitute explanatory gain.

Philosophy of biology therefore matters to APS for a precise reason. It clarifies the questions that biological explanation generates, the concepts through which those questions are expressed, and the standards by which proposed answers can be distinguished. APS offers one systematic organisational response to those questions, while comparative assessment determines what that response actually explains.

Life persists, evolution transforms that persistence, and biological explanation clarifies the organisation that makes both possible.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

Why does biological inquiry repeatedly generate philosophical questions concerning life, organisation, persistence, function, normativity, purpose, individuality, cognition, meaning, and explanation?

### Architectural Role

This article provides the philosophical orientation for APS by explaining why conceptual, explanatory, and ontological questions arise from biological inquiry itself. It establishes why biological organisation is legitimately a subject of philosophical as well as empirical investigation without treating APS's philosophical commitments as evidence of explanatory success.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

The article builds on the recognition that biological explanation concerns living systems whose organisation is maintained through continual material and structural change. It therefore presupposes the distinction between viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and the organised persistence of that organisation through change.

### Subsequent Explanatory Developments

The questions identified here develop into more specific APS investigations of biological reality, organisation, function, normativity, purpose, agency, cognition, and explanation. **APS as Philosophy** examines the philosophical implications of APS's organisational commitments, while comparative explanatory methodology establishes how explanatory proposals are assessed against specified explananda and established comparators.

### Related Explanatory Questions

- How should biological organisation be understood? 
- What distinguishes description from explanation? 
- How should function, normativity, and purpose be naturalised? 
- What makes an organisational account explanatorily informative rather than merely redescriptive? 
- How should alternative or complementary biological explanations be compared?

### Position Within APS

This article explains **why the philosophical questions arise**. It does not establish APS as their preferred solution. APS supplies an organisational account centred on viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation, but that account remains subject to comparative explanatory assessment. The article therefore connects the philosophical foundations of APS with its explanatory methodology while preserving the distinction between philosophical orientation, substantive APS proposals, and the comparative assessment of their explanatory contribution.

## Introduction
Biology explains an extraordinary range of phenomena. It investigates how molecular mechanisms operate, how organisms develop, how physiological systems regulate activity, how populations evolve, how ecological interactions unfold, and how living systems respond to changing conditions. These explanatory achievements employ different methods, concepts, models, and evidential standards because they address different questions.

APS does not propose that these forms of explanation become biological only when they are redescribed in organisational terms. Nor does it assume that causal, mechanistic, informational, developmental, evolutionary, ecological, or other explanations are incomplete simply because they do not explicitly invoke APS concepts. Explanatory adequacy must first be assessed relative to the explanandum an explanation is intended to address.

APS nevertheless identifies an important explanatory problem that cuts across many areas of biology: **organised persistence**. Living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their continuity despite material turnover, environmental perturbation, developmental transformation, physiological change, and, over longer temporal extents, historical and evolutionary transformation. Explaining how this is possible requires attention to organisation as well as to the particular mechanisms and processes through which that organisation is materially realised.

This article develops the APS approach to that problem. Its claim is not that organised persistence replaces the diverse explananda of biology, but that it constitutes a significant explanatory target in its own right. Where an established biological explanation and an APS organisational analysis address the same target, any additional explanatory gain must be demonstrated through target-matched comparison rather than inferred from APS architecture alone.

**Where This Article Fits**

*This article occupies the point at which APS's substantive account of living organisation meets its methodology of biological explanation. Biological Explanation — What Needs to Be Explained introduces organised persistence as an explanatory problem, while the present article asks how that problem can be investigated without treating it as the universal target of biological explanation.*

*Its role is therefore both substantive and methodological. It develops organisational continuity analysis for cases in which organised persistence is genuinely at issue, while preserving the adequacy of causal, mechanistic, developmental, evolutionary, ecological, informational, and other explanations for the explananda they address. Where APS and another approach address the same target, any claim of additional explanatory gain requires target-matched comparison and materially relevant evidence.*

*The article should therefore be read as establishing a controlled APS approach to organised-persistence explanation, not as claiming that all biological explanation must be reconstructed in APS terms.*

## Description, Diagnosis, and Explanation

Description, diagnosis, and explanation perform different but related roles within biological inquiry. Description identifies structures, processes, behaviours, measurements, and patterns. Diagnosis identifies states or conditions of a system, such as whether an organism or subsystem is functioning, failing, recovering, compensating, or remaining within viable bounds. Explanation addresses why a phenomenon occurs, how it is produced or maintained, and which dependencies are relevant to its occurrence.

These activities can overlap without being identical. A biological phenomenon may be described accurately without the dependencies responsible for it being explained. A condition may also be diagnosed without explaining how it arose or how the system maintains, compensates for, or recovers from it. Conversely, explanatory work commonly depends upon accurate description and may be informed by diagnostic distinctions.

APS uses these distinctions when organised persistence is the explanandum. Description can establish what structures, processes, or changes are present; diagnosis can identify the organisational condition of a living system; and explanation can investigate the dependencies through which viable continuity is maintained or re-established. This does not make all biological explanation continuity analysis. It specifies the relationship among description, diagnosis, and explanation when the persistence of living organisation is the problem being investigated.

## Classical Models of Explanation

Philosophy of science has developed multiple influential accounts of scientific explanation. Deductive–nomological approaches emphasised derivation from general laws, causal approaches investigated relations of causal dependence, and mechanistic approaches examined organised entities and activities responsible for particular phenomena. Other traditions have emphasised intervention, unification, statistical relevance, modelling, prediction, or pragmatic understanding.

Biology makes productive use of many of these explanatory resources. Living phenomena depend upon causal interactions, material mechanisms, regularities, historical processes, developmental relations, ecological conditions, and other empirically investigable dependencies. APS therefore has no reason to treat causal or mechanistic explanation as intrinsically deficient.

A different question arises when the explanandum concerns the persistence of living organisation itself. A causal explanation may identify factors producing a particular effect, while a mechanistic explanation may show how a phenomenon is generated. Whether either also explains how the phenomenon contributes to, depends upon, or modifies organised persistence depends on the explanatory target. APS makes those organisational relations available for investigation without assuming that every biological explanation must include them.

## Mechanism and Organisational Context

Mechanistic explanation is indispensable across biology. Molecular interactions, metabolic pathways, physiological regulation, signalling networks, developmental processes, repair systems, and ecological interactions are materially implemented through mechanisms whose investigation has produced some of biology's greatest explanatory successes.

APS asks an additional organisational question where it is relevant: how does a mechanism participate in the maintenance, re-establishment, or transformation of living organisation? A repair mechanism, for example, can be explained in terms of its component operations while also being investigated for its contribution to restoring viable organisation after damage. Physiological regulation can be analysed mechanistically while also being examined in relation to the conditions within which the organism continues functioning.

These explanatory questions should not be conflated. A mechanism does not become biologically explanatory only when its contribution to persistence has been specified, and an organisational description does not supersede the mechanism through which a biological effect is materially produced. Rather, mechanism and organisation may disclose different dependencies relevant to different explananda.

Where the explanandum is organised persistence, mechanistic detail becomes especially important because organisational claims require material implementation. APS cannot establish a biological dependency merely by describing an abstract organisational relation. The relevant constraints, processes, mechanisms, and interactions must be shown to participate in the continuity being explained.

## Organised Persistence as an Explanatory Target

Living systems remain organised through continual change. Their material constituents turn over, cells divide and die, tissues remodel, physiological states fluctuate, development transforms bodily organisation, behaviour alters organism–environment relations, and ecological circumstances change. Yet biological continuity can be maintained across these transformations.

APS calls this explanatory problem **organised persistence**: how living organisation maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its continuity despite change. Organised persistence is not a second definition of life. Life is **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. Organised persistence identifies the continuity problem generated by that organisation: how it continues functioning while the material and relational conditions through which it is realised continually change.

Two APS commitments are especially relevant to this problem. Viability identifies the conditions within which living organisation can continue, while constraint closure concerns the mutually sustaining organisation of constraints through which the system contributes to maintaining the conditions of its own activity. These concepts provide resources for investigating persistence, but their presence in APS architecture does not by itself explain any particular biological case.

Organised persistence should therefore be treated as an explanatory target rather than as a universal criterion for biological explanation. Some biological investigations directly concern persistence, regulation, repair, resilience, development, identity, or continuity and may therefore be especially amenable to organisational analysis. Others address explananda for which organised persistence is peripheral or irrelevant. The significance of the APS framing must be established relative to the question being asked.

## Agency, Process, and Scale in Biological Explanation

APS organises its account through Agency, Process, and Scale. These are **analytic projections rather than components of reality**. They do not identify independent causes, entities, domains, or hierarchical levels. Each provides a way of examining the same viability-oriented, constraint-closed living organisation.

**Agency** concerns what living systems do. Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity: the present-tense activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained.

**Process** concerns how continuity is maintained despite change. It directs attention to the temporal organisation through which living systems persist while their material constituents, states, and relations are continually transformed.

**Scale** concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. It directs attention to the extent over which dependencies relevant to a particular explanatory target are materially organised without presupposing a hierarchy of biological levels.

These analytic projections do not constitute mandatory ingredients of every biological explanation. Their explanatory relevance depends upon the explanandum. Where organised persistence is being investigated, however, they can help distinguish questions concerning activity, continuity through change, and the spatial and temporal extent of the organisation involved.

APS therefore uses Agency, Process, and Scale to organise inquiry rather than to stipulate in advance what every adequate biological explanation must contain.

## Explanation as Organisational Continuity Analysis

When organised persistence is the explanandum, biological explanation can be approached as **organisational continuity analysis**. The task is to identify the materially implemented dependencies through which living organisation maintains or re-establishes viable conditions across change.

Such analysis may ask how viability is maintained under particular circumstances, which organisational relations are required for continuity, how perturbations alter those relations, which processes compensate for disruption, and how repair or reorganisation restores viable functioning. Developmental, physiological, behavioural, ecological, and evolutionary processes may become relevant where they contribute to the persistence or transformation being explained.

This approach does not require every relevant dependency to be expressed in APS terminology. Established mechanistic, developmental, ecological, evolutionary, and other explanations may already identify much or all of the relevant biology. The APS question is whether organising those dependencies around continuity reveals something explanatorily consequential that the comparator explanation does not already provide.

Organisational continuity analysis therefore generates assessable questions rather than an automatic explanatory verdict. It must identify the relevant dependencies, show their material implementation, specify the contrast or perturbation against which their role becomes visible, and establish whether the resulting analysis changes explanatory discrimination or understanding.

## Perturbation and Explanatory Analysis

Perturbation is especially useful for investigating organised persistence because disruption can reveal dependencies that remain difficult to distinguish under stable conditions. When a system continues functioning successfully, multiple processes may covary in ways that obscure which relations are necessary, compensatory, redundant, or incidental. Perturbation can separate these possibilities.

Injury can reveal dependencies involved in repair; physiological stress can expose compensatory regulation; developmental disturbance can reveal relations required for reliable reconstruction; and environmental change can disclose the conditions within which organismal activities remain viable. Failure can likewise reveal where continuity depends upon relations that were not evident while the system remained within its ordinary operating range.

Perturbation is therefore both an empirical and explanatory resource. It does not automatically disclose organisation, and breakdown does not by itself identify the dependencies responsible for successful persistence. Carefully designed perturbation, however, can provide evidence about how materially implemented constraints and processes alter the conditions under which continuity is maintained.

This makes perturbation particularly valuable for testing APS Dependency Hypotheses. If an organisational relation is claimed to matter for persistence, perturbation can help determine whether altering that relation changes the relevant biological outcome and whether the proposed dependency adds explanatory discrimination beyond an established account.

## Scale and the Organisation of Persistence

Biological dependencies relevant to persistence may be organised across different spatial and temporal extents. A physiological explanandum may depend upon molecular, cellular, tissue, organismal, or environmental relations, but these descriptions should not be treated as a fixed hierarchy of explanatory levels. What matters is where the dependencies relevant to the explanandum are materially organised.

APS uses Scale to make this question explicit. Scale concerns **where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents**. The appropriate extent cannot be determined in advance from APS architecture. It depends upon the phenomenon being explained and upon the evidence showing which relations make a difference to it.

This avoids two opposite errors. Restricting explanation prematurely to a narrow spatial or temporal extent may exclude dependencies that materially affect the explanandum. Conversely, invoking wider organisation merely because it is broader or more inclusive does not establish explanatory gain. A wider explanatory frame is useful only when the additional relations it introduces are relevant to the target.

Cross-scale influence should therefore be understood through materially implemented constraints and interactions that alter local conditions, not through one level of organisation superseding another. APS does not assign universal explanatory priority to any spatial or temporal extent.

## Process and Biological Persistence

Living organisation is maintained through activity and transformation rather than through material stasis. Metabolic turnover, development, repair, physiological adjustment, behaviour, reproduction, and ecological interaction all involve change, while evolutionary processes transform biological organisation across historical time.

Process-oriented approaches have consequently emphasised the inadequacy of treating organisms as materially fixed objects. APS shares the importance placed on temporal organisation but does not identify biological organisation with process as such. Many non-living systems also change continuously. The APS question concerns the organisation through which biological processes participate in viability-oriented persistence.

Process therefore provides one analytic projection of living organisation. It asks how continuity is maintained despite change and which transformations are compatible with, contribute to, or undermine continued functioning. This can be investigated through concrete mechanisms and dependencies rather than inferred from the fact that a system is dynamic.

Where process analysis contributes explanatory gain, that gain must arise from what it reveals about the specified explanandum. The mere redescribing of an established mechanism or historical sequence as a process does not constitute additional biological explanation.

## Explanatory Adequacy and Explanatory Gain

Explanatory adequacy is relative to an explanandum. A mechanistic explanation may be highly adequate for explaining how a molecular pathway produces a particular effect without addressing organismal persistence. An evolutionary explanation may account successfully for changes in trait frequencies without explaining physiological regulation. An ecological explanation may identify dependencies governing population dynamics without supplying an account of individual biological organisation.

APS therefore does not impose organised persistence as a universal test of explanatory adequacy. Instead, it asks whether an organisational analysis is relevant to the target under investigation and, where it is, whether it provides explanatory gain relative to an adequate comparator.

For an organised-persistence explanandum, several questions may guide the analysis:

- What continuity is being explained?
- Which viability-relevant conditions must be maintained or re-established?
- Which materially implemented constraints and processes make a difference to that continuity?
- What happens when the proposed dependency is perturbed?
- Does the organisational analysis reveal a dependency, contrast, or pattern not already captured by the comparator explanation?
- What evidence would distinguish genuine explanatory gain from redescription?

These questions are diagnostic rather than constitutive of biological explanation as such. Their purpose is to make APS claims assessable.

Explanatory gain is therefore both **target-relative and comparator-relative**. Greater conceptual breadth, organisational integration, or philosophical coherence does not establish gain. Nor does the ability to redescribe an existing explanation in APS vocabulary. Gain requires showing that the APS analysis improves explanation of the specified target relative to the strongest relevant alternative.

## Toward an Organisational Approach to Biological Explanation

APS develops an organisational approach to biological explanation by making the persistence of living organisation an explicit object of investigation. It asks how viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation is materially enacted, how continuity is maintained and re-established through change, and which dependencies become visible when that organisation is perturbed.

This approach can draw upon causal, mechanistic, developmental, evolutionary, ecological, informational, mathematical, and other explanatory resources. Their relationship to APS should not be predetermined. In some cases an established explanation may already capture the dependencies relevant to organised persistence. In others, organisational analysis may integrate relations that were previously investigated separately, reveal a dependency omitted by the comparator, or generate a new empirical question. In still others, APS may provide no additional explanatory gain.

These possibilities are methodologically important because they prevent organisational explanation from becoming insulated from comparison. APS should succeed where its analysis improves understanding of a specified biological problem and should return a null result where it does not.

The resulting position is therefore stronger than the claim that all biological explanations are ultimately explanations of organised persistence. APS instead proposes that organised persistence is a significant biological explanandum and provides a controlled methodology for investigating when organisational analysis makes an explanatory difference.

## Why This Matters

Clarifying the status of organised persistence helps connect APS's substantive account of life with its comparative methodology. Concepts such as viability, regulation, function, resilience, repair, adaptation, development, and biological agency often concern relations through which living organisation continues or is transformed, but their explanatory significance cannot be established merely by placing them within a common conceptual architecture.

The organisational question becomes scientifically productive when it generates discriminating hypotheses about materially implemented dependencies. Why does one system recover from perturbation while another fails? Which constraints permit continuity through developmental transformation? Which forms of regulation are necessary for maintaining viable conditions? Which organism–environment relations alter the conditions under which persistence is possible? Such questions can be investigated empirically and compared with established explanations.

This also clarifies the role of APS in relation to contemporary theoretical biology. APS need not treat existing frameworks as competing alternatives, incomplete fragments, or automatically complementary contributions to a single explanatory scheme. Instead, it can ask which explananda they address, which dependencies they identify, where their targets genuinely overlap, and whether APS contributes additional explanatory discrimination.

In this way, organised persistence functions not as a claim of universal explanatory priority but as a researchable problem around which substantive and comparative APS work can be organised.

## Conclusion

Biological explanation is plural because biology investigates many different phenomena. Causal, mechanistic, developmental, evolutionary, ecological, informational, mathematical, and organisational explanations may each be adequate for the questions they address. APS does not acquire explanatory priority simply by redescribing these achievements in the vocabulary of organisation.

APS nevertheless identifies **organised persistence** as an important explanatory target: how viability-oriented, constraint-closed living organisation maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its continuity despite material turnover, perturbation, and transformation. Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections through which aspects of that problem can be investigated, while mechanisms, constraints, and interactions provide its material implementation.

The resulting explanatory task is empirical as well as conceptual. Organisational dependencies must be specified, materially grounded, and tested against relevant contrasts or perturbations. Where an established explanation already accounts adequately for the same target, APS must show what additional explanatory work its organisational analysis performs.

APS therefore approaches biological explanation through a disciplined question rather than a predetermined answer: **when organised persistence is the explanandum, what organisation makes that persistence possible, and does making that organisation explicit improve the explanation?**

## What Does Biology Explain?

Biology explains many different phenomena. It investigates how genes are expressed, how molecular and physiological mechanisms operate, how organisms develop, how behaviour changes in relation to environmental conditions, how ecological interactions are organised, and how populations and lineages evolve. These explanations employ different methods and identify different dependencies because they address different explananda.

There is therefore no reason to assume that biological explanation must ultimately reduce to a single explanatory question. A mechanistic explanation of a molecular pathway, an evolutionary explanation of a trait distribution, and an ecological explanation of population dynamics can each succeed relative to their own targets without first being reconstructed as explanations of living organisation.

APS nevertheless asks a further question. Living systems are themselves distinctive forms of organisation whose continuity is maintained despite continual change. Their material constituents turn over, their physiological states fluctuate, development transforms them, their relations with their environments change, and their organisation can be disrupted and restored. How such systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their continuity is therefore a biological problem requiring explanation.

APS calls this problem **organised persistence**. It proposes organised persistence as a principal explanatory target for investigating living organisation, not as the obligatory explanandum of biology as a whole.

For a guided entry into how APS develops biological explanation, see:

→ [How APS Explains Life — A Two-Step Guide](/orientation/how-aps-explains-life/)

## Where This Article Fits

This article establishes the explanatory target that precedes the more detailed APS analysis of biological explanation. Its question is not how all biological explanations should be structured, but why the persistence of living organisation constitutes an explanatory problem in its own right.

The distinction matters because APS does not infer from the existence of living organisation that every biological explanandum must ultimately be redescribed in terms of organised persistence. Instead, it asks when continuity, viability, repair, regulation, transformation, or related features of living organisation are themselves at issue and therefore require organisational explanation.

The successor article, *Biological Explanation and Organised Persistence*, takes the next step. It asks how organised persistence can be investigated, what materially implemented organisational dependencies would count as explanatory, and under what conditions an APS analysis provides explanatory gain relative to an adequate comparator. The present article therefore establishes the **explanandum**; the successor establishes the controlled methodology for investigating it.

## Mechanism and Living Organisation

Mechanistic explanation has been extraordinarily productive across biology. By identifying relevant entities and activities and showing how their organised interactions produce phenomena, mechanistic inquiry has transformed understanding of molecular biology, physiology, development, neuroscience, and many other biological fields.

APS does not treat these explanations as intrinsically incomplete. If the explanandum concerns how a molecular pathway produces an effect, a mechanistic explanation may provide precisely the explanation required. The fact that it does not also explain the persistence of the organism does not constitute a defect if organismal persistence was not the target.

A different explanatory question arises when the explanandum concerns living organisation itself. Mechanisms can operate in systems that do not maintain the conditions of their own continued activity, whereas living systems are organised so that their processes contribute to maintaining and re-establishing conditions required for continued functioning. Where this organisation is the target, identifying component operations alone may not answer the question being asked.

APS therefore supplements mechanistic inquiry with an organisational question where the explanandum requires it: how do the relevant mechanisms participate in the maintenance, re-establishment, or transformation of living organisation?

This does not establish organisational explanation as superior to mechanistic explanation. It distinguishes two possible explanatory targets. Where both approaches address the same target, their comparative adequacy must be assessed rather than inferred from their different vocabularies.

## From Description to an Organisational Explanandum

Biological investigation depends upon accurate description. Structures must be identified, pathways traced, activities measured, interactions characterised, and patterns established before many explanatory questions can be answered. Description and explanation are therefore closely connected even though they are not identical.

APS becomes relevant when the explanatory question concerns the organisation through which a living system continues functioning despite change. In that context, describing what processes occur does not by itself establish how those processes contribute to maintaining or re-establishing viable organisation.

The relevant question is therefore not merely:

> What happens?

but, where organised persistence is the explanandum:

> How do these processes contribute to the maintenance or re-establishment of living organisation through change?

This formulation is deliberately conditional. It does not turn every biological process into a persistence mechanism or require every biological explanation to answer an APS question. It specifies what must be investigated when the continuity of living organisation is itself the explanatory target.

## Organised Persistence as an APS Explanatory Target

Living systems do not persist by remaining unchanged. Their molecular constituents are continually replaced, cells divide and die, tissues remodel, physiological conditions fluctuate, behaviour changes, development transforms organismal organisation, and relations with surrounding environments continually alter.

Continuity is therefore compatible with extensive transformation. APS uses **organised persistence** to identify the explanatory problem posed by this continuity through change.

Organised persistence is not a second definition of life. APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. Organised persistence identifies what follows as an explanatory problem once living organisation is understood in this way: how does such organisation maintain and re-establish the conditions of its continuity despite the transformations through which it exists?

Two APS concepts are especially relevant to this explanatory target. **Viability orientation** concerns the organisation through which activities bear differently upon the conditions required for continued functioning. **Constraint closure** concerns the organisation in which constraints are maintained through processes that those constraints in turn help regulate or enable.

Together these concepts provide APS with resources for investigating how living organisation persists, but they do not establish the explanation of any particular case merely by being invoked. A biological explanation requires specification of the relevant processes, constraints, dependencies, and material implementation.

Organised persistence therefore functions as an explanandum. APS must still show how persistence is achieved in particular systems and under particular conditions.

## Agency and Biological Normativity

Organised persistence is enacted through biological activity. APS calls this **biological agency**: viability-oriented organisational activity. Agency is not an additional force acting alongside biological mechanisms, nor does it require consciousness, deliberation, representation, or intention. It identifies what living systems do as they enact and sustain their organisation.

This activity is normatively structured in a specifically biological sense. APS understands biological normativity as **viability-relative asymmetry**. Conditions, processes, and outcomes differ in how they bear upon the maintenance and re-establishment of viable organisation. Some contribute to continued functioning, some make little relevant difference, and others undermine or destroy the organisation on which continued functioning depends.

The resulting asymmetry does not require an external observer to assign biological value. Nor does it imply mental evaluation or moral judgement. It arises from the material organisation of a system for which different conditions have different consequences for viability.

This provides an organisational basis for investigating concepts such as function, regulation, success, failure, and malfunction. Within APS, function concerns present-tense viability-relative organisational contribution. A process can therefore be investigated not only in terms of what effect it produces but, where relevant, in terms of how that effect bears upon the organisation whose persistence is being explained.

Again, this is an APS explanatory question rather than a mandatory component of every biological explanation.

## Process and Scale in Biological Explanation

Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary **analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed living organisation**. They are not independent components, causes, domains, or hierarchical levels.

**Agency** concerns what living systems do. **Process** concerns how continuity is maintained despite change. **Scale** concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

Process is particularly important to organised persistence because living continuity is realised through transformation rather than stasis. Metabolism, development, regulation, repair, behaviour, ecological interaction, and evolution involve changes occurring over different temporal extents. The explanatory question is not simply whether change occurs, but which transformations participate in maintaining, re-establishing, or historically transforming living organisation.

Scale makes a related issue explicit. Dependencies relevant to persistence may be materially organised across different spatial and temporal extents. Which extents matter cannot be determined in advance from APS architecture; they must be identified relative to the explanandum and supported by evidence.

APS therefore does not search for a privileged explanatory level or treat wider scales as superseding local mechanisms. Cross-scale influence must be materially implemented through constraints and interactions that alter relevant local conditions. Scale-sensitive explanation asks where the dependencies responsible for the phenomenon are organised.

Agency, Process, and Scale thus organise APS inquiry into living organisation without functioning as universal requirements imposed upon biological explanation generally.

## Genes, Traits, Evolution, and Organised Persistence

Genes, traits, populations, and evolutionary processes are indispensable objects of biological explanation. APS does not treat explanations centred upon them as deficient merely because they do not foreground organised persistence.

Living systems are nevertheless materially required for many of the phenomena such explanations investigate. Gene expression occurs within organised biological systems; development depends upon continuing biological activity; reproduction requires organisms or other reproducing biological organisation; and evolutionary processes operate through histories of variation, inheritance, persistence, reproduction, and differential success.

These relations can motivate **dependency questions**, but dependency must not be confused with explanatory priority. The fact that phenomenon X depends upon living organisation does not establish that an explanation of living organisation is a better explanation of X. Nor does it show that an established genetic or evolutionary explanation is incomplete.

APS can instead ask a more discriminating question: where an explanandum genuinely involves the relation between genetic, developmental, evolutionary, or other processes and organised persistence, what dependencies connect them, how are those dependencies materially implemented, and does making them explicit improve the explanation?

This converts a broad architectural intuition into an assessable explanatory hypothesis. Possible outcomes include genuine explanatory gain, useful integration, redescription of dependencies already known, or a null result in which the APS framing adds no consequential explanation.

## Explanatory Adequacy within APS

What counts as an adequate explanation depends upon the explanandum, the explanatory product sought, the dependencies claimed, and the evidence appropriate to them. APS therefore does not use organised persistence as a universal criterion for judging biological explanations.

A causal explanation may adequately identify what makes a difference to a biological outcome. A mechanistic explanation may adequately show how a phenomenon is produced. A developmental explanation may account for the generation of form, an evolutionary explanation for historical transformation, and an ecological explanation for interactions or population dynamics. None is required to answer an organised-persistence question unless that question forms part of its explanandum.

Where APS specifically investigates organised persistence, however, explanatory adequacy requires more than naming viability, organisation, or constraint closure. The analysis must identify how the proposed processes or relations bear upon the maintenance, re-establishment, or transformation of living organisation and must provide evidence appropriate to those claims.

This distinction is essential to APS methodology. Conceptual coherence, broader integration, or the ability to redescribe an existing explanation in organisational terms does not establish explanatory gain. Where APS and another approach address a matched target, comparative preference must depend upon what additional explanatory work the APS analysis performs.

Organised persistence therefore establishes a question to be investigated, not a verdict about the adequacy of existing biology.

## Why This Matters

Making organised persistence explicit as an explanatory target clarifies a class of questions that recur throughout biology. Regulation, repair, resilience, adaptation, development, function, malfunction, and organism–environment relations can all involve dependencies bearing upon the maintenance or transformation of living organisation.

Their relevance to persistence should not be assumed merely because they concern living systems. Instead, APS asks when those relations actually affect the viability and continuity under investigation. This creates a route from conceptual organisation to empirically assessable dependency claims.

The distinction also clarifies APS's relationship with other explanatory approaches. Mechanistic, developmental, evolutionary, ecological, informational, mathematical, and organisational explanations need not be assigned predetermined complementary roles, nor should they be ranked simply because they employ different concepts. Their relationship depends upon the explanandum.

The same control applies when APS is compared with contemporary theoretical frameworks. The Free Energy Principle, autonomy theory, systems approaches, evolutionary frameworks, and other programmes may address overlapping or different questions concerning living systems. APS must first establish whether their explanatory targets genuinely match before claims of complementarity, competition, redundancy, or explanatory gain can be assessed.

A detailed example is developed in *APS and the Free Energy Principle: Organisation, Formalism, and Explanatory Comparison*.

Organised persistence is therefore valuable not because it converts all biological explanation into APS explanation, but because it identifies a biological problem that can be formulated precisely enough to investigate, compare, and potentially fail to yield additional explanatory gain.

## From Explanatory Target to Explanatory Method

Identifying organised persistence as a principal APS explanatory target is only the first step. Once the explanandum has been specified, further questions arise. Which dependencies are relevant to the continuity being explained? How are they materially implemented? What does perturbation reveal about their contribution? Across what spatial and temporal extents are they organised? Which established explanation provides the appropriate comparator? What, if anything, does the APS analysis add?

These questions belong to the next stage of the explanatory architecture.

The successor article, *Biological Explanation and Organised Persistence*, develops organised-persistence explanation as a controlled form of organisational continuity analysis. It examines how Agency, Process, and Scale can organise inquiry into this explanandum while requiring APS-specific claims to remain target-relative, comparator-relative, materially grounded, and open to null explanatory results.

The transition is therefore from **what needs to be explained within this APS problem-space** to **how that explanatory problem can be investigated**.

## Conclusion

Biology explains many different phenomena through many successful explanatory practices. APS does not propose that these explanations become biological only when they are reconstructed around a single organisational target.

It identifies a more specific problem. Living systems maintain and re-establish their organisation despite continual material turnover, physiological change, developmental transformation, environmental perturbation, and historical change. APS calls this problem **organised persistence**.

Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Organised persistence asks how that organisation continues through change. Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections through which aspects of this problem can be investigated, while viability and constraint closure provide organisational concepts relevant to explaining it.

None of these commitments establishes in advance that APS provides explanatory gain over existing biological explanations. Organised persistence must be specified as an explanandum, its relevant dependencies identified and materially supported, and any comparative gain assessed against an adequate explanation of the same target.

The contribution of this article is therefore deliberately bounded: **it establishes organised persistence as a principal APS explanatory target without making it the universal explanandum of biology.**

The next question is how APS should investigate that target. For this, see:

## Introduction

Living systems have histories.

The organisation through which an organism regulates its activity, repairs damage, develops, reproduces, and interacts with its surroundings did not arise independently of evolutionary history. Contemporary organisms inherit structures, capacities, constraints, and developmental relations shaped through processes extending across generations.

Yet living systems also act in the present.

They maintain and re-establish conditions of viability through materially realised activity occurring now. APS describes this as **biological Agency: viability-oriented organisational activity** (Spencer 2026).

The relationship between these two explanatory perspectives requires care. An evolutionary history can help explain why a present organisation exists in the form that it does, but it does not thereby explain every aspect of how that organisation currently operates. Conversely, explaining present-tense Agency does not by itself explain the historical processes through which the relevant organisation arose or changed.

The central question is therefore:

> **How does present-tense biological Agency relate to historical evolutionary transformation without Agency becoming an evolutionary mechanism, evolution becoming Agency extended through time, or either explanation displacing the other?**

APS distinguishes three questions that can otherwise easily be conflated:

1. **Historical dependence:** how is present Agency conditioned by organisation with an evolutionary history?
2. **Evolutionary consequence:** when can materially realised agential activity alter conditions relevant to evolutionary outcomes?
3. **Explanatory gain:** when does analysing such activity explicitly under Agency improve explanation of a specified evolutionary explanandum?

These questions are related, but they are not equivalent.

## Where This Article Fits

This article examines the explanatory interface between biological Agency and evolution.

It does not redefine biological Agency. **Biological Agency as the Activity of Self-Maintenance** establishes the canonical APS account of Agency and the conditions governing its attribution.

Nor does this article establish why Agency is the defining activity of Life, why Agency does not require intention or cognition, or how Agency relates to organised persistence within the lifetime of a living system. Those questions belong to earlier articles in the Agency corpus.

The present article begins from those results and asks what follows when present-tense viability-oriented activity is considered in relation to evolutionary history.

Its scope is deliberately narrower than a general APS account of evolution. Questions concerning what evolves, lineage continuity, phylogenetic organisation, taxonomy, and the historical transformation of organised persistence belong to the APS treatment of evolution itself.

Here the focus remains on **Agency**.

The article therefore distinguishes the evolutionary history of the organisation through which Agency is enacted from the possible evolutionary consequences of agential activity and from the further question of whether explicit Agency analysis provides explanatory gain.

This prevents present activity and historical transformation from being separated completely while also preventing them from being collapsed into a single explanatory process.

## 1. Present Organisation and Evolutionary History

Every living organism exists through an organisation with a history.

Metabolic pathways, physiological regulatory systems, developmental architectures, sensory capacities, patterns of behaviour, reproductive systems, and organism–environment relations can all depend upon inherited organisation transformed through evolutionary processes.

Present Agency is therefore historically conditioned.

But historical dependence does not mean that evolutionary history provides a complete explanation of present activity.

Consider a regulatory capacity that currently contributes to maintaining viable physiological conditions. Its evolutionary history may help explain why the relevant structures and capacities exist. A present-tense physiological explanation may identify the mechanisms through which regulation occurs. An Agency analysis may ask how that activity is organised relative to conditions bearing upon the viability of the living system.

These explanations need not compete merely because they concern the same biological phenomenon.

They can address different dependencies.

This gives the first distinction required by the Agency–evolution interface:

> **The evolutionary history of an organisation and the present activity enacted through that organisation are distinguishable explanatory targets.**

Evolutionary history can condition the possibilities of present Agency without replacing an explanation of what the living system is currently doing.

Likewise, present Agency can depend upon historically formed organisation without becoming an explanation of its own evolutionary origin.

## 2. Historical Orientation: Organisation and Evolution

The distinction has deep roots in biological thought.

Aristotle investigated organisms as organised living beings whose parts and activities were intelligible in relation to the life of the whole organism. Darwin transformed biological explanation by providing a historical account of evolutionary change and adaptation without requiring external design.

These traditions should not be mapped directly onto APS.

Aristotle is not the historical representative of Agency, and Darwinian evolution is not the APS Process projection. Nor does contemporary biology add Scale as a third stage completing a historical sequence.

Their relevance here is more limited.

They illustrate a persistent distinction between questions about **how living organisation operates** and questions about **how biological organisation has been historically transformed**.

Darwinian explanation also changed the status of purposive language in biology. Adaptive organisation could be explained historically without supposing that organisms or an external designer consciously intended the resulting form.

APS accepts that achievement.

Its account of Agency does not restore intentional design to biology. Biological Agency does not require conscious purpose, and present viability-oriented activity should not be substituted for evolutionary explanations of adaptation.

The remaining question is how historically formed organisation can itself instantiate present-tense activity organised relative to viability.

That is the problem addressed here.

## 3. Historical Dependence

Present Agency depends upon materially realised organisation.

That organisation includes structures, processes, constraints, developmental relations, and capacities whose present forms can be historically conditioned.

This dependence matters because living systems do not begin each generation organisationally unconstrained. They inherit biological arrangements that shape what forms of activity are possible, how development proceeds, how physiological regulation occurs, and how organisms can interact with their surroundings.

Agency is enacted through this inherited and developing organisation.

But the phrase **historical dependence** must not be interpreted as a claim that evolution acts as a continuing external cause of every present agential event.

The immediate causes of a physiological adjustment, behavioural response, repair process, or developmental transition may be entirely present-tense and materially local.

Evolutionary history becomes explanatorily relevant when the question concerns why the organisation enabling those activities has the form, capacities, or dependencies that it does.

Historical and present-tense explanations can therefore intersect without becoming interchangeable.

A complete account of a particular phenomenon may sometimes require both. In other cases, one may be sufficient for the explanandum being investigated.

APS does not decide that question in advance.

## 4. Evolutionary Consequence

The explanatory relation also runs in another sense.

What organisms do can sometimes make a difference to conditions relevant to evolutionary outcomes. Work on niche construction and the extended evolutionary synthesis has emphasised ways in which organismal activity, development, and organism–environment relations can modify circumstances entering evolutionary processes (Odling-Smee, Laland, and Feldman 2003; Laland et al. 2015).

Organisms move, regulate development, alter resource use, modify aspects of their environments, reproduce differentially, interact with other organisms, and change circumstances under which developmental and reproductive processes occur.

Where such activities are instances of viability-oriented organisational activity, they may appropriately be analysed under Agency.

Some of those activities can also have consequences extending beyond their immediate contribution to present viability.

They may alter exposure to environmental conditions, patterns of reproduction, developmental outcomes, ecological relations, or other materially realised circumstances relevant to evolutionary change.

But this does **not** establish Agency as an evolutionary mechanism.

To say that Agency has an evolutionary consequence is only the beginning of an explanation.

The relevant activity must be specified. The material pathway through which it affects an evolutionary dependency must be identified. The historical outcome must be connected to established evolutionary processes appropriate to the explanandum.

The methodological rule is therefore:

> **Where agential activity makes an evolutionary difference, the materially realised activity and the evolutionary dependency through which that difference occurs must be specified.**

“Agency affects evolution” is not by itself an evolutionary explanation.

## 5. Agency Is Not an Additional Evolutionary Mechanism

Contemporary organism-centred approaches have argued that organismal activity can participate causally in evolutionary processes rather than being treated only as an outcome of selection (Walsh 2015).

APS can recognise this contribution without treating Agency itself as an additional causal force.

An organism's activity can alter conditions relevant to selection, reproduction, development, inheritance, ecological interaction, or other processes involved in evolutionary explanations. But the causal work in such cases is performed through materially realised activities and relations.

Agency does not enter the explanation as another mechanism alongside them.

Suppose an organism modifies its environment in a way that changes conditions encountered by itself or its descendants. An Agency analysis may clarify how the relevant activity is organised relative to the organism's viability. An evolutionary explanation may then establish whether and how the resulting environmental modification affects a historical outcome.

Those explanatory contributions should not be collapsed.

The first concerns the organisational character of present activity.

The second concerns the dependencies producing evolutionary change.

Agency can therefore become relevant to an evolutionary explanation without becoming an independent evolutionary mechanism.

This distinction protects APS from turning a useful analytic projection into an unspecified causal variable.

## 6. Evolution Is Not Agency Extended Through Time

The converse error must also be avoided.

Evolution should not be understood simply as Agency operating over longer periods.

Agency concerns viability-oriented organisational activity enacted by living systems in the present.

Evolutionary transformation concerns historical change across generations through materially realised processes and relations appropriate to evolutionary explananda.

Present activity can contribute to historical outcomes, but the existence of that contribution does not erase the distinction between the two.

Nor does organised persistence erase it.

Living systems maintain continuity through change during their lives. Historically extended biological continuities can also become relevant to evolutionary explanation. But this does not mean that organismal Agency simply expands through time until it becomes evolutionary Process.

The relevant question must instead be specified for the explanandum:

> **What organisation persists across the temporal extent relevant to the evolutionary question, through which materially realised relations is that continuity maintained, and how is it transformed?**

This question belongs primarily to the broader APS treatment of evolution.

Its importance here is negative but essential: historically extended continuity does not convert evolution into a temporally enlarged form of Agency.

## 7. Agency and Process

Agency and Process are complementary analytic projections of one living organisation.

Agency asks what living systems do.

Process asks how continuity is maintained despite change.

Evolution should not be identified with Process merely because evolution involves change through time.

Evolutionary theory addresses historical transformation through evolutionary mechanisms, relations, and dependencies. Process, by contrast, identifies an APS explanatory question concerning continuity through change.

The two can intersect.

For example, an evolutionary explanandum may concern the historical transformation of an organisation whose continuity across generations must itself be specified. Process questions may then become relevant to understanding the continuity being transformed.

But this does not make evolution the Process component of APS.

Nor does Process supply an alternative evolutionary theory.

The distinction matters because otherwise a methodological projection becomes confused with the biological phenomena and mechanisms being explained.

## 8. Agency and Scale

Evolutionary questions frequently extend beyond the spatial and temporal extent of an individual organism.

This does not require a hierarchy of explanatory levels.

Scale asks:

> **Where is persistence relevant to the explanandum organised across spatial and temporal extents?**

For some evolutionary questions, relevant dependencies may extend across organisms, generations, populations, developmental histories, ecological relations, or inherited environmental conditions.

These extents matter only through materially realised relations.

A wider scale does not act upon an organism. A lineage does not become an agent merely because its history extends beyond individual lifetimes. Nor does the presence of organismal Agency establish Agency at every broader extent involved in evolutionary explanation.

The task is instead to identify which relations across spatial and temporal extents make a difference to the evolutionary explanandum.

Scale therefore helps prevent a common conflation.

The temporal extension of an evolutionary process does not make it a larger form of Agency, just as the participation of many organisms in a historical process does not automatically create a higher-order agent.

## 9. From Present Activity to Historical Consequence

The Agency–evolution relationship can now be stated more precisely.

A present activity may be:

1. enacted through organisation with an evolutionary history;
2. viability-oriented for the organism in which it occurs;
3. materially consequential for conditions relevant to an evolutionary outcome; and
4. connected through specified evolutionary dependencies to historical transformation.

These propositions must be established separately.

The first concerns **historical dependence**.

The second concerns **Agency**.

The third establishes a possible **evolutionary consequence**.

The fourth belongs to the **evolutionary explanation** itself.

None follows automatically from the others.

A trait can have an evolutionary history without every present use of that trait being explanatorily important to evolution.

An activity can qualify as biological Agency without having significant evolutionary consequences.

An activity can alter an evolutionary condition without Agency terminology improving the evolutionary explanation.

And an evolutionary explanation can be adequate without explicitly invoking Agency.

The distinctions are therefore methodological safeguards against explanatory inflation.

## 10. Organised Persistence Across Different Temporal Questions

The APS account of Agency and organised persistence establishes that they are analytically distinguishable aspects of one living organisation.

That result should not be extrapolated without qualification into evolutionary history.

Within an organismal explanandum, organised persistence may concern continuity of living organisation through regulation, repair, development, and reorganisation.

An evolutionary explanandum may involve a different temporal extent and a different bearer of relevant continuity.

The question of what persists must therefore be determined by the explanandum rather than assumed from the presence of organismal Agency.

This is especially important because biological continuity can be organised through relations that extend beyond the lifetime of one organism.

Inheritance, reproduction, developmental recurrence, population processes, ecological relations, and lineage histories can all become relevant depending upon the evolutionary target.

But their relevance does not imply that all such organisation constitutes Agency.

Agency attribution and the identification of historically relevant continuity remain separate questions.

The Agency–evolution interface therefore requires attention to continuity without indiscriminate extension of agency attribution.

## 11. Explanatory Gain

The presence of Agency in an evolutionary context does not guarantee that Agency provides explanatory gain.

Suppose a well-established evolutionary explanation already specifies the variation, inheritance, developmental relations, ecological conditions, reproductive differences, and historical dependencies relevant to an explanandum.

Adding the statement that the organisms involved are agents may contribute nothing.

APS must permit that result.

The relevant comparative question is:

> **For this specified evolutionary explanandum, what does explicit analysis under Agency explain that the strongest relevant evolutionary comparator does not already explain?**

Several outcomes are possible.

Agency analysis might identify a viability-relative dependency that the comparator leaves implicit. It might show why particular present activities systematically alter conditions entering an evolutionary process. It might clarify a relationship between organismal activity and historical transformation that would otherwise remain underdescribed.

Alternatively, the established comparator may already explain all relevant dependencies.

In that case, the explanatory-gain verdict is null.

APS should not respond by redescribing the same mechanism in agential language and treating the redescription as additional explanation.

Explanatory gain must be demonstrated at the matched target.

## 12. Historical Dependence, Evolutionary Consequence, and Explanatory Gain

The architecture of the Agency–evolution relationship can therefore be expressed through three distinctions.

### Historical Dependence

Present Agency is enacted through living organisation whose structures, capacities, constraints, and relations have evolutionary histories.

This establishes historical conditioning.

It does not make evolutionary history a complete explanation of present Agency.

### Evolutionary Consequence

Some materially realised agential activities can alter conditions relevant to evolutionary outcomes.

This establishes a possible connection between present activity and historical transformation.

It does not make Agency an independent evolutionary mechanism.

### Explanatory Gain

Explicit Agency analysis contributes to an evolutionary explanation only when it identifies something explanatorily relevant that the strongest matched comparator does not already explain.

This establishes methodological significance.

It cannot be inferred merely from historical dependence or evolutionary consequence.

Together these distinctions allow APS to connect present organisation with evolutionary history without collapsing them.

## 13. The Architecture of Biological Explanation

Biological phenomena frequently support more than one legitimate explanatory question.

A present biological organisation can have a history.

A historically formed capacity can be active now.

Present activity can alter conditions with future consequences.

Historical transformation can change the organisation through which future activity occurs.

None of this requires those explanations to become one undifferentiated account.

APS treats Agency, Process, and Scale as **analytic projections rather than components of reality**.

Agency concerns what living systems do.

Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change.

Scale concerns where persistence relevant to an explanandum is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

Evolutionary explanations can intersect with all three questions without being reducible to any one of them.

This is the relevant architecture.

It is not a historical progression from Aristotle through Darwin to APS. It is not a hierarchy in which present organisation occupies one level and evolution another. And it is not a causal sequence in which Agency generates persistence and persistence subsequently becomes evolution.

Instead, the explanatory task is to identify the target, specify the relevant material dependencies, distinguish present activity from historical transformation, and determine whether relating them provides explanatory gain.

## Conclusion

Living systems are simultaneously historical and active in the present.

The organisation through which biological Agency is enacted has an evolutionary history. Present viability-oriented activity can also alter materially realised conditions that sometimes matter to evolutionary outcomes.

These facts establish an important relationship between Agency and evolution, but they do not erase their explanatory differences.

Biological Agency is not an additional evolutionary mechanism.

Evolution is not Agency extended across generations.

Historical dependence does not by itself establish evolutionary consequence, and evolutionary consequence does not by itself establish explanatory gain.

The relationship must instead be reconstructed for particular explananda.

Present Agency can be historically conditioned by inherited organisation. Agential activity can sometimes alter conditions entering evolutionary processes. Where it does, the material pathway connecting activity to historical consequence must be specified. And where Agency is proposed as explanatorily useful, its contribution must be assessed against the strongest relevant evolutionary comparator.

The resulting position is deliberately bounded:

> **Present-tense Agency and historical evolutionary transformation address distinguishable but potentially related explanatory dependencies. Biological Agency is enacted through living organisation with an evolutionary history, and materially realised agential activities can sometimes alter conditions relevant to evolutionary outcomes. Neither fact makes Agency an independent evolutionary mechanism or evolution a temporally extended form of Agency. Their explanatory relationship must be established for a specified explanandum through the material dependencies connecting present activity with historical transformation.**

This is how APS relates Agency and evolution without subordinating one to the other.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How does present-tense biological Agency relate to historical evolutionary transformation without Agency becoming an evolutionary mechanism, evolution becoming Agency extended through time, or either explanation displacing the other?

### Architectural Role

This article occupies the Agency–evolution interface within APS.

It does not provide a general theory of evolution and does not redefine Agency. Its function is to distinguish the historical conditioning of present Agency, the possible evolutionary consequences of materially realised agential activity, and the explanatory contribution Agency analysis can make to specified evolutionary explananda.

### Historical Dependence

Present Agency is enacted through living organisation whose structures, capacities, constraints, and relations can have evolutionary histories.

Historical dependence does not make evolutionary history a complete explanation of present activity.

### Evolutionary Consequence

Materially realised activities analysed under Agency can sometimes alter conditions relevant to evolutionary outcomes.

Such consequences must be connected to specified evolutionary dependencies. Agency itself is not an additional evolutionary mechanism.

### Relationship to Process

Process asks how continuity is maintained despite change.

Evolution involves historical transformation but is not therefore identical with the APS Process projection. Process questions can become relevant to evolutionary explananda where continuity through transformation requires explanation.

### Relationship to Scale

Scale asks where persistence relevant to an explanandum is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

Evolutionary explanation can involve relations extending across organisms and generations without those wider extents becoming higher-order agents or causally acting scales.

### Relationship to Organised Persistence

Agency and organised persistence remain analytically distinguishable.

When evolutionary questions concern historically extended continuity, the organisation whose persistence is relevant must be identified for the specified explanandum. It cannot simply be extrapolated from organismal Agency.

### Evolutionary-Theory Boundary

Questions concerning what evolves, lineage continuity, phylogenetic organisation, taxonomy, and the general historical transformation of organised persistence belong to the APS treatment of evolution rather than to this article.

This article remains focused on the explanatory relevance of present-tense Agency to evolutionary questions.

### Explanatory-Gain Condition

The presence of agential activity does not establish explanatory gain.

For a specified evolutionary explanandum, Agency analysis must be compared with the strongest relevant evolutionary explanation. If the comparator already explains the relevant dependencies adequately, the Agency contribution can be null.

### Corpus Significance

This article establishes a three-part control for the APS Agency corpus:

> **historical dependence → evolutionary consequence → explanatory gain**

The three must not be conflated.

Present Agency can depend upon historically formed organisation without evolutionary history replacing present-tense explanation. Agential activity can have evolutionary consequences without Agency becoming an evolutionary mechanism. And neither relationship establishes explanatory gain unless Agency contributes something to the specified explanandum beyond the strongest relevant comparator.

> **Where this article fits**
>
> This article explains how APS pathway arrows should be interpreted and assessed. Architectural Dependency is an APS-specific way of representing proposed relations among concepts. The Dependency-Hypothesis Audit provides a disciplined procedure for assessing those relations without treating the pathway itself as evidence.

## 1. Introduction

A pathway can appear more convincing than it is.

A sequence such as:

**A → B → C**

may suggest that A produces B, that B is necessary for C, or that the entire sequence has already been established by evidence. None of those conclusions follows from the diagram alone.

An arrow is not evidence. Visual coherence does not establish biological dependence, causal production, constitutive necessity, explanatory priority, or scientific support.

APS therefore treats this problem as a governing methodological constraint.

An APS pathway becomes scientifically assessable only when its arrows can be unpacked into explicit claims whose relation types, evidential bases, limits, alternatives, and failure conditions can be examined independently.

APS calls its interpretation of these pathway relations **Architectural Dependency**.

Architectural Dependency is specific to the APS pathway architecture. The procedure used to assess particular dependency hypotheses is designed for comparative application without presupposing that APS is correct.

The distinction is important:

- **Architectural Dependency** identifies how APS interprets the relations represented by its pathway arrows.
- **The Dependency-Hypothesis Audit** specifies how particular dependency claims can be assessed.
- Neither the arrow nor the pathway establishes the truth of the dependency represented.

## 2. Why APS Uses Explanatory Pathways

APS uses pathways because biological explanation often involves relations among concepts, activities, structures, conditions, and forms of organisation that cannot be displayed clearly in a single definition.

A pathway can serve several limited functions.

It can provide **orientation** by showing which concepts APS places in relation.

It can support **decomposition** by turning a broad framework into separately assessable connections.

It can provide **research guidance** by identifying relations that require evidence, clarification, comparison, or revision.

And it can support **revision** because individual arrows can be retained, qualified, retyped, or rejected without requiring the whole pathway to be defended intact.

For example, APS represents:

**Biological Evaluation → Significance**

and currently defines Biological Evaluation as the process through which agency generates significance.

That formulation identifies an APS relationship. The arrow does not by itself establish whether the relevant biological relation is causal, constitutive, enabling, constraint-based, conceptual, or mixed. Nor does it establish that the proposed relation is empirically supported.

Pathways therefore organise questions and expose connections to scrutiny. They do not settle those questions by their arrangement.

## 3. What Architectural Dependency Is

Architectural Dependency is the APS-specific interpretation of the relations represented by arrows in APS pathways.

It does not propose that all biological systems possess one universal dependency architecture.

It does not assume that every arrow expresses the same kind of relation.

It is not a general theory of biological explanation.

And it is not a fourth analytic projection alongside Agency, Process, and Scale.

Agency, Process, and Scale remain complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of living organisation. Architectural Dependency instead concerns how proposed relations within APS conceptual pathways are specified and assessed.

A pathway arrow therefore records a **dependency hypothesis**.

The hypothesis may eventually receive strong support, remain provisional, require qualification or retyping, prove redundant or redescriptive, yield no additional explanatory gain, or be rejected.

Its placement within APS provides no evidential privilege.

The Dependency-Hypothesis Audit is designed as a framework-neutral assessment procedure: its verdict should depend upon the specified claim, evidence, alternatives, comparator, and failure conditions rather than upon the desirability of preserving APS architecture.

## 4. What an APS Arrow Claims

The most general interpretation of an APS arrow is:

> **APS proposes that specified relata associated with A and B stand in an explanatorily relevant dependency relation under stated conditions.**

That formulation deliberately leaves the relation type open.

An arrow does not automatically mean:

- A causes B;
- A is necessary for B;
- B cannot exist without A;
- A is temporally prior to B;
- A is more fundamental than B;
- A occupies a lower biological level than B;
- A logically entails B;
- or A provides greater explanatory power than alternatives.

Those stronger claims require their own specification and evidence.

A dependency may be:

- **constitutive**;
- **enabling**;
- **constraint-based**;
- **structural**;
- **spatial**;
- **temporal**;
- **historical**;
- **conceptual**;
- or **mixed**.

The relation must therefore be identified before the evidence is assessed.

Where a single arrow compresses more than one relation, the claim should be decomposed or explicitly represented as mixed.

Where no defensible relation survives assessment, the arrow should be rejected rather than protected by its place in the pathway.

## 5. Relation Types and Evidential Requirements

Different dependency relations require different forms of evidence and argument.

The relation types considered here form a provisional and non-exhaustive set. They are analytical distinctions for clarifying dependency claims, not a universal taxonomy imposed upon biological explanation.

### Causal dependency

A causal dependency claims that variation in one specified factor makes a difference to another through materially realised processes or mechanisms.

Evidence may include intervention, perturbation, controlled comparison, mechanistic tracing, temporal ordering, or other forms of causal difference-making appropriate to the system.

Causal evidence does not automatically establish constitution, necessity, or conceptual priority.

### Constitutive dependency

A constitutive claim concerns what participates in making a phenomenon or organisation the kind of thing it is.

Evidence must therefore establish more than causal influence.

A factor may causally affect a system without constituting it. Conversely, a constitutive relation need not be adequately represented as one event producing another.

Constitutive claims require careful specification of the explanandum and of what is being claimed to belong to its organisation.

### Enabling dependency

An enabling relation identifies conditions under which an activity, organisation, or capacity can occur or continue.

Enabling conditions need not directly produce the phenomenon they enable.

The relevant question is whether the specified condition makes a defensible difference to the possibility, maintenance, range, or operation of the target under the conditions being considered.

### Constraint-based dependency

A constraint-based relation concerns how one materially realised organisation restricts, channels, enables, or modulates possibilities available to another process or activity.

Such claims require identification of the constraint, the constrained process, the material implementation of the relation, and the contrast against which constraint is being assessed.

### Structural dependency

A structural relation concerns dependence upon arrangement, topology, organisation, geometry, connectivity, or other materially realised relations among components.

Structural evidence does not automatically establish causal direction.

### Spatial dependency

A spatial dependency concerns how a biological relation depends upon spatial organisation, localisation, distribution, proximity, compartmentalisation, or other spatial conditions.

Spatial association alone does not establish dependency. The relevant spatial relation must make a specified difference to the explanandum.

### Temporal dependency

A temporal relation concerns dependence upon timing, duration, ordering, recurrence, rate, temporal integration, or other temporal organisation.

Temporal precedence alone does not establish causation.

### Historical dependency

A historical dependency concerns cases in which the present explanandum depends upon developmental, evolutionary, ecological, or other historical trajectories.

Historical explanation should not be substituted automatically for present-tense mechanism, nor should present mechanism erase historically relevant dependencies.

### Conceptual dependency

A conceptual dependency concerns relations among definitions, distinctions, explanatory commitments, or other elements of a conceptual architecture.

Conceptual indispensability within APS does not establish a material biological dependency.

A conceptual relation can therefore be valid within the framework while its proposed biological counterpart remains empirically unresolved.

### Mixed dependency

Many biologically important relations combine several of these types.

A relation may, for example, be simultaneously enabling and constraint-based, or historical and constitutive.

Mixed relations should be stated explicitly rather than compressed into an ambiguous arrow.

## 6. How APS Assesses Dependency Claims

The Dependency-Hypothesis Audit is designed to prevent a pathway arrow from carrying more explanatory or evidential weight than its supporting argument warrants.

The procedure contains ten linked requirements.

### 1. Explanandum and foil

State exactly what is being explained and in contrast with what.

This prevents movement between explanatory targets during comparison.

### 2. Claim and relata

Specify what is proposed to depend upon what.

Broad labels should be replaced, where possible, by assessable activities, structures, conditions, organisations, or concepts.

### 3. Relation type

Identify the kind of dependency being claimed.

Do not infer causation, constitution, enabling, necessity, or conceptual priority merely from arrow direction.

### 4. Material or argumentative basis

For a biological dependency, identify how the relevant relation is materially realised.

For a conceptual dependency, specify the argumentative or definitional basis.

The appropriate level of material specification depends upon the explanatory target and should not be replaced by an arbitrary demand for reduction.

### 5. Admissible variation or contrast

Ask what intervention, counterfactual, comparison, perturbation, historical contrast, or conceptual variation would test or clarify the dependency.

The contrast must be appropriate to the relation type and biological system.

### 6. Evidence and inferential limits

Identify what supports the claim and what that evidence does not establish.

Correlation, precedence, perturbation, conceptual coherence, or mechanistic association should not license stronger conclusions than the evidence warrants.

### 7. Alternatives and scope

Identify plausible rival relations, alternative architectures, alternative realisations, and conditions under which the proposed dependency may or may not hold.

### 8. Failure conditions

State in advance what finding would qualify, retype, or defeat the claim.

A dependency hypothesis must remain open to revision.

### 9. Strongest established explanation

Compare the dependency claim with the strongest relevant established explanation for the same explanandum.

The comparator should be represented with its full mechanistic, organisational, historical, or other relevant explanatory resources rather than in a weakened form.

### 10. Comparative-gain or null verdict

Ask whether introducing the dependency claim changes a defensible explanatory assessment.

A comparative-gain verdict requires a specified explanatory contribution that survives comparison.

Where the strongest established explanation already performs the relevant explanatory work, a principled no-gain verdict is appropriate.

The audit is therefore designed for neutral comparative use. It does not require an APS dependency claim to survive.

A rejected, redundant, redescriptive, or no-gain result is a legitimate methodological outcome.

## 7. Feedback, Constraint Closure, Time, and Scale

Dependency relations in living systems frequently occur within reciprocal organisation.

Feedback does not automatically invalidate a directional dependency. Nor does it automatically confirm one.

Suppose APS proposes:

**A → B**

and evidence also supports:

**B → A**

Several outcomes remain possible.

The original A → B relation may remain supported.

Its scope may need qualification.

The relation may need to be retyped as reciprocal or mixed.

Or the asserted asymmetry may fail.

The correct verdict depends upon the specified relata, relation type, evidence, explanatory target, and failure conditions.

Constraint closure is part of APS's canonical account of Life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

But constraint closure does not determine the verdict of an individual dependency claim.

[[box:dependency-and-constraint-closure]]

Reciprocal organisation can therefore be relevant evidence without becoming a predetermined result.

### Time

Dependency claims may extend across different temporal extents.

A relation may concern immediate physiological regulation, developmental transformation, recurrent activity, lifetime organisation, evolutionary history, or combinations of these.

Temporal extension does not itself determine relation type.

Historical dependence, present-tense causation, developmental constitution, and temporal enabling should therefore be distinguished where the explanatory target requires it.

### Scale

Dependency claims may also extend across different spatial extents.

APS does not treat these extents as a hierarchy of causal levels.

Where activity organised across one spatial or temporal extent alters conditions relevant to activity elsewhere, the relation must be materially implemented through identifiable interactions, structures, constraints, environmental conditions, or other dependencies.

Scale difference alone does not establish causal influence or explanatory priority.

## 8. Confidence, Failure, and No-Gain Outcomes

Dependency claims can differ in evidential confidence.

APS distinguishes **strongly supported**, **provisional**, and **speculative** dependency claims.

[[box:strong-provisional-and-speculative-dependencies]]

A strongly supported claim has clearly specified relata and relation type, appropriate material or argumentative specification, substantial evidence, explicit limits and scope, stated failure conditions, and survival against the strongest relevant established explanation.

Strong support does not mean final proof, universality, or immunity to revision.

A provisional claim is sufficiently specified and supported to guide inquiry, but important questions remain unresolved.

A speculative claim identifies an intelligible possibility whose current support is insufficient for provisional acceptance.

Confidence status is distinct from audit verdict.

A dependency may be:

- retained;
- qualified;
- retyped;
- rejected;
- redescriptive;
- redundant;
- associated with comparative explanatory gain;
- or associated with principled no additional gain.

A dependency hypothesis remains scientifically assessable only if evidence and argument can alter its status.

Failure is therefore informative.

If evidence defeats a proposed arrow, APS should revise the arrow.

If the relation is better represented differently, it should be retyped.

If the claim merely redescribes what an established explanation already explains, that should be recorded.

If no additional explanatory gain survives comparison, the correct verdict is no gain.

The pathway is not protected from these outcomes.

## 9. Applying the Method

A compact dependency assessment can proceed as follows.

**First**, identify the precise explanandum.

**Second**, specify the relata.

**Third**, type the proposed dependency.

**Fourth**, identify its material or argumentative basis.

**Fifth**, specify an admissible contrast or variation.

**Sixth**, assess the evidence and its limits.

**Seventh**, examine alternatives and scope.

**Eighth**, state failure conditions.

**Ninth**, reconstruct the strongest established explanation.

**Tenth**, issue a gain or null verdict.

Consider again:

**Biological Evaluation → Significance**

Within APS, Biological Evaluation is defined as the process through which agency generates significance.

That establishes a conceptual relation inside the framework.

It does not by itself establish the corresponding biological dependency.

An empirical dependency claim would require specification of what biological activities count as evaluation, what outcomes count as significance, how the proposed relation is materially realised, what variation would discriminate the claim, what evidence supports it, and what alternatives could explain the same target.

The strongest established explanation would then need to be reconstructed.

Only after that comparison could the proposed dependency be retained, qualified, retyped, rejected, judged redescriptive or redundant, or found to provide comparative explanatory gain or principled no gain.

Conceptual coherence, biological evidence, and comparative explanatory assessment are therefore distinct achievements.

None should be substituted for another.

## 10. The APS Dependency Map

APS uses a Dependency Map to display proposed relations among concepts in its current pathway architecture.

For purposes of displaying that architecture, APS presently uses the following formulations. Their scientific status is not adjudicated by this article.

**Life**

Viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

**Agency**

Viability-oriented organisational activity.

**Biological Evaluation**

The process through which agency generates significance.

**Significance**

Relationally real outcomes of biological evaluation that explain what matters to the living system.

**Integration**

The organisation of biological significance into functional unity.

**Cognition**

The organisation of biological significance across time.

**Mind**

Integrated evaluative cognitive processes maintaining a coherent self–world relation.

**Selfhood**

Continuity of self–world organisation through change.

**Reflective Agency**

The capacity to evaluate, prioritise, and direct one's own agency.

**Meaning**

Significance integrated across experience, reflection, and value.

The sequence should not be read as a chronology, developmental timetable, causal chain, hierarchy of biological levels, or logical deduction.

Each arrow records a proposed dependency relation requiring separate assessment.

The Life → Agency relation, for example, may invite constitutive, enabling, or other interpretations within APS, but its relation type must not be assumed from the pathway or from the compact definitions alone.

Likewise, later arrows may involve different relations and different evidential burdens.

Support for one relation does not establish support for the next.

The Dependency Map is therefore neither an APS doctrine that must be defended intact nor an empirical result already established.

It is a structured representation of separately assessable hypotheses.

An arrow may survive assessment.

Another may require qualification.

Another may need to be retyped.

Another may prove redundant or redescriptive.

Another may provide no additional explanatory gain.

And another may fail.

Revision of one relation does not automatically invalidate neighbouring relations, just as support for one relation does not validate the map as a whole.

## 11. Architectural Dependency within APS Research

Architectural Dependency has both a public-facing and a research-facing role.

Publicly, it explains how APS pathway arrows should be read.

Research-facing, it converts those arrows into claims that can be specified, compared, criticised, revised, and, where appropriate, rejected.

The Dependency-Hypothesis Audit was developed to discipline this assessment.

Its purpose is not to manufacture support for APS but to make proposed dependency claims assessable under explicit burdens and failure conditions.

A completed APS dependency study has reported a disciplined three-case application of the procedure that produced differentiated positive, transfer, and null verdicts.

That application does not establish a universal methodology of biological explanation.

Nor does it establish that the Dependency-Hypothesis Audit is superior to existing comparative explanatory methods.

It provides a worked application of a procedure designed to distinguish cases in which a dependency formulation contributes to an explanatory assessment from cases in which it does not.

Nor does a successful application in one biological case validate the APS Dependency Map.

Each dependency claim retains its own evidential burden.

This separation is essential.

Architectural Dependency belongs to APS.

The Dependency-Hypothesis Audit is designed for comparative use without requiring an APS-positive verdict.

Whether it succeeds in that role must be assessed through its applications rather than inferred from its design.

## 12. Conclusion

APS pathways are useful only if their arrows remain open to scrutiny.

Architectural Dependency therefore treats each pathway arrow as a defeasible dependency hypothesis rather than as evidence, causal fact, logical implication, or guaranteed explanatory advance.

The resulting assessment requires:

- a specified explanandum and foil;
- explicit relata;
- relation typing;
- appropriate material or argumentative specification;
- admissible variation or contrast;
- evidence and inferential limits;
- alternatives and scope;
- failure conditions;
- comparison with the strongest established explanation;
- and a comparative-gain or principled no-gain verdict.

These requirements are not unique operations invented by APS. They draw upon familiar disciplines of scientific and philosophical analysis.

The specific contribution of Architectural Dependency within APS is narrower: it applies those disciplines consistently to the heterogeneous claims compressed within APS pathways.

The scientific credibility of APS pathway-based claims therefore depends heavily on how their arrows are specified and assessed.

An arrow should survive only to the extent warranted by its relation type, evidence, limits, alternatives, failure conditions, and comparative performance.

Some arrows may receive strong support.

Some may remain provisional or speculative.

Some may require qualification or retyping.

Some may prove redundant, redescriptive, or associated with no additional explanatory gain.

Some may fail.

That possibility is not a weakness of the method.

A pathway whose relations can be independently challenged and revised is methodologically stronger than one protected from criticism by the coherence of the diagram in which they appear.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

**How should the arrows connecting APS concepts be interpreted and assessed without treating pathway structure itself as evidence?**

### Architectural Role

This Core article establishes **Architectural Dependency as the APS-specific interpretation of pathway relations** and specifies the Dependency-Hypothesis Audit used to assess individual dependency claims.

It does not establish a new dependency ontology or a general theory of biological explanation.

### Preceding Conceptual Dependencies

This article presupposes the frozen APS distinction between:

- Life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation;
- Biological Agency as viability-oriented organisational activity;
- Agency, Process, and Scale as complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections;
- and Organised Persistence as continuity of living organisation through change.

These are APS conceptual commitments. Their presence in the framework does not by itself validate any particular pathway arrow.

### Methodological Commitments

Architectural Dependency requires that:

- arrows be treated as hypotheses rather than evidence;
- relation types be identified rather than inferred from diagram direction;
- biological dependencies be materially specified where appropriate;
- conceptual dependencies be distinguished from material biological dependencies;
- evidence be matched to the relation claimed;
- alternatives and failure conditions remain explicit;
- the strongest established explanation be reconstructed fairly;
- comparative gain not be presumed;
- and no-gain, redundancy, redescription, retyping, qualification, or rejection remain legitimate outcomes.

### Subsequent Use

The method can be applied to individual APS pathway relations in later research.

Such applications must not infer the validity of an entire pathway from one successful dependency assessment.

Nor may a pathway definition substitute for empirical evidence where the proposed relation is biological rather than merely conceptual.

### Position Within APS

Architectural Dependency provides a methodological control on APS pathway architecture.

It turns visual connections into separately assessable dependency hypotheses while preserving the possibility that particular relations may survive, change, or fail.

Its purpose is therefore not to secure the pathway but to make the pathway answerable to explicit explanatory and evidential assessment.
