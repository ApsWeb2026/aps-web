# Why Life Is Not Control Theory

Control theory has profoundly shaped modern understandings of regulation, stability, coordination, and organisation.

Across cybernetics, physiology, systems biology, neuroscience, robotics, artificial intelligence, and related fields, control-theoretic approaches investigate how systems regulate variables, respond to perturbation, coordinate activity, maintain dynamically significant conditions, and alter their behaviour through feedback.

These approaches do not constitute a single homogeneous theory. Some control systems operate relative to externally specified targets or setpoints. Biological and cybernetic approaches may instead investigate endogenous regulation, distributed control, adaptive coordination, reciprocal feedback, and dynamically maintained relations among interacting processes. Control-theoretic explanation therefore cannot be identified simply with centralised command, fixed targets, engineering design, or externally imposed goals.

These frameworks provide powerful explanatory and mathematical resources for understanding biological regulation.

APS accepts their scientific importance and does not claim that control-theoretic explanation is merely descriptive, intrinsically mechanistic, externally imposed, or incapable of explaining biological organisation.

The narrower claim of this article is one of non-identity.

Within APS:

**Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

This definition does not identify life with control architecture, feedback, regulation, stability, optimisation, or the maintenance of particular variables. Living systems may exhibit these phenomena, and control-theoretic approaches may provide substantive explanations of them, without control thereby constituting the APS definition of life.

The resulting difference is therefore a point of comparison rather than a verdict about explanatory superiority. Where APS and a control-theoretic approach address the same biological explanandum, their relative adequacy must be assessed through target-matched comparison rather than inferred from their conceptual architecture.

[[box:how-aps-relates-to-other-theoretical-frameworks]]

## What Control Theory Explains Well

Control-theoretic approaches provide substantive explanatory resources for many important biological phenomena, including:

- feedback regulation
- homeostasis
- behavioural stability
- coordinated adjustment
- dynamic regulation
- adaptive responses to perturbation
- distributed regulation among interacting processes

They also provide powerful formal resources for analysing regulatory dynamics, coupled systems, feedback relations, stability, adaptation, and coordination.

These are not merely descriptions of processes awaiting a separate organisational explanation. Depending upon the explanandum, a control-theoretic account may provide a substantive, sufficient, or preferable biological explanation.

Control-theoretic approaches also differ in what they seek to explain and how control is characterised. Some applications concern specified variables and target states. Others investigate distributed regulation, endogenous feedback, adaptive coordination, or dynamically maintained relations within biological systems. No single control architecture should therefore stand for control theory as a whole.

APS approaches these explanations with a different substantive proposal about life itself. It defines life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and asks how that organisation maintains and re-establishes continuity through change.

>> **Where this article fits:** This is a Life-demarcation clarification. It asks whether genetic replication or a gene-centred description should be identified with life itself. It does not adjudicate the comparative adequacy of gene-centred evolutionary theories as a class. Broader comparison belongs to *Gene-Centric Biology and APS* and to the APS comparative-explanation programme.

## A Familiar Idea — and Its Problem

“Life is DNA’s way of making more DNA” is a memorable expression of the importance of heredity and replication. It captures a genuine feature of biological continuity: genetic material is transmitted across generations, heritable differences contribute to evolutionary change, and DNA plays a central role in familiar terrestrial life. The difficulty arises only when this explanatory importance is converted into an identity claim about life itself.

Read modestly, the slogan draws attention to the evolutionary significance of genetic replication, a role APS has no reason to deny. Read more strongly, however, it suggests that living systems are fundamentally vehicles whose biological significance is exhausted by the replication of genetic material. It is this stronger identification, rather than the explanatory importance of genes, that APS rejects.

The distinction matters because an account can explain an important biological process without thereby defining the organisation in which that process occurs. Genetic replication, inheritance, and evolutionary change are central features of familiar life, but their explanatory importance does not establish that life itself should be identified with the replication of genetic material.

## What Genetic Explanations Explain

Gene-centred, population-genetic, molecular, and other evolutionary approaches provide substantive explanations of biological phenomena. They illuminate inheritance and genetic variation, differential transmission, evolutionary change, the consequences of selection, and the persistence of heritable differences across generations. These are genuine explanatory achievements and should not be reduced to mere description simply because APS organises its account of life around a different explanatory target.

The question addressed here is therefore deliberately narrower: **does the explanatory importance of genetic replication establish that life itself should be identified with genetic replication?** The answer is no. An explanation of how heritable material is transmitted, how genetic variants change in frequency, or how selection acts upon heritable differences need not also constitute a definition of living organisation.

Keeping these questions separate is methodologically important. If genetic and organisational explanations address different explananda, their difference does not establish that one has displaced the other. Only where they address a genuinely matched biological target does comparative explanatory adequacy become the relevant question.

## Replication Is Not a Definition of Life

DNA replication is a biological process occurring within organised material systems. In familiar cellular life, replication depends upon molecular machinery, energy supply, regulation, membrane organisation, appropriate chemical conditions, and other processes through which cells maintain the conditions under which genetic activity can occur. DNA therefore does not replicate biologically in isolation from organised cellular activity.

This is a material dependency claim, but its implications must be kept bounded. The dependence of genetic replication upon organised cellular conditions does not by itself establish that APS provides a superior explanation of genetics, heredity, or evolution, nor does it imply that every explanatory question concerning genes should be reformulated in APS terminology. A molecular or population-genetic explanation may be entirely adequate for the explanandum it addresses.

What the dependency does establish is more limited. The existence and replication of DNA within living systems should not be confused with a definition of the living organisation in which those processes occur. Genetic replication can be indispensable to the continuity of familiar biological lineages without being identical with the organisation that makes those lineages living systems.

## Life in APS

APS defines life as:

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

This organisation consists in the ongoing modulation of constraints through which a system actively maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence. Life, on this account, is not identified with a particular component or isolated process but with the organisation through which biological continuity is actively maintained despite material and environmental change.

Within that organisation, **biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity**. Agency is therefore not an additional component alongside DNA, metabolism, regulation, membranes, or other biological processes. It names the activity through which viability-oriented living organisation is enacted and sustained.

Constraint closure contributes to specifying this organisation by describing relations in which relevant constraints are maintained through the activity of the system whose organisation they help make possible. Genetic processes can participate materially in these relations and can be indispensable to their continuation across generations. Their participation, however, does not make genetic replication identical with the organisation as a whole.

The distinction is therefore not between genes and organisation as competing biological realities. Genes are part of living organisation, and their activities contribute to it. The question is whether one important class of biological processes should be identified with life itself. APS answers that question negatively.

## Genes Within Living Organisation

Recognising this distinction does not diminish the biological importance of genes. Genetic processes contribute to the production and regulation of biological components, developmental continuity, inheritance, phenotypic variation, and evolutionary change. Their effects are extensive, and in many explanatory contexts a genetic account may be sufficient or preferable.

**Where This Article Fits**

*This article is part of the APS programme of testing the framework against its strongest explanatory comparators. The comparison follows the protocol established in [Comparative Explanatory Methodology in Theoretical Biology](/articles/comparative-explanatory-methodology-theoretical-biology/): the approaches are compared through their explanatory targets, organising principles, conceptual architectures, characteristic strengths, and explanatory scope before conclusions about their relationship are drawn. It does not ask whether APS and enactivism use the same concepts, or whether one framework should replace the other. It asks the more restricted question of what explanatory difference follows when APS and a strongest biologically grounded enactivist account are required to address the same biological target.*

*The article therefore applies the APS distinction between architectural difference and comparative explanatory gain. Its conclusion is deliberately bounded: APS and biologically grounded enactivism remain architecturally non-identical, but for the common target examined here neither has been shown to establish an explanatory dependency unavailable to the other. Questions concerning other enactivist targets, including the relation between life and cognition, remain open to separate target-matched investigation.*

## Introduction

APS and enactivism occupy substantially overlapping explanatory territory. Both take living organisation seriously. Both reject pictures of organisms as merely passive recipients of environmental causes. Both address agency, normativity, regulation, organism–environment relations, and the biological conditions under which features of the environment can matter to an organism. Important enactivist accounts additionally connect these concerns to sense-making, cognition, and continuity between life and mind.

These similarities do not make APS an enactivist framework. APS defines life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and biological agency as the viability-oriented activity through which living organisation sustains the conditions of its persistence. It distinguishes agency from Biological Evaluation, significance, and cognition, while treating Agency, Process, and Scale as analytic projections of one organised biological reality. Biologically grounded enactivism develops a different conceptual architecture around autonomy, precariousness, adaptivity, normativity, sense-making, and agency.

The existence of two architectures, however, raises a harder question than whether their concepts are identical. Different frameworks can partition the same biological problem differently without thereby explaining different things. Conversely, apparently similar terminology can conceal important differences in the dependencies each framework claims are required for an explanandum.

The relevant question is therefore not whether APS can be shown to be conceptually distinct from enactivism. It can. The question is what explanatory difference follows from that distinction when both approaches are required to address the same biological target.

This article performs that comparison. It first establishes a common explanatory target without building either APS or enactivist terminology into the question. It then reconstructs the strongest biologically grounded enactivist answer independently of APS. Only after that reconstruction is APS admitted to the comparison. The two accounts are then compared dependency by dependency, with differences in terminology or conceptual granularity excluded as evidence of explanatory gain unless they establish a corresponding difference in what must be explained or in the dependencies required to explain it.

The result is deliberately non-triumphal. APS and biologically grounded enactivism remain architecturally non-identical. Yet for the target examined here, their differences do not presently establish comparative explanatory gain for either framework. The case therefore illustrates a more general methodological point: **architectural difference is not yet explanatory difference, and explanatory difference is not yet explanatory superiority**.

## Architectural Difference Is Not Yet Explanatory Difference

Comparison between general biological frameworks can easily become comparison between vocabularies. One framework speaks of autonomy where another speaks of organisation; one invokes adaptivity where another distinguishes evaluation and regulation; one describes sense-making where another distinguishes significance from cognition. Such contrasts can be philosophically important, but their explanatory significance cannot be inferred merely from the existence of different terms.

At least four questions must be distinguished.

First, are the concepts themselves different? Two frameworks may define their terms differently or divide the explanatory territory at different places.

Second, do those conceptual differences produce different explanatory architectures? A framework may treat two phenomena as distinct where another treats them as aspects of a single relation, or may place an explicit dependency between processes that another describes within a broader category.

Third, does the architectural difference establish a difference in explanatory dependency? That requires showing that one account identifies something as necessary for explaining the target that the other account omits, denies, or cannot represent without altering its explanation.

Fourth, does the dependency difference produce comparative explanatory gain? Even a genuine difference does not automatically favour the framework that contains the additional distinction. The additional dependency must improve explanation of the matched target rather than merely increase conceptual detail.

These questions establish a sequence of increasingly demanding claims:

**conceptual difference → architectural difference → explanatory dependency difference → comparative explanatory gain**

The arrows in this sequence are not automatic implications. Each transition requires independent justification.

This matters especially for APS. APS has developed an increasingly differentiated explanatory architecture in which life, biological agency, Biological Evaluation, significance, integration, cognition, organised persistence, function, normativity, and scale-sensitive organisation are not interchangeable. That differentiation can increase the precision with which biological questions are posed. It does not follow that APS explains a phenomenon better than another framework that partitions the same explanatory dependencies differently.

## Introduction: Why Life Is Not Information Processing

Across biology, neuroscience, cognitive science, cybernetics, systems theory, and artificial intelligence, it has become increasingly common to describe living systems as information processors. Cells detect signals, organisms respond to environmental differences, neural systems transform sensory inputs, genomes are described in informational terms, cognition is modelled computationally, and behaviour may be analysed through the processing and transmission of signals. From this perspective, biological organisation can be investigated in terms of acquiring and processing information, coordinating responses, storing instructions, and transmitting consequential differences within and between systems.

Informational approaches have been scientifically powerful and extraordinarily productive, and APS fully recognises their importance within biology. They provide substantive explanatory resources for understanding many aspects of signalling, regulation, coordination, communication, learning, cognition, and adaptive responsiveness.

APS nevertheless rejects the stronger identity claim that information processing by itself constitutes the APS definition of life. Living systems may process, transmit, transform, and respond to information, but APS defines life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. This establishes a difference in explanatory architecture rather than a verdict about comparative explanatory adequacy. Whether informational approaches can also provide substantive explanations of living organisation, and whether either approach provides explanatory gain where their targets coincide, are separate comparative questions.

## Different Explanatory Architectures

Informational approaches encompass a heterogeneous range of explanatory practices involving signalling, coding, communication, computation, representation, regulation, information flow, and related concepts. These approaches need not assign the same theoretical status to information, nor need they treat information processing as the defining principle of life.

APS organises its account differently. It defines life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and distinguishes biological evaluation, biological significance, semiosis, information, representation, cognition, and meaning within its own explanatory architecture.

These differences do not establish that informational concepts are explanatorily downstream of APS concepts, nor that viability, evaluation, or semiosis are universally prior to information. Where APS proposes necessary, asymmetric, or ordering relations among these phenomena, those relations should be treated as proposed Dependency Hypotheses requiring assessment rather than as consequences established by the architecture itself.

The relevant comparative question is therefore not which vocabulary or architecture is more fundamental, but what explanatory work each performs for a specified biological target. Depending upon the target, informational and APS explanations may overlap, complement one another, remain independent, enter into tension, prove redundant, or provide differently distributed explanatory gain.

## The Explanatory Contribution of Informational Approaches

Informational approaches became influential because they successfully explain many important biological phenomena.

They illuminate signal transduction, neural communication, sensory coordination, adaptive responsiveness, distributed regulation, behavioural flexibility, learning, prediction, and communication. Informational frameworks have consequently transformed molecular biology, neuroscience, systems biology, cybernetics, cognitive science, and computational modelling.

APS recognises these as substantive explanatory achievements rather than merely informational descriptions of processes whose real explanation must be supplied elsewhere.

Informational approaches may provide sufficient or preferable explanations for particular biological targets, including aspects of signalling, regulation, coordination, communication, learning, and adaptive responsiveness. Such explanations do not require reconstruction through APS merely because APS organises its own account around living organisation.

Where informational approaches and APS address different explanatory questions, no direct competition need follow. Where they address the same target, their relative explanatory contribution requires target-matched comparison.

## Information and Living Organisation

Informational processes in biology occur within materially organised systems possessing structures, boundaries, regulatory relations, and capacities for differential responsiveness. Informational approaches can investigate these relations in their own terms and may provide substantive explanations of signalling, regulation, coordination, communication, development, cognition, and other biological phenomena.

APS approaches living organisation through a different explanatory architecture. It defines life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and treats organised persistence as the problem of how that organisation maintains and re-establishes continuity through change. Within this account, informational activity may participate in the organisation through which living systems regulate their conditions and respond to consequential differences.

APS may therefore ask whether particular forms of biological information depend upon maintained viability, biological evaluation, significance, semiosis, or other organisational relations. These are legitimate questions about biological dependency, but their answers cannot be inferred simply from the position assigned to information within APS architecture.

Where APS proposes that a particular informational relation depends upon specified organisational conditions, that relation should be treated as a Dependency Hypothesis requiring biological and comparative assessment. Informational approaches may themselves explain boundaries, regulation, signalling, persistence, and organism–environment relations through different conceptual and empirical resources.

The comparative question is therefore not whether information must be placed inside APS, but what each explanatory architecture contributes when the biological target is genuinely shared.

## Introduction

> **Where this article fits:** This is a Life-demarcation clarification. It asks whether intelligence should be identified with life itself. It does not attempt to provide a complete theory of intelligence, cognition, consciousness, or artificial intelligence, and it does not infer APS explanatory superiority from the distinction.

Intelligence has become an increasingly important explanatory concept across biology, cognitive science, artificial intelligence, and philosophy. Systems may be described as intelligent when they learn, solve problems, adapt flexibly, integrate information, anticipate possibilities, or coordinate behaviour across changing conditions. Living systems can exhibit many such capacities, and advances in artificial intelligence have made the relationship between intelligence and biological organisation increasingly difficult to ignore. Yet the presence of intelligence, or of behaviour reasonably described as intelligence-like, should not be confused with a definition of life.

APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. This definition does not require strategic reasoning, abstraction, symbolic representation, planning, or the other capacities commonly associated with intelligence. Conversely, systems may exhibit sophisticated problem-solving or adaptive performance without thereby satisfying the APS definition of life. The central claim of this article is therefore one of non-identity: **life is not defined by intelligence, and intelligence-like capacity alone does not establish life**.

This distinction is deliberately limited. It does not show that theories of intelligence are explanatorily inadequate, nor does it establish that APS offers a better account of the phenomena those theories seek to explain. Rather, it separates two questions that are easily conflated: what makes a system alive, and what makes a system intelligent. Once those questions are distinguished, the relationships among life, biological agency, cognition, intelligence, and artificial systems can be examined without assuming that one of these concepts defines the others.

## Intelligence Within the APS Architecture

APS distinguishes life, biological agency, Biological Evaluation, biological significance, Integration, cognition, Mind, intelligence, Selfhood, Reflective Agency, and Meaning rather than treating them as interchangeable descriptions of increasing biological complexity. For the purposes of the present argument, only a small part of this architecture is required.

Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation, while biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity. Biological Evaluation is the process through which agency generates significance, and biological significance is a relationally real product of biological evaluation. Integration concerns the organisation of what matters into functional unity, while cognition is the organisation of biological significance across time. Intelligence can then be used for a further family of capacities that may include flexible problem-solving, abstraction, planning, learning, strategic coordination, and other forms of context-sensitive cognitive performance.

These distinctions establish an explanatory architecture rather than a hierarchy of increasingly important biological properties. Nor should their ordering automatically be read as a universal causal, temporal, or evolutionary sequence. APS uses them to distinguish explanatory questions that might otherwise be collapsed. In particular, the present article does not require the stronger proposition that every form of intelligence must arise through precisely the same sequence of organisational transitions.

The Life-demarcation point is narrower. Living organisation need not display intelligence in any strong sense, while intelligence-like performance need not establish viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Life and intelligence may therefore be related in biological systems without being identical explanatory categories.

## Why Intelligence Became Explanatorily Central

The association between life and intelligence is understandable because living systems display many capacities that overlap with contemporary conceptions of intelligent behaviour. They respond adaptively to changing conditions, coordinate activity flexibly, regulate their interactions with their surroundings, and in some cases learn from prior interactions or alter behaviour in ways sensitive to future possibilities. At greater degrees of cognitive elaboration, organisms may plan, communicate, solve unfamiliar problems, or coordinate behaviour socially. It is therefore unsurprising that intelligence has often seemed to capture something important about the distinctive capacities of living systems.

The growth of artificial intelligence has made this association more complicated. Artificial systems can now solve problems, optimise performance, recognise patterns, generate complex outputs, and adapt their responses to changing inputs without resembling conventional organisms in their material organisation. Behaviour that once seemed to provide strong evidence of intelligence can therefore be produced by systems whose biological status is not thereby established.

APS recognises the explanatory importance of these developments but asks a different question: **what distinguishes the organisation of a living system from capacities that may be exhibited by living or non-living systems?** This directs attention away from behavioural sophistication alone and toward the organisation in which that behaviour is realised. Optimisation, prediction, learning, and problem-solving may all be explanatorily important, but none by itself determines whether the system exhibiting those capacities is alive.

## Biological Intelligence Occurs Within Living Organisation

Biological intelligence, where it occurs, is realised within living systems. The intelligent capacities of an animal, for example, are materially realised through cells, tissues, nervous systems, bodies, developmental histories, and organism–environment relations that depend upon the continuing organisation of the organism. In such cases, intelligence does not occur independently of biological organisation but through material processes maintained within it.

This claim should not, however, be universalised into the proposition that intelligence as such presupposes life. Once intelligence is characterised in functional terms such as problem-solving, learning, prediction, planning, or adaptive coordination, comparable capacities may be realised in artificial and computational systems that are not biologically alive. The appropriate distinction is therefore between **biological intelligence**, which is realised within living organisation, and **intelligence or intelligence-like capacity considered more generally**, which does not by itself establish biological status.

This distinction also prevents a material-dependence claim from becoming an assertion of explanatory superiority. The fact that biological intelligence is realised within living organisation does not show that every theory of intelligence must begin with the APS definition of life, or that biological organisation provides the best explanation of every phenomenon associated with intelligence. It establishes only that intelligence and life should not be treated as identical categories.

## Life Does Not Require Intelligence

The APS definition of life does not require intelligence. A living system must maintain and re-establish the conditions of its own persistence through viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation, but nothing in that definition requires strategic reasoning, abstraction, symbolic planning, explicit representation, or flexible problem-solving of the kinds ordinarily associated with intelligence.

This point is especially important because biological organisation extends far beyond organisms conventionally described as intelligent. Living systems can regulate metabolism, repair damage, maintain boundaries, respond to changing conditions, and coordinate viability-relevant activity without possessing the elaborated cognitive capacities ordinarily invoked when intelligence is discussed. Intelligence is therefore not necessary as a defining criterion of life.

This conclusion should not be confused with the separate and contested question of how broadly cognition should be attributed across living systems. APS distinguishes biological agency from cognition and cognition from intelligence. The claim that life does not require intelligence consequently does not decide whether particular organisms, cells, or other biological systems should be regarded as cognitive. It establishes only that a system may satisfy the APS definition of life without satisfying a demanding criterion of intelligence.
