## Introduction

How do living systems remain alive despite continually changing conditions?

Organisms persist while undergoing material turnover, energetic exchange, developmental transformation, environmental interaction, perturbation, injury, repair, and decay. Their material constituents and relations change continually, yet sufficient organisational continuity is maintained for them to continue functioning as living systems.

This continuity cannot be explained by static structure alone. Living systems continually sustain, regulate, repair, reorganise, and re-establish conditions required for their continued functioning. Nor are these conditions maintained in isolation. Organism–world relations provide resources, constraints, perturbations, and opportunities that materially alter what continued functioning requires and what forms of activity remain possible.

APS uses **viability** to identify the system-relative conditions under which continued functioning can be maintained and re-established.

Viability is therefore not simply the fact that a system remains alive. It concerns the conditions relative to which ongoing biological activity can support, undermine, or restore continued functioning. A living system may remain extant temporarily while those conditions deteriorate, just as maintaining viability may require substantial reorganisation rather than preservation of a fixed state.

Within APS, viability must be understood as an aspect of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed living organisation. It is analytically distinguishable from biological agency and organised persistence, but it is not an intermediate component linking them. Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity, while organised persistence concerns the continuity of living organisation through change. Viability identifies the conditions relative to which that activity and continuity acquire their persistence-related significance.

This relationship gives viability an important organising role within APS. It helps clarify how function, normativity, development, resilience, organism–world relations, and cognition can be investigated in relation to conditions that support or undermine continued biological functioning without implying that viability by itself explains these phenomena or supersedes their established biological explanations.

## Viability Beyond Survival

Viability is sometimes interpreted narrowly as survival, but the two should be distinguished. Survival describes an outcome—the continued existence of a system—whereas viability concerns the organisational conditions under which continued functioning remains possible.

The distinction matters because continued presence does not guarantee continued viability. A system can remain extant temporarily while progressively losing regulatory, physiological, developmental, or relational capacities required for its continued functioning. Conversely, maintaining viability can require instability, repair, adaptation, reorganisation, or transformation when existing organisation is no longer adequate under changed conditions.

Viability therefore concerns more than occupancy of a momentary state. It directs attention to whether the organisation required for continued functioning is being maintained or can be re-established through ongoing activity.

[[box:viability-is-not-mere-survival]]

This temporal dimension is particularly important because living systems are dynamically maintained organisations. Their continued functioning depends not upon remaining materially unchanged but upon preserving and reconstructing relevant organisational relations despite continual turnover and perturbation.

The distinction between survival and viability also prevents a misleading contrast between living and non-living persistence. A non-living structure may endure for long periods without participating in the maintenance of conditions required for its own continued functioning. Living systems, by contrast, are organised through activity that is viability-oriented: what occurs within and through the system can make a difference to whether viable conditions are maintained, undermined, or restored.

## Viability and Organised Persistence

Living systems maintain viability through ongoing regulation, repair, reorganisation, material exchange, and interaction with their environments. Depending upon the system and explanatory target, this can involve metabolic organisation, physiological regulation, repair of damage, responses to perturbation, developmental reorganisation, reconstruction of degraded organisation, and changes in organism–world relations.

These activities should not be understood as steps in a sequence leading from Agency through Viability to Organised Persistence. They are different aspects of the ongoing organisation through which living systems continue functioning.

Biological agency identifies the viability-oriented organisational activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained. Viability identifies the system-relative conditions in relation to which that activity supports or undermines continued functioning. Organised persistence identifies the explanatory problem of how sufficient continuity of living organisation is maintained and re-established despite continual change.

The relationship is therefore analytic rather than sequential. Viability does not convert agency into persistence, and organised persistence is not a later state produced after viability has first been secured. Living organisation persists through activity whose consequences are significant relative to viability conditions.

This distinction also clarifies why viability is central without functioning as a bridge or independent layer within APS. Its importance lies in making explicit the persistence-relative asymmetry between conditions compatible with continued functioning and conditions that undermine it.

Within APS, organised persistence consequently asks how one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation maintains and re-establishes continuity through change.

## Viability and Temporal Organisation

Viability is inherently temporal because the conditions required for continued functioning cannot be understood at an isolated instant. Living systems undergo continual material replacement, physiological adjustment, developmental transformation, environmental interaction, and internal reorganisation. What matters is not material identity across time but the maintenance and re-establishment of organisation sufficient for continued functioning.

This processual character connects viability with **Process**, the APS analytic perspective concerned with how continuity is maintained despite change. Process does not constitute an additional component of living organisation. It directs explanatory attention toward temporal organisation: how activities and relations unfolding at different rates contribute to, undermine, or restore viable conditions.

Living systems must often coordinate processes with different temporal characteristics, respond to changing internal and external conditions, preserve continuity despite material turnover, reorganise under perturbation, and integrate developmental transformation with ongoing regulation. These requirements make temporal organisation central to understanding how viable functioning continues through change.

Viability should therefore not be conceived as a static state that a living system simply possesses. Viable conditions are continually affected by biological activity and organism–world relations, and they may be maintained, degraded, or re-established over time.

Temporal organisation thus helps explain why persistence does not require invariance. Living systems can remain continuous precisely because their organisation is capable of changing in ways that maintain or re-establish viability-relevant conditions.

## Viability and Constraint Closure

Viability and constraint closure are analytically distinguishable aspects of the APS account of living organisation, but they should not be represented as independently constituted components that are subsequently combined.

Constraint closure concerns materially realised relations among constraints through which ongoing biological activity is selectively channelled and mutually sustained. Viability orientation concerns the system-relative asymmetry between conditions compatible with continued functioning and conditions that undermine it.

Within the canonical APS formulation—

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

—neither term is a temporal or explanatory precursor of the other. Constraint closure does not first generate a neutral organisation that viability subsequently renders biological, and viability is not an additional ingredient added to otherwise independently constituted closure.

This distinction matters because closure considered abstractly does not by itself establish Life, agency, normativity, or organised persistence. At the same time, viability orientation should not be treated as an independent discriminator whose presence alone settles biological status. The APS claim concerns one materially realised organisation whose constraints and activities are organised relative to conditions of continued functioning.

Under perturbation or changing circumstances, those conditions may be maintained, undermined, or re-established through changes in organisation. Viability therefore directs attention to the significance of organisational change relative to continued functioning without extending “beyond” closure as though the two belonged to successive explanatory layers.

## Viability and Normativity

Viability is central to the APS account of biological normativity because living systems exist under conditions in which different states, activities, and relations have unequal consequences for continued functioning.

Some changes support or restore viable conditions; others undermine them. This is a **viability-relative asymmetry**. It does not require conscious judgement, intention, moral evaluation, or an external observer assigning value to biological states. The asymmetry arises from the organisation of the living system itself and the consequences different conditions have for its continued functioning.

Within APS, this provides the organisational basis for biological normativity. A regulatory response can succeed or fail, a process can contribute to or undermine function, and a perturbation can be tolerated or damaging because these differences bear upon viability-relevant conditions.

Viability therefore also bears upon biological evaluation and significance. Biological activity acquires significance insofar as differences in conditions or relations matter to the organisation's continued functioning. APS describes Biological Evaluation as the process through which agency generates significance, while viability provides the persistence-relative conditions in relation to which those differences matter.

Function is related but should not be collapsed into viability. Within APS, a biological function is a present-tense viability-relative organisational contribution. A structure, process, or activity is functional insofar as its contribution bears upon maintaining or re-establishing viable organisation in the relevant context.

These relationships do not form a linear sequence in which Agency produces Viability, which produces Function, which then produces Normativity. Agency, viability, function, normativity, evaluation, and significance are analytically distinguishable aspects of living organisation whose precise explanatory relationships depend upon the target under investigation.

## Viability and Agency

Agency and viability are closely related within APS, but their relationship is not reciprocal interaction between two independently constituted entities.

**Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity.**

Agency therefore identifies what living systems do as living organisations: they regulate, modulate constraints, repair, reorganise, coordinate, and alter organism–world relations in ways whose consequences bear upon continued functioning. Such activity need not involve cognition, deliberation, conscious intention, or behavioural choice.

Viability identifies the system-relative conditions that give this activity its persistence-related orientation. Activity can support, undermine, or restore those conditions, and this asymmetry makes it possible to distinguish successful regulation from failure without attributing mental evaluation to the system.

It is therefore misleading to say either that Agency first maintains an independently existing Viability or that Viability subsequently gives Agency its orientation. Within APS, viability orientation is already intrinsic to the definition of biological agency. The distinction between the two is analytic: Agency concerns the activity of living organisation, while viability concerns the conditions relative to which that activity is significant for continued functioning.

This relationship also clarifies why agency should not be reduced to overt behaviour. Regulation, constraint modulation, repair, physiological adjustment, developmental reorganisation, and changes in organism–environment relations can all instantiate biological agency when they constitute viability-oriented organisational activity.

Agency and viability consequently illuminate different explanatory questions concerning the same living organisation rather than forming successive links in a causal chain.

## Viability Across Biological Domains

Viability-relevant conditions can be investigated across many biological domains, but this does not mean that viability constitutes the same explanatory object in every domain or that all biological organisation should itself be described as viable.

In physiology, viability concerns conditions under which metabolic, regulatory, and other organismal activities can continue functioning. Physiological processes can maintain or restore these conditions, while physiological failure can progressively undermine them.

Development presents a different problem. Organisms undergo substantial transformation while remaining living systems. Viability therefore bears upon how changing developmental organisation remains compatible with continued functioning throughout those transformations.

Evolution concerns historical transformation across generations. APS does not treat viability as an alternative to evolutionary explanation. Evolutionary theory explains changes in populations and lineages through established evolutionary processes, while viability can be used to ask how the continued functioning of organisms bears upon the historical processes in which they participate.

Ecology makes organism–world dependence especially explicit. Living systems acquire resources, encounter perturbations, modify surroundings, and participate in relations that alter viability-relevant conditions. These environmental relations are materially constitutive of the circumstances under which organismal viability is maintained or undermined, but this does not require attributing biological agency or organismal viability indiscriminately to ecological systems themselves.

Cognition requires a further distinction. APS does not identify viability-oriented regulation with cognition. Cognition involves a more specific organisation of biological significance across time, while viability provides part of the biological context within which significance arises. Life does not require cognition; cognition presupposes Life.

These examples illustrate why **Scale** should not be understood as a hierarchy of biological levels. Viability-relevant relations may extend across different spatial and temporal extents, and explanations should track where the materially relevant constraints, activities, dependencies, and environmental conditions are organised for the target under investigation.

Scale therefore asks where persistence is organised rather than locating viability at a single privileged level or distributing a single form of viability uniformly across cells, organisms, ecosystems, and lineages.

## Viability and Resilience

Viability does not require perfect stability. Living systems continually encounter perturbation, resource variation, injury, developmental change, and degradation. If viable organisation depended upon preservation of a fixed state, persistence under ordinary biological conditions would be impossible.

Resilience is relevant because living systems can sometimes absorb disturbance, reorganise activity, repair damage, and re-establish conditions compatible with continued functioning. In this sense, resilience can contribute to viability without being identical to it.

This distinction matters because resilience should not be equated with resistance to change. Physiological flexibility, developmental plasticity, behavioural adjustment, and other forms of reorganisation can preserve continuity precisely by altering existing organisation.

Viability therefore concerns neither rigidity nor return to an invariant state. What matters is whether relevant organisation can continue or be re-established under changed conditions.

This reinforces the processual character of living organisation. Persistence is maintained through transformation, and resilience identifies one family of capacities through which disruption can be accommodated without loss of the organisation required for continued functioning.

## Viability in APS

Within APS, viability belongs to the canonical account of Life rather than occupying an intermediate position in a chain linking Agency to organised persistence.

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

From this starting point, different APS concepts identify different explanatory questions concerning the same living organisation.

**Agency** concerns what living systems do: viability-oriented organisational activity.

**Viability** concerns the system-relative conditions under which continued functioning can be maintained and re-established.

**Organised persistence** concerns the maintenance and re-establishment of living organisation through change.

**Process** asks how continuity is maintained despite change.

**Scale** asks where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

These formulations do not divide living systems into components or establish a hierarchy of explanatory levels. Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Viability and organised persistence identify related aspects and explanatory targets within that organisation.

This architecture allows APS to compare biological explanations without assuming that every phenomenon is explained by viability or that all biological concepts reduce to a single principle. Development, physiology, ecology, evolution, cognition, function, normativity, adaptation, and resilience retain their distinctive explananda and established explanatory resources.

The methodological question is instead whether making viability-relevant organisation explicit clarifies a particular explanandum, discriminates among alternatives, constrains inference, or otherwise adds explanatory work relative to the strongest relevant comparator. Such gain must be demonstrated rather than inferred from the integrative reach of the vocabulary itself.

Viability is therefore important within APS because it identifies a common persistence-relative concern without functioning as a universal explanatory answer.

## Why Viability Matters

Clarifying viability helps APS distinguish several questions that can otherwise become conflated.

First, it clarifies the difference between continued existence and continued functioning. A system can remain present while losing the organisation required for its future continuation, making survival alone an inadequate representation of viability.

Second, viability makes explicit the asymmetry underlying the APS account of biological normativity. Conditions and activities can support, undermine, or restore continued functioning, providing a naturalised basis for success and failure without requiring conscious evaluation.

Third, viability helps relate biological agency to persistence without inserting a causal bridge between them. Agency is already viability-oriented organisational activity, while organised persistence concerns how the living organisation enacted through such activity maintains continuity through change.

Fourth, viability directs attention to organism–world relations. The conditions relevant to continued functioning are not wholly internal to an organism; resources, constraints, perturbations, and opportunities can be materially organised through relations extending beyond its boundary.

Finally, viability provides a useful comparative question across biological domains: what conditions are relevant to continued functioning in this system, over this temporal extent, and for this explanandum?

These contributions make viability an important organising concept within APS. They do not establish that viability is a uniquely APS discovery or that invoking viability automatically improves an explanation. Organisational, autonomy, physiological, ecological, evolutionary, and other biological approaches already provide substantial explanatory resources concerning self-maintenance, regulation, persistence, and environmental dependence.

The scientific value of the APS treatment therefore depends upon what additional explanatory work it performs in particular comparisons.

## Conclusion

Living systems persist under conditions of continual material, developmental, physiological, and environmental change. Their continuity depends not upon remaining static but upon maintaining and re-establishing organisation compatible with continued functioning.

APS uses **viability** to identify the system-relative conditions relevant to that continued functioning.

Viability is not equivalent to survival, equilibrium, stability, fitness, or simple persistence. Nor is it an independent component inserted between biological agency and organised persistence. It belongs within the canonical APS account of Life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

This perspective clarifies several relationships within the framework. Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity. Organised persistence concerns the continuity of living organisation through change. Process asks how that continuity is maintained, while Scale asks where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. Function, normativity, resilience, evaluation, and cognition raise further explanatory questions whose relations to viability must be specified rather than assumed.

Viability consequently occupies an important integrative position within APS without serving as a universal explanatory bridge. Its principal role is to make explicit the system-relative conditions in relation to which biological activity can support, undermine, or restore continued functioning.

That role also places viability under the same comparative discipline as other APS concepts. Its scientific contribution must be assessed relative to particular explanatory targets, relevant evidence, and the strongest established comparator.

Viability therefore helps APS formulate a central biological question with greater precision: **under changing conditions, what organisation and relations must be maintained or re-established for this living system to continue functioning as the organisation it is?**

# Persistence — Organised Continuity Through Time

## Introduction

Living systems are continuous without remaining unchanged. Their molecular constituents turn over, physiological states fluctuate, cells divide and die, tissues remodel, development transforms structure and function, and relations with surrounding environments continually alter. Nevertheless, organisms can maintain a recognisable continuity through these transformations.

This continuity presents an important biological problem. Material permanence cannot provide a general explanation because living systems continually exchange matter with their surroundings. Structural immobility cannot provide the answer because development, repair, adaptation, and ageing involve substantial organisational change. Persistence in living systems therefore cannot be equated with remaining materially or structurally the same.

APS approaches this problem through **organised persistence**: the continuity of living organisation through change. Persistence in this sense is neither static endurance nor mere duration. It concerns how living organisation maintains and, when disrupted, re-establishes conditions required for continued functioning while the system itself undergoes continual transformation.

This concept must be distinguished from the APS definition of life. **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.** Organised persistence is not an alternative definition. It identifies a further explanatory problem: given such living organisation, how is its continuity maintained, re-established, or transformed through time?

Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections through which APS investigates that question. Agency concerns what living systems do. Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change. Scale concerns where persistence and its relevant dependencies are organised across spatial and temporal extents. These are analytic projections of one living organisation rather than independent components, causes, domains, or hierarchical levels.

Organised persistence is therefore a principal explanatory target within APS. It does not follow that every biological explanation must ultimately become an explanation of persistence. The relevance of persistence depends upon the explanandum.

## Where This Article Fits

This Anchor article provides the principal APS conceptual account of **persistence as organised continuity through time**. Its task is to clarify what persistence means in living systems, why it cannot be reduced to material permanence or static endurance, and how continuity can be maintained through continual transformation.

It should be distinguished from two closely related articles in the APS explanation architecture. *Biological Explanation — What Needs to Be Explained* establishes organised persistence as a principal APS explanandum while explicitly rejecting the claim that it is the universal target of biological explanation. *Biological Explanation and Organised Persistence* takes the further methodological step of asking how that explanandum should be investigated, what organisational dependencies would count as explanatory, and when an APS analysis provides explanatory gain relative to an adequate comparator.

The relationship is therefore controlled and cumulative. The present article clarifies **the concept of organised persistence**; *Biological Explanation — What Needs to Be Explained* establishes **its status as an APS explanatory target**; and *Biological Explanation and Organised Persistence* develops **the methodology through which that target can be investigated**.

This article should consequently be read as a conceptual foundation for organised-persistence inquiry, not as a claim that persistence provides the hidden explanandum of every biological science.

## Why Persistence Is a Biological Problem

Persistence can initially appear straightforward: an organism exists at one time and continues to exist at another. Yet the apparent simplicity disappears once the material and organisational dynamics of living systems are considered.

Organisms continually exchange matter and energy with their surroundings. Molecular components are synthesised and degraded, physiological states change, cells and tissues are reorganised, and behaviour alters in relation to changing circumstances. Development transforms the organism throughout its life history, while damage, disease, environmental perturbation, and ageing can disrupt previously established organisation.

If biological continuity required material sameness, these transformations would continually destroy it. If it required structural immobility, development and repair would themselves become incompatible with persistence. Living continuity must therefore be compatible with substantial change.

APS treats this as an organisational problem. The explanatory question is not how an organism avoids transformation, but how living organisation remains sufficiently continuous while transformation occurs. In some circumstances this involves maintaining existing organisation; in others it requires compensation, repair, reorganisation, or the establishment of new viable relations.

Persistence is therefore not resistance to change. It is continuity organised through change.

This formulation also distinguishes biological persistence from mere duration. Many non-living structures can remain present for long periods, but their continued existence need not depend upon activity that maintains and re-establishes the conditions of their own functioning. APS is concerned with the distinctive case in which continuity depends upon living organisation itself.

## Organised Persistence

APS uses **organised persistence** to identify the continuity of living organisation through transformation. The concept directs attention away from the preservation of fixed material states and toward the activities, constraints, relations, and processes through which viable organisation continues.

Living systems regulate physiological conditions, coordinate development, repair damage, modify behaviour, reorganise activity following perturbation, and alter their relations with surrounding environments. Depending upon the explanandum, these activities may contribute to maintaining or re-establishing the organisation required for continued functioning.

Organised persistence does not imply that every process occurring within an organism contributes positively to continuity. Biological activity can be ineffective, damaging, pathological, or incompatible with continued viability. Nor does persistence imply indefinite survival. Every organism eventually ceases to persist.

The explanatory issue is instead how particular forms of living organisation maintain continuity for some period despite conditions under which that continuity could fail.

This makes organised persistence an explanatory problem rather than a property that can simply be attributed to a system. To explain persistence in a particular case requires identification of the dependencies through which continuity is maintained or restored. Invoking organisation, viability, or persistence without specifying those dependencies does not itself provide the explanation.

Organised persistence therefore supplies APS with a target for inquiry. What maintains continuity? What can disrupt it? Which organisational relations are required? What changes can be accommodated? Which cannot? How is viable organisation restored after perturbation? These questions convert the general concept into case-specific explanatory problems.

## Persistence, Life, and Viability

The relation among life, viability, and persistence requires particular care because the concepts are closely connected without being interchangeable.

APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. This organisation consists in the ongoing modulation of constraints through which a system actively maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence.

Viability concerns the conditions within which continued functioning remains possible. These conditions need not correspond to a single fixed state. Living systems can remain viable across ranges of physiological, developmental, behavioural, and environmental conditions, and those ranges can themselves change.

Persistence concerns continuity through time. The connection with viability is therefore substantive: a living system can persist only while its organisation remains compatible with continued functioning. Yet persistence should not be defined simply as successful viability, nor viability as persistence viewed from another perspective.

The distinction becomes clearer when disruption is considered. A system may move toward conditions that threaten viability while still remaining temporarily continuous. Conversely, reorganisation may alter many features of the system while restoring conditions required for continued functioning. The explanatory task is to identify how these changes affect the organisation through which persistence is maintained.

Viability also provides the basis for biological normativity within APS. Conditions are not biologically equivalent because they differ in how they bear upon continued functioning. This **viability-relative asymmetry** makes success, failure, function, malfunction, regulation, and repair biologically intelligible without requiring mental evaluation or external purpose.

Persistence does not generate that normativity simply because continuity can succeed or fail. Rather, viability-oriented organisation establishes the asymmetries through which particular changes become relevant to persistence.

## Persistence and Biological Agency

Biological persistence is active because living continuity depends upon what living systems do.

APS defines **biological agency as viability-oriented organisational activity**. Agency is the present-tense activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained. Regulation, repair, behavioural adjustment, developmental coordination, and other forms of activity can contribute to persistence where they materially affect the conditions required for continued functioning.

Agency should not, however, be represented as a separate mechanism added to persistence. Nor should persistence be treated as an external goal toward which an independent agent acts. Agency and persistence concern different analytic aspects of the same living organisation.

The relation becomes particularly clear under perturbation. Damage, physiological disturbance, environmental change, or developmental disruption can alter conditions relevant to viability. Living systems may respond by modifying activity, reorganising constraints, repairing damaged structures, or establishing different viable relations. Where such changes restore conditions required for continued functioning, agency contributes materially to organised persistence.

The relevant contribution must nevertheless be demonstrated rather than inferred from the fact that the activity occurs in a living system. Some responses fail, some are neutral with respect to the persistence being investigated, and some may worsen the disruption. APS therefore asks what difference the activity makes to the viability-relevant organisation at issue.

Agency identifies the activity through which persistence can be enacted and re-established; it does not guarantee successful persistence.

## Persistence and Biological Identity

The continuity of living systems also bears upon questions of biological identity. An organism can undergo extensive material turnover and developmental transformation while remaining recognisably the same biological individual. This makes material sameness an inadequate general criterion of organismal identity.

APS treats organisational continuity as an important basis for investigating this problem. An embryo and an adult can differ profoundly in morphology, physiology, behaviour, and ecological relations while remaining connected through one continuing developmental history. Repair and regeneration can likewise replace or reorganise material structures without necessarily creating a new individual.

Organised persistence helps explain how such continuity is possible. What persists is not an invariant collection of matter but a living organisation capable of remaining continuous through transformation.

This does not mean that organised persistence by itself resolves every problem of biological individuality or identity. Different biological cases can require different criteria, and questions concerning organisms, colonies, symbioses, developmental systems, or other biological individuals cannot be settled merely by invoking continuity.

The more limited APS claim is that material turnover does not preclude biological identity and that continuity of living organisation provides an important explanatory basis for investigating identity through change.

## Process and Persistence

Persistence is intrinsically temporal. It cannot be identified from an isolated state because persistence concerns relations among states and activities through time.

Within APS, **Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change**. This makes process central to the analysis of organised persistence without turning every biological process into a persistence mechanism.

Metabolism, physiological regulation, development, repair, behavioural activity, and other processes can contribute to persistence where they maintain or re-establish relevant organisational conditions. Their explanatory significance depends upon what difference they make to the continuity being investigated.

Development provides a particularly clear illustration. Organisms persist through developmental change not because developmental organisation remains constant, but because transformation occurs within a continuing biological history. Structures emerge, functions alter, capacities develop, and organism–environment relations change. Continuity is therefore maintained through organised transformation rather than through preservation of a fixed state.

Repair illustrates a different temporal relation. Here previously established organisation has been disrupted and activity contributes to restoring or replacing conditions required for continued functioning. Persistence can therefore involve both maintenance and re-establishment.

Process allows these different temporal patterns to be analysed without treating persistence as stasis. The relevant question is how continuity is organised through the transformations characteristic of the biological system under investigation.

## Scale and the Organisation of Persistence

Persistence also raises questions about where relevant dependencies are organised.

Within APS, **Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents**. Scale does not refer to a hierarchy of biological levels, nor does APS assume that persistence is produced by interactions among independently explanatory scales.

The dependencies relevant to organismal persistence can be materially implemented across different spatial extents. Physiological regulation may involve relations among molecular processes, cells, tissues, organs, behaviour, and environmental conditions. The temporal extent of those dependencies may range from rapid regulatory activity to development, repair, ageing, or longer histories of organism–environment interaction.

Which extents matter cannot be determined from APS architecture alone. They depend upon the explanandum and upon evidence showing that particular relations make a difference to the persistence being explained.

Nor does wider extent automatically confer explanatory priority. A dependency extending beyond the immediate site of a phenomenon matters only where materially implemented constraints or interactions alter conditions relevant to that phenomenon. APS therefore does not replace local causation with explanation from a supposedly higher level.

Scale-sensitive analysis asks where the relevant organisation is realised, over what temporal extent it operates, and how materially implemented relations across those extents contribute to the continuity under investigation.

## Persistence Across Biological Explananda

Questions of persistence arise in many areas of biology, but they do not necessarily concern the same persisting subject or require the same explanation. APS therefore distinguishes the recurrence of persistence questions from the stronger claim that all biological domains investigate one underlying persistence process.

In physiology, persistence questions can concern how metabolic, regulatory, and functional organisation remains within or returns to conditions compatible with continued functioning. Compensation, homeostatic regulation, repair, and physiological reorganisation can become relevant where they materially contribute to such continuity.

Development presents a different problem because continuity occurs through substantial transformation. The organism does not merely preserve an existing organisation; its organisation changes through growth, differentiation, maturation, and ageing. Development therefore provides a particularly important case for examining continuity without material or structural sameness.

Ecological explanation introduces further questions. Organismal persistence can depend upon resources, interactions, environmental conditions, and other relations extending beyond the organism's immediate boundaries. These dependencies can be essential to explaining persistence without requiring APS to treat an ecosystem itself as an agent or as an organism-like persisting subject.

Evolution must be distinguished still more carefully. Evolutionary explanation concerns historical transformations in populations and lineages and cannot simply be identified with organismal self-maintenance extended across generations. Organised persistence can nevertheless generate specific evolutionary questions—for example, concerning how forms of living organisation remain viable, reproduce, vary, and become historically transformed. Whether persistence adds explanatory value to a particular evolutionary explanandum must be established rather than assumed.

Cognition likewise should not be reduced to persistence. Biological agency becomes cognitive when integrated biological significance modulates activity across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities in ways not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation. Such cognition remains realised within living organisation and can contribute to viability, but its distinctive explanatory target concerns the organisation of biological significance across time rather than persistence alone.

Organised persistence can therefore connect questions across biological inquiry when continuity is genuinely at issue. It does not convert physiology, development, ecology, evolution, and cognition into different expressions of one universal explanatory problem.

## Persistence, Resilience, and Perturbation

Persistence does not require the absence of disruption. Living systems encounter injury, physiological disturbance, developmental perturbation, environmental fluctuation, disease, and other conditions capable of undermining viability. Continuity can therefore depend upon capacities for compensation, repair, recovery, and reorganisation.

Resilience is relevant where a system can maintain or re-establish viable organisation despite such disruption. This need not mean returning to precisely the previous state. A resilient system may reorganise in ways that preserve continued functioning under altered conditions.

This distinguishes resilience from simple resistance. Resistance limits the effect of perturbation; resilience can also involve changes through which viable organisation is restored or reorganised after disturbance.

Perturbation is especially informative because disrupting a candidate dependency can reveal whether it contributes materially to persistence. If altering a regulatory relation, constraint, signalling pathway, environmental dependency, or other organisational feature systematically changes the system's capacity to maintain or restore viable organisation, the perturbation can provide evidence about the dependency under investigation.

Perturbation does not automatically reveal organisation, however. A system may change following intervention for many reasons. The explanatory claim requires a specified dependency, an appropriate contrast, and evidence connecting the perturbation to the persistence-related outcome.

[[box:perturbation-reveals-organisation]]

Perturbation therefore provides one important route for testing organised-persistence hypotheses. Repair, compensation, adaptation, and recovery can make otherwise difficult-to-observe dependencies experimentally accessible, but their interpretation remains relative to the explanandum and evidence.

## Persistence and Organisational Failure

An account of organised persistence must also accommodate failure.

Living organisation does not guarantee continued viability. Regulatory processes can become ineffective, repair can fail, developmental trajectories can become pathological, environmental conditions can exceed tolerable ranges, and accumulated disruption can undermine the organisation required for continued functioning.

Failure is not external to the explanatory problem. It helps identify the dependencies through which persistence is maintained. Contrasts between successful maintenance and breakdown, recovery and non-recovery, or viable and non-viable trajectories can reveal which organisational relations are consequential.

This is another reason APS should not define persistence simply as the inevitable result of living organisation. Persistence is contingent upon materially realised relations that can be disrupted. Its explanation therefore requires attention to both the organisation through which continuity is achieved and the conditions under which that organisation ceases to be sufficient.

Viability-relative asymmetry makes these differences biologically consequential. Some organisational changes maintain or restore conditions of continued functioning; others undermine them. The distinction provides a naturalised basis for discussing biological success and failure without treating persistence as intentional purpose.

## Why Organised Persistence Matters

Organised persistence matters within APS because continuity through change is a genuine explanatory problem presented by living organisation.

The concept draws together questions concerning maintenance, repair, development, resilience, identity, and temporal organisation without requiring them to be reduced to a single mechanism. It directs attention to the dependencies through which living systems remain continuous despite material turnover, internal transformation, and changing relations with their environments.

Its integrating value must nevertheless remain conditional. A molecular mechanism can be explained adequately without explaining organismal persistence if persistence is not part of the explanandum. An evolutionary change can be explained without treating lineage continuity as organised self-maintenance. An ecological interaction need not be reconstructed as a persistence relation merely because it occurs among living systems.

APS therefore does not place organised persistence at the centre of every biological explanation. It identifies persistence as a principal explanatory target **where the maintenance, re-establishment, or transformation of living continuity is genuinely at issue**.

This bounded formulation makes the concept more rather than less scientifically useful. It permits persistence claims to be specified, compared with established explanations, tested through material dependencies, and rejected where the APS framing adds no explanatory gain.

Organised persistence consequently functions as an important conceptual bridge between the APS account of life and its methodology of biological explanation without becoming a universal explanatory principle imposed upon biology.

## From Persistence to Biological Explanation

The conceptual account developed here establishes a phenomenon requiring explanation but does not itself determine how that explanation should proceed.

To say that an organism exhibits organised persistence is not yet to identify the dependencies through which it persists. Explanation requires specification of what is being maintained, what threatens or transforms it, which relations make a difference to continuity, how those relations are materially implemented, and what evidence supports the proposed dependency.

This distinction separates the present article from the two principal explanatory articles that follow from it.

*Biological Explanation — What Needs to Be Explained* asks why organised persistence constitutes a legitimate APS explanandum while preserving the plurality of biological explanation.

*Biological Explanation and Organised Persistence* then develops a controlled methodology for investigating that explanandum. It asks how organisational dependencies can be specified, how perturbation and contrast can provide evidence, how relevant spatial and temporal extents should be identified, and how any claim of explanatory gain should be assessed relative to an adequate comparator addressing the same target.

The conceptual movement is therefore:

**organised continuity through change → organised persistence as an explanandum → controlled investigation of persistence-related dependencies**

This is an explanatory dependency within the APS corpus, not a claim that every biological inquiry must follow the sequence.

## Conclusion

Living systems remain continuous while undergoing continual transformation. Their matter changes, their physiological states fluctuate, development reorganises them, environmental relations alter, and disruption can require repair or reorganisation. Biological persistence therefore cannot generally be understood as material permanence, structural immobility, equilibrium, or passive endurance.

APS describes this problem as **organised persistence**.

The concept must remain distinct from the definition of life. **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.** Organised persistence asks how that organisation maintains and re-establishes continuity through change.

Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections through which this problem can be investigated. Biological agency concerns the viability-oriented organisational activity through which continuity can be maintained or restored. Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite transformation. Scale concerns where persistence and its relevant dependencies are organised across spatial and temporal extents.

These analytic resources do not establish the explanation in advance. Particular persistence claims require materially implemented dependencies, appropriate contrasts, and evidence showing how the proposed organisation contributes to continuity. Failure and perturbation are therefore as informative as successful maintenance.

Organised persistence also does not subsume biological explanation generally. It is a principal APS explanatory target when continuity, maintenance, repair, resilience, identity, or transformation of living organisation forms part of the explanandum. Other biological explanations can remain adequate without being reconstructed in persistence terms.

The resulting APS claim is deliberately bounded but substantial: **living continuity through change is an organisational phenomenon requiring explanation, and organised persistence identifies that phenomenon without turning it into either a second definition of life or the universal explanandum of biology.**

>
> This article treats biological organisation as a **target of explanation rather than as a replacement for established biological explanations**. Within APS, Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of the same living organisation.

## 1. The Problem of Biological Continuity

Living systems persist despite continual change.

Their molecular constituents turn over. Cellular structures are repaired and replaced. Physiological conditions fluctuate. Development transforms form and function. Organisms alter their relations with surrounding environments. Yet through such changes, living systems can maintain continuity of organisation.

This creates a distinctive explanatory problem. Biological continuity cannot always be identified with the persistence of unchanged material structures.

Molecular, biochemical, physiological, structural, developmental, and other biological explanations identify materially specified features and processes through which living systems function. The organisational question does not compete with these explanations. It asks how the relevant activities, structures, constraints, and dependencies are related in ways that allow living organisation to remain continuous despite change.

APS refers to this continuity as **organised persistence**.

Organised persistence is the continuity of living organisation through change. It is the continuity requiring explanation, not an independent mechanism or activity that produces persistence.

## 2. Biological Organisation as a Target of Explanation

Biological organisation should not be identified merely with the arrangement of biological parts.

Living systems consist of materially realised structures and processes, but organisation concerns the relations through which those structures and processes contribute to the continued functioning of the system.

These relations include dependencies among activities, structures, constraints, and conditions. Their explanatory relevance depends on the biological target under investigation.

Appealing to organisation cannot substitute for specifying the material activities through which it is realised.

Organisation has explanatory value when it identifies such relations, not when it merely redescribes a system as an integrated whole.

[[box:organisation-is-not-structure]]

Constraint closure is an important part of the APS account of living organisation. It identifies networks in which constraints involved in maintaining biological activity depend, directly or indirectly, upon other processes and constraints within the organisation whose continuation they help sustain.

Constraint closure does not by itself exhaust the APS account of living organisation. APS also asks how the relevant organisation is enacted through viability-oriented activity, how continuity is maintained despite change, and across what spatial and temporal extents persistence is organised.

## 3. Viability, Regulation, and the Organisation of Persistence

Within APS, living organisation is analysed relative to viability.

**Viability** concerns the conditions under which living organisation can continue to function. Changes in internal or external conditions can therefore differ in their consequences for continued functioning. Some remain compatible with persistence; others impair or destroy the organisation on which continued functioning depends.

Within the APS analysis, this asymmetry provides the limited sense of biological normativity required here. Conditions matter biologically insofar as they differ in their consequences for the viability of the organisation concerned.

This does not imply conscious evaluation, intention, preference, or judgement.

Living systems can regulate, repair, reorganise, acquire resources, modify relations with their environments, and otherwise respond to changing conditions in ways relevant to continued functioning. These activities may contribute to maintaining or re-establishing the organisation required for persistence.

APS characterises the living organisation at issue as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

Organised persistence identifies the continuity of that organisation through change. It does not identify an additional mechanism or causal force.

The organisation relevant to persistence is therefore not a static arrangement. Within APS, it is understood through materially realised activities and dependencies through which conditions relevant to continued functioning can be maintained and re-established.

## 4. Agency, Process, and Scale as Analytic Projections

APS analyses living organisation through three complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections.

**Agency** concerns **what living systems do**.

Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity. Regulation, repair, behaviour, physiological adjustment, resource acquisition, and environmental modification may express biological agency without individually defining it. Within APS, agency is not an additional capacity attached to an otherwise complete living system; it identifies the activity through which viability-oriented living organisation is enacted and sustained.

Agency does not require intention, consciousness, deliberation, symbolic representation, or cognition.

**Process** concerns **how continuity is maintained despite change**.

Living organisation persists through ongoing material, physiological, developmental, and environmental transformation. Process directs attention to the dynamics through which organisational continuity is maintained or re-established rather than treating persistence as static stability.

**Scale** concerns **where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents**.

The relevant extent depends on the explanatory target. Scale does not divide biological reality into hierarchical levels, nor does it assign intrinsic explanatory priority to molecular, cellular, organismal, ecological, or longer temporal descriptions.

Where relations across different extents are explanatorily relevant, they must be materially implemented through identifiable interactions, conditions, constraints, or dependencies. Differences of scale alone do not establish causal coupling.

Agency, Process, and Scale are therefore complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of one living organisation. They are not stages, independent causes, components, hierarchical levels, or ontological domains.

## 5. Persistence Through Developmental Transformation

Development makes the problem of organised persistence especially clear.

A developing organism can undergo substantial changes in size, morphology, physiology, behaviour, and relations among tissues and organs while remaining continuous as the same developing living organisation.

Continuity therefore need not require structural invariance.

Development can involve the regulated production, removal, replacement, and reorganisation of structures. What the analysis tracks as continuing is an organised relation among activities, constraints, and conditions through which the developing system remains viable while changing.

APS can analyse this through Agency, Process, and Scale.

Agency concerns the viability-oriented activities through which development is enacted. Process concerns how continuity is maintained through developmental transformation. Scale concerns which spatial and temporal extents are relevant to the developmental explanandum.

These do not provide three separate explanations. They are complementary analytic projections of the same developing organisation.

Development does not independently establish the APS account. Established developmental explanations retain their mechanisms, evidence, and explanatory targets. Development instead provides a clear case in which the explanatory problem of continuity through transformation can be examined.

## 6. Organisation in an Environment and Across Scale

Living organisation is materially realised and environmentally dependent.

Organisms depend upon exchanges of matter and energy and upon environmental conditions compatible with continued functioning. They can also modify those conditions through their own activity.

Dependence on an environment does not erase the distinction between the organisation of the living system and the wider systems with which it interacts.

Coupling alone does not establish constraint closure, and dependence alone does not establish biological agency or normativity.

Likewise, relations extending across spatial or temporal extents should not be represented as causal traffic between abstract biological levels.

Where wider organisation alters local activity, the relation must be materially implemented through identifiable conditions, interactions, constraints, or dependencies. Local activity contributes to wider organisation through similarly material relations.

Scale therefore identifies where persistence is organised for the explanatory target at issue. It does not introduce a hierarchy in which wider scales automatically control smaller ones or possess greater explanatory importance.

## 7. Biological Organisation as a Target of Explanation

Treating biological organisation as a target does not imply that every biological question is fundamentally organisational.

Different questions require different explanatory resources.

A molecular mechanism may adequately explain one target. Physiological organisation may be relevant to another. Developmental history, ecological relations, or evolutionary history may be required for others.

Mechanistic and organisational explanations therefore need not compete. A mechanism can form part of an organisational explanation where the target requires specification of the wider dependencies under which the mechanism operates. Conversely, no additional organisational account is required merely because a mechanism can be redescribed as belonging to a larger system.

No spatial or temporal scale is automatically privileged.

The organisational perspective becomes particularly relevant where identifying relations among activities, structures, constraints, and conditions makes an explanatory difference to the target.

Whether it provides additional explanatory gain must be assessed relative to that target and to the strongest established explanation available for it.

Within APS, what is tracked as persisting is neither matter alone nor an abstract pattern detached from material realisation. It is the continuity of materially realised living organisation through change.

APS characterises Life as:

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

Organised persistence identifies the continuity of that living organisation through change.

# Constraint Closure — What It Does and What It Does Not Do

> **Where this article fits:** This article clarifies the contribution and limits of constraint closure within APS. Constraint closure identifies an indispensable organisational relation in living systems, but closure considered abstractly does not exhaust the explanation of viability-oriented organisation, biological agency, or organised persistence. For the broader APS account of constraint as continuity-producing organisation, see *Constraint — How Organisation Becomes Persistence*.

Constraint closure has become an important concept in contemporary theoretical biology.

It identifies a distinctive form of organisation in which constraints are not merely imposed independently upon processes. Constraints participate in relations of mutual dependence through which they contribute to maintaining the organisation that sustains them.

This provides an important alternative to explanations that treat biological organisation simply as the aggregate result of independently specified parts or linear causal sequences.

APS therefore treats constraint closure as indispensable to explaining living organisation.

But its explanatory contribution must be stated carefully.

Closure considered abstractly does not by itself establish:

- life
- biological agency
- normativity
- viability orientation
- or organised persistence

This does not mean that these are additional components that must be attached to an independently constituted closure structure.

APS instead understands living organisation as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

Constraint closure identifies one indispensable feature of that organisation. Its biological significance becomes fully intelligible only in relation to the ongoing activity through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

[[box:organisational-biology-and-persistence]]

## What Constraint Closure Does

Constraint closure explains an important aspect of organisational coherence.

In a constraint-closed organisation:

- constraints participate in relations of mutual dependence
- constrained processes contribute to maintaining relevant organisational conditions
- those conditions contribute to the continued operation or regeneration of the constraints
- and organisational continuity depends upon these relations being sustained through ongoing activity

Closure therefore helps explain how biological processes can form a mutually dependent organisation rather than merely a collection of causally interacting processes.

It directs explanatory attention toward:

- reciprocal organisational dependence
- maintenance of enabling conditions
- regeneration of constraints
- coordination among processes
- and the organisation through which continued activity remains possible

This contribution is substantial.

But constraint closure should not be interpreted as an underlying structural layer upon which living organisation is subsequently constructed.

Within APS, closure is an analytic characterisation of relations operating within the living organisation whose persistence is being explained.

It therefore contributes to explaining organised persistence without independently constituting organised persistence as a whole.

## Closure and Temporal Organisation

Constraint closure cannot be adequately understood as a static arrangement.

Relations of mutual dependence persist only insofar as the activities through which they are realised continue through time.

Living systems undergo:

- material turnover
- environmental variation
- internal change
- perturbation
- degradation
- repair
- and reorganisation

Their organisation therefore cannot depend upon preserving an unchanged material configuration.

Constraints must be maintained, regenerated, modified, or re-established as conditions change.

The explanatory importance of closure is consequently temporal as well as relational.

A description of constraint relations at a single moment does not by itself explain how those relations continue to be realised across changing circumstances.

APS addresses this problem through organised persistence.

Living systems persist because organisation is continually enacted across material and temporal change. Constraint closure contributes to that persistence insofar as mutually dependent constraints remain organised through the processes that maintain and re-establish the conditions for continued activity.

Closure is therefore not adequately represented as a frozen configuration.

Its biological significance lies in dynamically maintained organisation through time.

## What Constraint Closure Does Not Do

Constraint closure has explanatory limits.

Identifying reciprocal dependence among constraints does not, by itself, establish that every system exhibiting such dependence should be treated as living, agential, normative, or biologically organised.

The distinction is important because concepts such as:

- mutual dependence
- self-maintenance
- stability
- feedback
- persistence
- and organisational closure

can apply, in different senses, beyond living systems.

Closure therefore cannot serve as an unrestricted criterion from which biological status is inferred solely from the presence of reciprocal organisational dependence. Recent attempts to formalise autopoietic closure reinforce the importance of distinguishing an organisational relation from its formal representation. Ramírez-Tagle (2026), for example, proposes a categorical–thermodynamic calculus of closure while explicitly presenting it as a programmatic formalisation rather than a completed theory of living organisation.

APS does not resolve this problem by dividing reality into a generic closure structure and a second viability component that converts that structure into life.

Instead, the relevant explanatory object is the living organisation itself.

For APS, life is **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

Closure considered in abstraction captures an indispensable relation within that organisation, but does not exhaust the organisation through which biological persistence is maintained.

This distinction preserves the explanatory importance of constraint closure without asking the concept to establish more than it can support.

## Closure and Viability

Viability is not an additional ingredient attached to closure.

Nor does APS propose that a system first becomes constraint-closed and then acquires a separate viability orientation.

The distinction is analytic.

Constraint closure directs attention to relations of organisational dependence through which constraints participate in maintaining the conditions of their continued operation.

Viability directs attention to the asymmetry between conditions under which living organisation can continue and conditions under which that organisation degrades or ceases.

In living systems these are not two independently operating organisations.

They are aspects of one organisation.

Living activity occurs under conditions in which some changes are compatible with continued organisation while others threaten, impair, or terminate it. The organisation of activity relative to those conditions is what gives viability its explanatory significance.

APS therefore describes life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

This formulation does not combine two independent mechanisms.

It characterises the organisation of living systems from complementary explanatory perspectives: the mutual dependence through which organisation is maintained and the viability conditions relative to which that maintenance matters for continued persistence.

## Closure and Agency

Constraint closure and biological agency must also be distinguished without treating them as separate stages or components.

**Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity.**

Agency is therefore not something added to closure after a system has become sufficiently complex, nor does agency arise merely because closure becomes coupled to an additional regulatory mechanism.

Constraint closure characterises relations through which organisational conditions are mutually maintained.

Agency characterises what living organisation does: it modulates activity in relation to the conditions of its own continued functioning and persistence.

These descriptions concern the same living organisation under different explanatory projections.

This matters because closure alone should not be used as a synonym for agency.

A demonstration of reciprocal constraint dependence establishes an organisational relation. A claim of biological agency additionally concerns viability-oriented organisational activity.

APS therefore preserves the distinction while rejecting an architecture in which closure and agency are independent entities that must subsequently be joined.

## Closure, Perturbation, and Reconstruction

Perturbation can make otherwise unobvious organisational dependencies visible.

When conditions change, investigators can examine:

- which processes are disrupted
- which constraints cease to be maintained
- which organisational relations are restored
- how activity is reorganised
- whether relevant conditions are re-established
- and whether continued functioning is preserved

Such cases can reveal how persistence depends upon organisation rather than upon the preservation of an unchanged state.

Living systems frequently maintain continuity through material replacement, regulation, repair, compensation, or reorganisation.

These responses should not be treated as evidence that every biological perturbation produces adaptive reconstruction, nor should passive physical processes be excluded from biological explanation.

Rather, perturbation provides an explanatory probe.

It can reveal dependencies that remain difficult to identify under relatively stable conditions and can show how materially implemented constraints alter the local conditions under which continued organisation is possible.

The relevant contrast is therefore not simply between passive non-living stability and active biological reconstruction.

It is between descriptions of persistence as unchanged state and explanations of the organisation through which living systems can maintain or re-establish conditions of continued functioning despite change.

## Avoiding Overgeneralisation

Constraint closure is powerful partly because relations of mutual dependence occur across many forms of organised activity.

That generality also creates a risk.

If every instance of reciprocal dependence, self-maintenance, feedback, or organisational stability is treated as sufficient evidence of biological organisation or agency, the relevant concepts lose discriminatory value.

Chemical systems, engineered systems, ecological organisations, and social organisations may exhibit forms of:

- reciprocal dependence
- feedback
- persistence
- constraint
- or self-maintenance

without that fact alone establishing that they instantiate biological agency or living organisation.

APS therefore does not infer biological status merely from formal similarity.

The relevant question is what organisation is materially realised in the system under investigation and what explanatory claim the evidence supports.

This is especially important when concepts developed to explain organisms are extended to other domains.

Similarity in organisational description can justify comparison.

It does not by itself justify identity of biological status.

Constraint closure should therefore retain its explanatory generality without becoming an unrestricted licence for attributing life, agency, normativity, or cognition wherever mutually dependent processes can be identified.

## Constraint Closure Reframed

Constraint closure remains indispensable to biological explanation.

It identifies relations of mutual dependence through which constraints contribute to maintaining the organisation that sustains them.

It helps explain how biological activity is coordinated, how enabling conditions are regenerated, and how organisation can continue despite ongoing material change.

But closure considered abstractly is not a complete explanation of:

- life
- biological agency
- normativity
- viability orientation
- or organised persistence

These explanatory limits do not require APS to supplement closure with a collection of independently constituted biological components.

The relevant explanatory object remains one living organisation.

APS describes that organisation as viability-oriented and constraint-closed, enacted through ongoing activity and maintained across spatial and temporal extents.

Agency, Process, and Scale provide analytic projections of this organisation rather than components of reality.

From the perspective of Agency, explanation concerns what living systems do through viability-oriented organisational activity.

From the perspective of Process, explanation concerns how continuity is maintained despite change.

From the perspective of Scale, explanation concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

Constraint closure contributes to this explanatory architecture by identifying relations of mutual dependence through which the conditions of continued organisation are maintained and regenerated.

Its importance therefore lies neither in explaining life by itself nor in serving as a preliminary structure that becomes biological only when something else is added.

It is indispensable because living persistence is organised.

## Introduction

What are living systems doing when they remain alive?

Biology answers this question indirectly. Organisms regulate internal conditions, acquire resources, repair damage, coordinate development, respond to environmental change, and reproduce. These activities are investigated through physiology, ecology, development, evolution, and behaviour. Beneath this diversity, however, lies a more fundamental question: what unifies these activities as expressions of life?

This article answers this question through the concept of biological agency.

Agency is not a specialised capacity possessed only by animals, nor is it synonymous with behaviour, cognition, intelligence, or conscious intention. Biological agency is the viability-oriented activity through which living systems continually maintain and re-establish the conditions required for their own organised persistence. Organisms remain alive because they continuously participate in maintaining themselves.

Within APS, this understanding gives Agency a central analytic role. Living systems do not merely persist. They actively contribute to maintaining the organisation that makes persistence possible. A bacterium regulating its internal chemistry, a plant reorganising growth in response to changing conditions, and an animal repairing damage each exemplify biological agency because the activity of the living system contributes to sustaining its viability. Their mechanisms differ profoundly, but the relevant organisational principle remains the same.

Within APS, Agency is the analytic projection concerned with what living systems do. Biological agency is the present-tense viability-oriented organisational activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained. It is not an additional characteristic that appears once life already exists, nor an independent cause alongside Process and Scale.

Understanding agency in this way clarifies why living systems possess functions, why biological processes can succeed or fail, why normativity arises within biology, and why biological explanation differs from explanations of non-living systems. Agency provides one analytic projection through which these phenomena become conceptually connected within APS.

Whereas *Agency as the Defining Activity of Life* introduced the APS rationale for treating Agency as the defining activity of life, the present article develops the APS account of biological agency through self-maintenance, viability, organised persistence, function, normativity, and biological explanation. It also addresses a further question that becomes unavoidable once agency is recognised throughout living organisation: where is attribution of biological agency warranted?

## Where This Article Fits

This article provides the core APS account of **biological agency**. Its principal task is to explain what Agency means within APS: biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity, enacted through the continuing self-maintenance by which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

Other APS articles develop distinct questions arising from this account. *Agency as the Defining Activity of Life* addresses why Agency occupies a defining position in the APS account of living organisation. *Biological Agency Without Intentions* distinguishes biological agency from intention, deliberation, and psychological goal attribution. *Agency and Organised Persistence* develops the relationship between present-tense viability-oriented activity and the continuity that activity sustains. *Biological Agency, Evolution, and the Architecture of Biological Explanation* extends the analysis into evolutionary and explanatory questions, while *Agency in Contemporary Biology — The Emerging Agency Tradition and the APS Framework* compares the APS account with contemporary approaches to biological agency.

The present article therefore owns the foundational question of **what biological agency is and where its attribution is warranted**. It does not follow from APS that every biological component, subsystem, collective, or organisational extent constitutes an independent agent. Nor does an attribution of agency follow from assigning a system to a particular biological level. Agency must be grounded in the viability-oriented organisation instantiated by the candidate system, while Scale asks across what spatial and temporal extent the organisation relevant to that attribution and explanandum must be followed.

This distinction also separates **agency attribution** from **agency explanation**. A biological organisation may warrant analysis as an agent without Agency necessarily providing explanatory gain for every phenomenon involving that organisation. APS therefore requires both the attribution of agency and its explanatory use to be justified relative to the biological organisation and explanandum under investigation.

## Why Agency Requires Clarification

Biology has achieved extraordinary explanatory success in revealing how living systems function. It explains metabolism, development, physiology, evolution, ecology, behaviour, and countless other aspects of biological organisation with increasing precision. Yet the success of these disciplines does not by itself resolve a more fundamental conceptual question: what unifies these diverse activities as the characteristic activity of life?

Biologists routinely describe organisms as regulating, repairing, adapting, developing, reproducing, and interacting with their environments. These descriptions identify important biological processes, but they do not by themselves make explicit the organisational activity through which these processes collectively sustain a living system. The concept of biological agency remains widely employed across the life sciences, yet its meaning often shifts between behaviour, autonomy, control, goal-directedness, cognition, or decision-making according to disciplinary context. The result is not conceptual failure but conceptual plurality.

This plurality reflects an underlying explanatory question that has not always been explicitly distinguished from the many biological phenomena through which it is expressed. The issue is not whether organisms act, but what kind of activity their actions collectively instantiate. Clarifying biological agency therefore requires identifying the organisational activity common to the diverse processes through which living systems maintain themselves.

Within APS, biological agency is not introduced as an additional biological mechanism, a competing theory, or a replacement for existing explanations. Rather, Agency provides an analytic projection through which activities already investigated across biology can be examined in relation to viability-oriented living organisation. Agency identifies the ongoing activity through which organisms continually sustain the conditions required for their own organised persistence.

The status of Agency as an analytic projection is clarified in [Living Reality and Biological Explanation — What Does APS Mean by an Analytic Projection?](/articles/what-does-aps-mean-by-an-analytic-projection/). That article explains how Agency can concern a materially real feature of living organisation while the distinction between Agency, Process, and Scale belongs to explanatory analysis rather than constituting a division of biological reality.

Clarifying agency in this way also clarifies its relationship to neighbouring biological concepts. Function describes the present contribution of traits and processes to viability. Development investigates the continuing reorganisation of living organisation across an individual life history. Evolution explains the historical transformation of biological organisation across generations. Ecology examines how organisms maintain viability within changing environmental relations. These remain distinct explanatory domains with their own explananda, methods, and evidential requirements. APS does not infer from their relation to living organisation that Agency supersedes their established explanations.

The purpose of this article is therefore not to argue that biology has overlooked agency, nor to replace existing biological explanations with a single organising principle. It is to make explicit the APS concept of biological agency and to establish the conditions under which its attribution and explanatory use are warranted.

## Agency as Activity Rather Than Property

Biological agency is often described as though it were a property that living systems possess. Organisms are said to have agency in much the same way that they have mass, shape, or genetic material. Within APS, however, agency is understood differently. Agency is not a static attribute added to an already living system. It is the continuing activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained.

This distinction is fundamental because living systems do not remain alive simply by possessing particular structures or components. Every organism continually regulates, repairs, reorganises, exchanges matter and energy with its surroundings, and responds to changing conditions. These activities are not secondary consequences of life; together they contribute to the ongoing organisational activity through which living systems maintain themselves. Agency therefore exists only in and through the continuing organisational activity of living systems.

Understanding agency as activity rather than property also explains why it cannot be identified with particular biological capacities such as movement, nervous systems, cognition, intelligence, or conscious intention. A bacterium regulating its internal chemistry, a plant reorganising its growth in response to environmental conditions, and an animal repairing damage all exhibit biological agency despite profound differences in their mechanisms. What unites them is not a shared behavioural repertoire but viability-oriented organisational activity.

Agency is therefore something living systems continually enact rather than something they simply possess. Agency is realised through the ongoing modulation of the organisational constraints and relations through which the conditions required for continued living are maintained. It is inseparable from the activity through which organisms sustain themselves despite continual internal and external change.

Viewing agency in this way also clarifies its relationship to the organisation of life itself. If agency were merely a property of living systems, it would remain unclear how that property contributes to biological continuity. Within APS, by contrast, agency is the present-tense activity through which living organisation continually maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence. The explanatory focus therefore shifts from identifying a characteristic possessed by organisms to understanding the activity through which biological organisation is continuously realised.

## Where Should Biological Agency Be Attributed?

Defining biological agency as viability-oriented organisational activity raises an important question of attribution. Living systems contain cells, tissues, organs, regulatory networks, symbiotic partners, and other organised structures and processes. Some biological phenomena also involve collectives extending beyond the conventional boundary of an individual organism. It does not follow that every identifiable organisation within, around, or beyond an organism constitutes an independent biological agent.

This problem has become increasingly prominent in contemporary discussions of biological agency. Bourrat and Barron (2026), examining proposals that agency and goal-directedness occur at multiple levels of biological organisation, distinguish the question of whether an entity is a plausible candidate for goal-directedness from the further question of whether goal-directed explanation is useful in a particular case. Their analysis is important because it resists the indiscriminate extension of agent language while retaining the possibility that agency-relevant organisation need not be confined to one conventionally privileged biological level.

APS shares the need for discrimination but approaches the problem through viability-oriented organisation rather than hierarchical position. A cell does not qualify as an agent merely because cells are conventionally assigned to one biological level, just as a tissue, organism, colony, or other collective does not acquire agency simply by occupying a wider organisational extent. Conversely, membership within a larger organisation does not by itself establish that a candidate cannot instantiate agency. The relevant question is whether the candidate organisation itself enacts viability-oriented activity through which the conditions of its own persistence are maintained or re-established.

This requires distinguishing **being an agent** from **participating in the organisation through which agency is enacted**. A molecular process, tissue structure, regulatory constraint, or cellular activity may make an indispensable contribution to the viability of a living system without thereby constituting an independent agent. Its causal and functional importance is not in question. What remains to be established is whether the candidate itself instantiates the viability-oriented organisation required for attribution of biological agency.

Nor should independent reproduction be treated as a universal APS criterion of agency. Reproduction can provide important evidence concerning biological autonomy and historical continuity, but the APS definition of agency concerns present-tense viability-oriented organisational activity. Sterility, developmental dependence, or participation within a larger biological organisation therefore cannot settle the attribution question by themselves. What matters is the organisation actually instantiated and the dependencies through which its viability is maintained.

Scale enters only after this distinction is made clear. Scale does not identify a hierarchy of candidate agents, nor does increasing spatial extent generate progressively higher forms of agency. It asks where the organisation relevant to the attribution is materially realised across spatial and temporal extents. Determining whether a candidate is an agent may therefore require following relations beyond an initially selected boundary, but widening the explanatory extent does not itself create another agent.

A further distinction concerns explanatory use. Even where attribution of biological agency is warranted, it does not follow that Agency provides the best explanation of every phenomenon involving that system. A mechanistic, physiological, developmental, ecological, or evolutionary explanation may already adequately explain the specified target. Agency provides explanatory gain only where making viability-oriented organisational activity explicit clarifies something about the explanandum that the relevant comparator leaves unresolved.

Bourrat and Barron therefore provide an important pressure test for APS. The APS account must be permissive enough to recognise biological agency wherever the requisite viability-oriented organisation is materially realised, but discriminating enough to prevent distributed biological organisation from becoming indiscriminate agent attribution. Agency is neither reserved for a privileged level nor multiplied automatically across every organisational extent.

## Self-Maintenance and Viability

If biological agency is an ongoing organisational activity rather than a property possessed by living systems, then that activity must itself be identified. Within APS, the principal expression of biological agency is self-maintenance organised relative to viability. Living systems continually maintain and re-establish the conditions required for their own continued organisation despite persistent internal change and environmental perturbation.

Self-maintenance is not simply the preservation of existing structures. Living organisms continuously replace molecular components, reorganise physiological processes, regulate exchanges with their surroundings, repair damage, and modify their organisation in response to changing conditions. The persistence of a living system therefore consists not in static continuity but in the continual renewal and reorganisation through which its viability is maintained.

Viability provides the normative reference for this activity. Biological organisation is not evaluated according to an external purpose or predetermined design but according to whether activity contributes to maintaining the conditions under which the organism can continue functioning as a living system. Activities that sustain those conditions contribute positively to viability; activities that undermine them contribute negatively. In this sense, viability establishes the biological asymmetry through which the organisation of living systems becomes intelligible.

Agency is therefore expressed through the continual organisational activity of self-maintenance. Organisms do not first exist as complete entities and subsequently engage in self-maintenance. Rather, their continuing existence depends upon the organisational activity through which viability is sustained. Agency is realised in the ongoing regulation and modulation of the relationships, processes, and constraints that enable living organisation to continue through change.

Understanding self-maintenance in this way also clarifies why biological agency differs from mere physical persistence. A flame may persist while fuel remains available, and a river may maintain a recognisable course despite continual material change, but neither thereby instantiates the viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation characteristic of life. Living systems continually contribute to generating, regulating, and re-establishing the organisational conditions upon which their viability depends. Self-maintenance is therefore not merely persistence through time; it is a principal expression of the activity through which living organisation maintains itself.

## Agency and Organised Persistence

Self-maintenance explains a principal form through which biological agency is enacted. Organised persistence identifies the continuing organisation sustained through that activity. These concepts are closely related within living systems yet remain analytically distinct. Agency identifies viability-oriented organisational activity; organised persistence identifies continuity of organisation through change.

Organised persistence should not be understood as a static condition or an enduring state. Living systems persist while molecular components are replaced, cells divide, tissues remodel, physiological processes fluctuate, and environmental conditions change. Biological continuity therefore does not require material invariance. It depends upon organisation being sufficiently maintained, renewed, reorganised, or re-established for the living system to continue functioning as that system.

This distinction clarifies why persistence can itself become a biological explanandum rather than a passive consequence of existence. A living organism does not continue merely because particular material components endure. Its persistence depends upon ongoing activity that regenerates, regulates, and reorganises conditions relevant to viability. Organised persistence is therefore neither a fixed structure nor an independently operating process. It identifies the continuity of living organisation maintained through change.

Agency and organised persistence are consequently closely related without being conceptually identical. Agency identifies the present-tense viability-oriented organisational activity through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their persistence. Organised persistence identifies the continuity of organisation that this activity helps sustain through change. Distinguishing them allows APS to ask separately what living systems do and what continuity that activity maintains.

This relationship is central to the explanatory architecture of APS, but it does not make Agency a substitute for the mechanisms investigated across biology. Mechanistic, physiological, developmental, ecological, and other explanations identify the processes and relations through which particular biological phenomena occur. Agency becomes explanatorily relevant when the explanandum concerns how such activity contributes to maintaining or re-establishing viability-oriented organisation.

The relationship between Agency and organised persistence also raises a question about explanatory extent. The organisation relevant to agency may depend upon materially realised relations extending beyond an initially selected boundary. APS addresses this through Scale.

## Agency and Explanatory Scale

Biological agency is enacted through materially realised biological activity in the present. Regulation, repair, growth, development, metabolism, and organism–environment interaction occur through identifiable processes, structures, constraints, and relations. Explaining an instance of agency therefore requires identifying the organisation through which viability-oriented activity is realised.

The spatial and temporal extent required for that explanation is a separate question. Within APS, **Scale** is the analytic projection concerned with where persistence relevant to an explanandum is organised across spatial and temporal extents. It does not divide biological reality into independent hierarchical levels, and it does not identify progressively wider or higher forms of agency.

This distinction is especially important when biological organisation is nested or distributed. Cellular activity can contribute to tissue organisation; tissue organisation can contribute to organismal viability; organism–environment relations can alter the conditions under which self-maintenance occurs; and developmental or evolutionary history can help explain why present organisation has the capacities it does. Which of these relations must be included depends upon the explanandum. Their relevance cannot be inferred merely from their occurrence at different spatial or temporal extents.

Nor does the inclusion of wider organisation establish another agent. A tissue may be indispensable to organismal agency without itself satisfying the conditions for independent attribution of biological agency. Conversely, a candidate organisation should not be excluded from agency merely because it occurs within, overlaps with, or depends upon a wider organisation. Attribution depends upon the viability-oriented organisation instantiated by the candidate; Scale concerns the extent across which that organisation and its relevant dependencies must be followed.

APS therefore does not treat wider extents as exerting independent causal control over local biological processes. Scales do not act upon one another. Where organisation extending beyond an initially selected boundary alters local activity, explanation should identify the materially implemented interactions, constraints, environmental conditions, inheritances, or other relations through which that difference is produced.

This also makes explanatory extent target-dependent. Some instances of agency may be adequately explained within a relatively bounded organisation. Others may require relations extending across broader spatial or temporal extents. A wider explanation is warranted only when those additional relations make a difference to explaining the specified target.

Agency and Scale are therefore complementary analytic projections rather than different locations of biological reality. Agency asks what living systems do: they enact viability-oriented organisational activity. Scale asks where the persistence relevant to explaining that activity is organised. Neither determines the other, and neither establishes a hierarchy of agents.

The distinction between agency attribution and explanatory Scale prepares the transition to function and normativity. Once the relevant viability-oriented organisation has been identified, particular processes, traits, and activities can be assessed according to the contributions they make to maintaining that organisation.

## Agency, Function, and Normativity

Understanding biological agency as viability-oriented organisational activity clarifies the meaning of biological function. Within APS, functions are not defined simply by the structures that perform them, nor solely by the evolutionary histories through which those structures arose. A biological function is the present-tense organisational contribution that a trait, process, or activity makes to maintaining the viability of the living system in which it participates.

This perspective follows directly from the conception of agency developed in the preceding sections. If living systems continually maintain themselves through viability-oriented organisational activity, then the functional significance of a biological activity concerns its contribution to that continuing organisation. Evolutionary history may explain how a trait or capacity arose and why it has its present organisation, but history does not by itself constitute the function the activity presently performs.

Viewing function in this way also clarifies biological normativity. Living systems instantiate a viability-relative asymmetry between conditions and activities that sustain their organisation and those that undermine it. This asymmetry is neither imposed from outside the organism nor derived from conscious judgement. It arises from the organisation of the living system itself.

Within APS, biological normativity should not be confused with morality, conscious evaluation, or intentional purpose. It concerns the viability-relative difference made by conditions and activities to continued living organisation. Nor does it require deliberation, representation, or cognition. Biological normativity is therefore compatible with forms of life lacking the cognitive capacities required for reflective evaluation.

Agency, function, and normativity are analytically distinguishable aspects of living organisation. Agency identifies viability-oriented organisational activity. Function identifies the present organisational contribution of particular traits, processes, or activities to viability. Normativity identifies the viability-relative asymmetry through which those contributions differ biologically. Their relationship does not make them interchangeable.

This understanding also clarifies why APS treats biological purpose without requiring intention. Living organisation can be viability-oriented without consciously anticipating future states, representing goals, or acting under external design. Purpose in this restricted biological sense concerns viability-oriented organisation rather than psychological intention. More demanding forms of goal-directedness, cognition, and reflective purpose require additional criteria and should not be inferred from biological agency alone.

The organisational activity described by Agency also depends upon constraints through which biological processes are channelled and maintained. This introduces the relationship between agency and constraint closure.

## Agency, Constraint Closure, and Organisational Maintenance

The continuing activity of biological agency depends upon an organisation capable of maintaining and re-establishing conditions through which that activity remains possible. Within APS, this organisational architecture is described through constraint closure. Agency identifies viability-oriented organisational activity; constraint closure identifies the organisation of mutually dependent constraints through which that activity can be sustained.

Biological constraints should not be understood merely as external restrictions imposed upon otherwise independent processes. They are materially realised organisational conditions that channel biological activity. Enzymatic regulation, membrane organisation, developmental control, physiological regulation, and many other biological processes involve constraints whose operation depends upon other parts of the living organisation.

These constraints are themselves maintained, modified, repaired, or replaced through biological activity. Living organisation therefore persists not because its constraints remain unchanged but because the organisation through which they make a difference is continually maintained and reorganised.

Constraint closure describes the organisation through which mutually supporting constraints contribute to maintaining the conditions required for their continued operation. It does not eliminate the need to identify the particular mechanisms, processes, and material relations through which this organisation is realised. Nor should constraint closure be treated as an independently acting cause.

This relationship clarifies the complementary roles of agency and constraint closure within APS. Agency asks what living systems do: they enact viability-oriented organisational activity. Constraint closure characterises the organisation through which relevant constraints are mutually maintained. These descriptions concern different explanatory relations within the same living organisation rather than identifying separate entities or causal levels.

Their relationship also clarifies organised persistence. Persistence is not produced by Agency or constraint closure acting as independent causes. It is the continuity of living organisation realised through materially implemented activity and relations. Agency, constraint closure, and organised persistence therefore make different explanatory distinctions within one viability-oriented organisation.

This prevents constraint closure from becoming a complete explanation of life by itself. Constraint closure identifies a defining organisational feature of living systems, while Agency identifies the present-tense activity through which living organisation is enacted. The canonical APS definition therefore remains: **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.** Biological agency is the activity through which that organisation is enacted and sustained.

## Why Agency Is Fundamental to Life

The preceding sections have developed an integrated account of biological agency. Agency is the viability-oriented organisational activity through which living systems continually maintain and re-establish the conditions of their persistence. Self-maintenance identifies a principal expression of this activity; organised persistence identifies the continuity sustained through it; function and normativity articulate viability-relative contribution and asymmetry; constraint closure characterises the organisation through which relevant constraints are maintained; and Scale asks where persistence relevant to an explanandum is organised across spatial and temporal extents. Together, these distinctions locate Agency within APS without turning it into an independent mechanism, property, or hierarchical level.

Agency is fundamental not because it replaces existing biological explanations but because it identifies what living systems do as living systems. Biological disciplines investigate metabolism, physiology, development, evolution, ecology, behaviour, and cognition through different explananda, methods, and bodies of evidence. Their explanatory achievements do not depend upon being redescribed in agency language. APS instead asks when making viability-oriented organisational activity explicit helps clarify how a specified phenomenon relates to living persistence.

This perspective also explains why Agency should not be confined to behaviour, movement, cognition, or decision-making. These are particular biological capacities or phenomena. Agency identifies the more general viability-oriented organisational activity presupposed by living persistence. Cognition can therefore involve agency without agency thereby becoming cognition, just as behaviour can express agency without defining it.

The explanatory importance of Agency follows from the APS definition of life. If life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation, then biological agency identifies the activity through which that organisation is enacted and sustained. This does not make Agency an additional substance or force. It identifies an analytic projection of the living organisation itself.

For this reason, APS proposes biological agency as the defining activity of life. The proposal does not alter the empirical content of biological science or introduce a new biological mechanism. It identifies an explanatory relation between the organisation characteristic of life and the activity through which that organisation continues.

The attribution boundary developed in this article is essential to that proposal. If Agency were attributed wherever biological causation, organisation, function, or goal-like behaviour occurred, the concept would lose discriminatory value. Biological agency must therefore be grounded in viability-oriented organisation rather than inferred from complexity, hierarchical position, functional contribution, or suggestive behavioural description alone.

## Implications for Biological Explanation

The account of biological agency developed throughout this article has implications beyond the definition of a single biological concept. If agency is understood as viability-oriented organisational activity, it provides one analytic projection through which APS can compare explanations concerned with how living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their persistence. Agency itself is not a comparative explanatory methodology; it functions within the broader APS methodology.

Modern biology encompasses a wide range of explanatory traditions. Molecular biology investigates biochemical processes and their organisation, physiology examines regulation and organismal activity, developmental biology explains transformation across individual life histories, evolutionary biology accounts for historical change across generations, and ecology investigates organism–environment relations. Each retains its own explanatory targets, methods, concepts, and standards of evidence.

APS does not seek to replace this explanatory plurality with a single theory or organising principle. Agency analysis becomes relevant when viability-oriented organisational activity forms part of the explanandum or when making that organisation explicit contributes something not supplied by the strongest established explanation of the same target.

This distinction is important because attribution and explanation can come apart. A bacterium may unambiguously qualify as a biological agent while a particular biochemical event within it is best explained mechanistically without invoking Agency. Conversely, an investigation of recovery from perturbation, self-maintenance, or viability-relative reorganisation may make the organisation of agency directly relevant to what requires explanation.

The distinction also clarifies the organisation of life and the organisation of explanation. Living organisation can be materially integrated without biological explanations forming a single hierarchy or unified explanatory theory. Different questions legitimately require different explanatory resources. APS therefore seeks comparative intelligibility rather than explanatory uniformity.

The methodological contribution of APS lies in asking when Agency makes an explanatory difference. An attribution of agency may be biologically warranted while adding little to the explanation of a particular phenomenon. Conversely, an explanandum concerned with self-maintenance, viability-relative activity, or the organisation through which persistence is re-established may require Agency to be made explicit. The appropriate judgement is comparative: APS must assess the agency-based analysis against the strongest established explanation of the same target rather than assuming explanatory gain from the vocabulary of agency itself.

## Conclusion

Biological agency is not a specialised capacity confined to behaviour, cognition, or intentional action. Within APS, biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity: the present-tense activity through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

Understanding agency in this way clarifies why living systems differ from merely persistent physical structures. Organisms continually regulate, repair, reorganise, and modify conditions relevant to their viability. Self-maintenance is therefore a principal expression of biological agency rather than an additional property possessed by an otherwise complete living system.

This account also places limits on agency attribution. Biological organisation can contain many causally important components, processes, constraints, and nested or distributed relations without every such organisation constituting an independent agent. Participation in the organisation through which agency is enacted is not sufficient for independent agency attribution. The relevant question is whether the candidate itself instantiates viability-oriented organisation through activity that maintains or re-establishes conditions of its own persistence.

Neither hierarchical position nor spatial extent answers that question. Scale does not identify levels at which agents appear. It asks where persistence relevant to the explanandum is organised across spatial and temporal extents. Determining whether agency is instantiated may require following materially realised relations across those extents, but explanatory extent and agency attribution remain distinct questions.

A further distinction concerns explanatory gain. Even warranted agency attribution does not establish that Agency provides the best explanation of every phenomenon involving an agent. Existing mechanistic, physiological, developmental, ecological, or evolutionary explanations may already be sufficient. Agency becomes explanatorily useful where making viability-oriented organisational activity explicit clarifies the specified target beyond what the relevant comparator explains.

APS therefore neither reserves agency for a privileged biological level nor distributes agents indiscriminately throughout biological organisation. Agency is attributed where viability-oriented organisational activity is materially instantiated and used explanatorily where that attribution makes a demonstrated difference to the explanandum.

**Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

Biological agency is the activity through which that organisation is enacted and sustained.

## Introduction

**Where This Article Fits**

*This article provides the core APS account of **Process**, the analytic projection concerned with how continuity is maintained, reorganised, or re-established despite change. It develops the dynamic dimension of organised persistence while distinguishing Process from the materially realised biological processes through which living organisation is maintained.*

*APS therefore shares important explanatory concerns with processual approaches to biology without requiring process ontology as its metaphysical foundation. Process is an analytic projection rather than a claim that living systems are fundamentally processes, just as Agency and Scale are analytic projections rather than independent components or levels of biological reality. The article establishes this distinction for subsequent APS treatments of development, repair, regeneration, evolution, temporal organisation, and other cases in which continuity through change becomes an explanatory target.*

Biological systems are often described in terms of structure: cells, tissues, organs, and networks. Processes may then be described as events or activities occurring within or between these structures.

This distinction can be useful, but living organisation cannot be understood solely as the persistence of unchanged structures. Living systems undergo continual material turnover, physiological change, development, repair, and reorganisation. The importance of such dynamic continuity has become a major theme in contemporary processual philosophy of biology (Dupré & Nicholson 2018; Nicholson & Dupré 2018).

Structures and processes are therefore biologically interdependent. Membranes must be maintained, components renewed, physiological relations regulated, and damaged organisation repaired or reorganised. Materially realised biological activity contributes to the continuing organisation through which living systems persist.

APS uses **Process** as the analytic projection concerned with how such continuity is maintained despite change. It does not make Process an entity, causal force, or biological component in addition to the activities and relations being investigated.

## Process Beyond Change

In conventional usage, a process may be understood as a sequence of events or activities unfolding through time.

That usage remains legitimate. Biological processes such as metabolism, repair, development, physiological regulation, and signalling are materially realised activities with particular mechanisms and consequences.

Processual approaches to biology go further. They have argued that metabolic turnover, life cycles, ecological interdependence, and other features of living systems motivate understanding biological entities themselves as processes rather than enduring substances (Dupré & Nicholson 2018). Questions of biological identity and persistence have consequently become central to process ontology (DiFrisco 2018).

Recent work extends this processual orientation directly into phylogenetic theory. Kearney and Haber (2026) argue that contemporary phylogeny is better understood as a multidimensional processual system comprising spatial, temporal, and hierarchical dimensions and interacting lineage histories rather than as a single absolute history. They accordingly propose process ontology as a foundation for contemporary phylogenetics. APS can recognise the explanatory importance of historically extended, changing lineage organisation without requiring that stronger ontological conclusion.

APS introduces a related but distinct analytic question. **Process asks how continuity is maintained, reorganised, or re-established despite change.**

Temporal succession alone is therefore insufficient. Events may follow one another without contributing to the continuing organisation of a living system. Process analysis asks how materially realised activities and relations contribute to continuity through such change.

## Process and Processual Biology

APS shares with processual biology an emphasis on turnover, activity, transformation, and the problem of biological persistence. It therefore rejects any picture of living systems as simply static structures whose identity is independent of the activities through which they are maintained.

The agreement does not require complete metaphysical agreement.

Contemporary processual philosophy has developed the stronger proposal that living beings should be understood ontologically as processes rather than substances or things (Dupré & Nicholson 2018; Nicholson & Dupré 2018). APS does not require that conclusion in order to use Process analytically.

For APS, the biological facts and the explanatory projection should be distinguished. Material turnover, repair, metabolism, development, regulation, and other biological processes are materially real. **Process**, capitalised as an APS term, is the analytic projection that asks how such activities contribute to continuity despite change. The philosophical status of this distinction is developed in [Living Reality and Biological Explanation — What Does APS Mean by an Analytic Projection?](/articles/what-does-aps-mean-by-an-analytic-projection/). That article explains how Process can direct an investigator-selected explanatory question toward materially real biological organisation without treating Process as an independent component, cause, or division of that reality.

APS can therefore take the empirical motivations of processual biology seriously while leaving broader questions about process ontology open. Its immediate methodological commitment is narrower: when continuity through change is the explanatory target, biological explanation should identify the materially realised activities and organisational relations through which that continuity is maintained, reorganised, or re-established.

## Process as Organisational Activity

Living organisation depends upon ongoing material activity.

Constraints can shape the possibilities available to biological activity, while biological activities can maintain, modify, regenerate, or reorganise constraints upon which continued organisation depends. Organisational approaches formalise an important version of this relationship through the distinction between processes and constraints and through closure among mutually dependent constraints (Montévil & Mossio 2015; Moreno & Mossio 2015).

The biological work is performed by these materially realised activities, processes, constraints, and organisational relations. **Process is the analytic projection through which APS asks how they contribute to continuity despite change.**

This distinction prevents the analytic category from being mistaken for an additional biological cause. Process does not itself generate, maintain, or reorganise living systems. It brings into explanatory focus how living organisation accomplishes such maintenance and reorganisation through materially realised activity.

## Process and Constraint Closure

Constraint closure concerns organisation in which relevant constraints are mutually sustained through the activities and dependencies of the system (Montévil & Mossio 2015; Moreno & Mossio 2015).

Such organisation cannot persist through temporal passage alone. Constraints, material conditions, and the activities through which they are maintained may themselves require renewal, repair, modification, or reorganisation. Organisational accounts of biological regulation further show how regulatory activity can modulate constitutive organisation in response to changing internal and external conditions (Bich et al. 2016).

Process therefore asks **how constraint-closed organisation is maintained or re-established through change**. The answer must identify the materially realised activities and dependency relations through which this occurs.

Process does not itself enact constraint closure. Nor does the place of a relation within APS establish its causal importance. Relevant dependencies must be materially specified and assessed for the biological target under investigation.

## Process, Agency, and Scale

Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

- **Agency** concerns what living systems do.
- **Process** concerns how continuity is maintained despite change.
- **Scale** concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

These are analytic projections rather than components of reality. They are not independent causes, entities, domains, dimensions, or hierarchical levels that subsequently interact.

Their distinction reflects different explanatory questions that can be asked about the same materially realised living organisation.

An explanatory target may make one, two, or all three projections relevant. APS does not assume that every biological explanation requires all three, and the relevance of a projection must be established by the question being investigated.

## Process and Viability

Living systems persist only while conditions compatible with their continued organisation are maintained or re-established.

Material biological activities such as metabolism, physiological regulation, development, repair, and behavioural adjustment can contribute to maintaining or reorganising those conditions. Organisational accounts of regulation provide detailed examples of how biological control can contribute to maintenance under perturbation without treating regulation as an abstract force acting independently of its material implementation (Bich et al. 2016).

Process asks how such activities contribute to continuity through change.

Viability and Process therefore answer different questions within APS. Viability concerns conditions under which continued organisation can succeed or fail. Process concerns how living organisation maintains, loses, reorganises, or re-establishes continuity as those conditions and the system itself change through time.

Process is not the means by which maintenance occurs. The means are the materially realised biological processes, constraints, interactions, and organisational relations identified by the relevant explanation.

## Process, Mechanism, and Organisation

Taking biological process seriously does not require treating mechanistic explanation as inadequate.

Mechanistic approaches characteristically explain phenomena by identifying organised entities and activities that produce them (Machamer, Darden & Craver 2000). Such explanations can therefore be dynamic and materially specific rather than merely descriptions of static structure.

The organisational and mechanistic perspectives can also be complementary. Work connecting autonomy theory with mechanistic explanation argues that organisational questions about self-maintenance can guide investigation while mechanisms specify the activities through which such organisation is materially realised (Bich & Bechtel 2021).

APS adopts the same methodological restraint. Process does not replace mechanism. Where a mechanistic explanation adequately answers the biological question, APS does not require its reconstruction. Process becomes relevant when the explanatory target specifically concerns how the activities identified by such explanations contribute to continuity, reorganisation, or recovery through change.

## Process Across Spatial and Temporal Extents

Biological processes can occur across different spatial and temporal extents.

Molecular interactions may contribute to physiological organisation. Physiological activities may participate in organismal regulation. Development unfolds across substantial portions of an organism's lifetime. Ecological relations may alter conditions relevant to persistence, while evolutionary change extends across generations.

These facts do not imply that scales themselves interact, coordinate biological activity, or exercise causal influence.

Where organisation extending beyond a local boundary alters local activity, explanation should identify the materially implemented constraints, interactions, and conditions through which that alteration occurs.

Process and Scale therefore ask distinguishable questions. Process asks how continuity is maintained despite change. Scale asks where the persistence relevant to the explanandum is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

Both may be relevant to a particular explanation, but neither their joint relevance nor a wider explanatory extent should be assumed in advance. Additional spatial or temporal extent is warranted when the materially realised relations it includes make an explanatory difference to the target.

## Why Process Matters

Process helps APS formulate a specific family of explanatory questions:

- How is continuity maintained despite continual material and organisational change?
- Which biological activities maintain, renew, or reorganise conditions required for persistence?
- How can organisation remain continuous when components and structures change?
- Under what conditions is continuity maintained, transformed, re-established, or lost?

These questions do not make structural or mechanistic explanation secondary. Structural, compositional, mechanistic, developmental, physiological, evolutionary, mathematical, and other explanations may each be sufficient for the biological targets they address.

Process becomes relevant where the explanatory target specifically concerns how living organisation continues or reorganises through change.

## Conclusion

Living systems persist through materially realised activity rather than through the invariance of all their components or structures.

Components are renewed, constraints maintained or reorganised, physiological relations regulated, and damaged organisation repaired or re-established. Processual and organisational approaches have independently made the biological significance of such dynamic maintenance increasingly explicit (Montévil & Mossio 2015; Dupré & Nicholson 2018).

APS uses **Process** to investigate how this continuity is accomplished.

Process is therefore not what happens to life, nor is it a force or activity through which life exists. It is the analytic projection concerned with **how continuity is maintained despite change**.

The distinction is analytic; the biological activities, processes, constraints, interactions, and organisational relations through which continuity is maintained are materially real.

TTogether with Agency and Scale, Process provides one analytic projection through which the same viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation can be investigated. Which of these projections contributes to an explanation depends upon the biological target under investigation.

## Scale — How Life Is Coordinated Across Space and Time

> **Where this article fits:** This Core article establishes the meaning and explanatory role of Scale within APS. Scale asks where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. It does not introduce an additional biological mechanism, causal level, or component of living organisation. Material spatial relations can themselves contribute constitutively to biological organisation; Material spatial relations can themselves contribute constitutively to biological organisation; Scale provides the analytic projection through which the spatial and temporal extent of that organisation becomes the target of analysis.

## Introduction

Biological organisation is materially realised across space and time. Molecules interact within cells; cellular structures occupy particular positions and orientations; cells form tissues whose organisation depends upon boundaries, geometries, local environments, and material relations. Organisms, in turn, maintain their activity through processes distributed across different spatial extents and temporal durations. Biology often describes such differences using terms such as level, hierarchy, size, resolution, or scale. These descriptions can be useful, but they do not by themselves explain how living organisation persists.

APS uses **Scale** in a more specific explanatory sense:

**Scale is the analytic projection concerned with where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.**

Scale therefore does not designate a separate entity or causal domain within a living system, nor is it simply another name for spatial structure. The explanatory object remains one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Scale makes explicit the spatial and temporal extents across which the organisation relevant to a particular biological explanandum is materially realised and maintained.

## Scale Beyond Levels

Biology frequently organises phenomena into familiar domains such as molecular, cellular, tissue, organismal, population, ecological, and evolutionary, and nothing in APS requires abandoning those descriptions. The difficulty arises when descriptive divisions are treated as though they were independently acting causal levels, or when assigning a phenomenon to a level is taken to explain it. A molecule does not act because it belongs to a molecular level, a cell does not regulate because it occupies a cellular level, and an organism does not persist because organismal organisation occupies a higher position in a hierarchy. In each case, the relevant causal interactions are materially realised interactions among processes, structures, constraints, and environmental conditions.

These relations can extend across spatial and temporal extents that do not correspond neatly to a single conventional level. APS therefore does not replace one hierarchy with another hierarchy called Scale. Instead, Scale asks:

**Across what spatial and temporal extents is the organisation relevant to this explanandum actually realised?**

This shifts explanatory attention from a presumed ordering of levels toward the organisation that must be identified in the biological case itself.

## Scale as an Analytic Projection

Scale does not itself organise biological activity; living systems do. Their organisation is materially realised through processes, structures, boundaries, constraints, interactions, and environmental relations extending through space and time. Scale identifies the analytic projection through which the extent of that organisation is investigated. This distinction matters because statements such as “Scale coordinates activity” or “causation operates between scales” can inadvertently turn an analytic distinction into a causal entity. The philosophical status of this distinction is developed in [Living Reality and Biological Explanation — What Does APS Mean by an Analytic Projection?](/articles/what-does-aps-mean-by-an-analytic-projection/). That article explains how Scale can direct an investigator-selected explanatory question toward materially real spatial and temporal organisation without turning Scale itself into a biological level, causal domain, or division of reality.

APS instead asks what materially implemented relations alter the conditions under which local activity occurs, how those relations are maintained, and across what spatial and temporal extents the resulting organisation must be analysed. A physiological process, for example, may depend upon molecular reactions, cellular organisation, tissue architecture, vascular relations, neural regulation, and organism–environment interaction. The explanatory task is not completed by saying that these processes occur at different scales; it requires identifying the organisation through which those materially realised relations contribute to continued functioning.

Scale tells us **where that organisation must be sought and delimited**.

## Spatial Structure and Scale

The distinction between **spatial structure** and **Scale** is especially important. Hermida and Ladyman (2026) argue that biological organisation often consists in, or depends upon, spatial structure together with physical interactions among the parts of biological systems. Spatial features such as position, configuration, shape, directionality, orientation, boundaries, and location can therefore matter directly to biological organisation. APS does not redescribe these material spatial relations as Scale. A membrane boundary, the orientation of a cellular structure, the position of a signalling component, or the geometry of a tissue can contribute materially to what a biological system does; those relations belong to the organisation being explained.

**Scale is the analytic projection under which we ask where that organisation is realised across spatial and temporal extents.**

The distinction can therefore be stated simply:

**Spatial organisation concerns materially realised biological relations. Scale concerns the explanatory identification of the extents across which organised persistence is realised.**

The two are closely related, but they are not interchangeable. Recognising the constitutive importance of spatial structure also does not require APS to make a general claim against physical or part–whole explanation. Hermida and Ladyman explicitly distinguish their criticism of genetic and molecular reductionism from physical and suitably spatially informed part–whole explanation, and APS is compatible with that methodological caution. Its concern is not to establish Scale as a rival to physical, mechanistic, spatial, or compositional explanation, but to clarify what must be specified when the organisation relevant to persistence extends beyond the boundaries assumed by a more local description.

## Coordination Across Spatial and Temporal Extents

Living organisation often depends upon relations realised across more than one spatial or temporal extent. Molecular processes operate within cellular structures, cellular activity occurs within tissue organisation, and physiological regulation depends upon materially realised relations distributed through an organism. Organismal activity likewise modifies and is modified by environmental conditions. None of these statements requires scales themselves to interact: the causal work is performed by the materially realised processes and constraints.

A wider organisational arrangement can alter local conditions because that arrangement is implemented through material relations that constrain the possibilities available to local activity. Conversely, local processes can modify the material organisation within which other processes occur. APS therefore avoids treating cross-scale influence as causation exercised by one level over another. The explanatory question is instead:

**Which materially realised relations connect the relevant activities, and across what spatial and temporal extents must those relations be followed to explain continued organisation?**

Persistence may depend upon such distributed coordination, but the coordination belongs to the living organisation. Scale identifies where that organisation is distributed.

## Homeorhesis and Organised Persistence

Living organisation is not maintained by preserving an unchanged state. Material components turn over, structures change, development alters form, environmental conditions vary, and regulatory relations are repeatedly maintained, modified, and re-established. Homeorhesis can describe this persistence through an organised trajectory rather than through static constancy.

Scale does not provide the continuity of such a trajectory. Rather, it asks across what spatial and temporal extents the materially realised organisation underlying that continuity must be tracked. A trajectory may therefore require explanation over seconds or years, within a cell or across an organism, depending upon the explanandum. What persists is the organisation; Scale identifies the extent over which that persistence is organised.

## Scale and the APS Triad

Scale is one of three analytic projections through which APS organises biological explanation:

- **Agency** concerns what living systems do.
- **Process** concerns how continuity is maintained despite change.
- **Scale** concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

These are **analytic projections rather than components of reality**. They are not three independent mechanisms, three stages through which living systems pass, or biological levels, and persistence does not arise from their interaction. The explanatory object is one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation: Agency makes its activity explicit, Process makes its continuity through change explicit, and Scale makes the spatial and temporal extent of its organised persistence explicit.

The three projections can therefore place different explanatory questions before the same biological case without dividing the living organisation into separate ontological dimensions.

## Scale-Indexed Organisation

Biological properties cannot automatically be transferred from one spatial or temporal extent to another. Evidence that a local process contributes to regulation does not establish that a larger aggregate is itself an agent; evidence that components exchange information does not establish cognition at every encompassing domain; and the persistence of an ecological or evolutionary pattern does not automatically establish that the ecological system, species, or lineage possesses the same organisational properties as a living organism.

Scale therefore performs an important discriminatory role in explanation. Claims must be warranted for the organisation to which they are attributed and for the spatial and temporal extent over which that organisation is materially realised. This is particularly important for concepts such as:

- biological agency
- normativity
- cognition
- semiosis
- individuality
- and organised persistence

Similarity across domains can justify comparison, but it does not by itself establish identity of biological organisation. Scale analysis therefore helps prevent the uncritical projection of explanatory concepts from one domain or extent to another.

## Why Scale Matters

Scale matters because biological explananda frequently depend upon organisation whose relevant boundaries cannot be assumed in advance. A local mechanism may be sufficient for one question, while another may require relations extending across cells, tissues, an organism, or organism–environment coupling. Still other questions concern developmental, ecological, or evolutionary phenomena whose spatial and temporal extents differ from those of organismal self-maintenance.

APS does not assume that every biological explanation must encompass the widest available extent, nor does it presume that a broader description is intrinsically more explanatory than a local one. The relevant extent is determined by the explanandum and by the materially realised dependencies required to explain it. Scale therefore helps clarify:

- why conventional level labels do not by themselves supply explanations;
- where the organisation relevant to an explanandum is materially realised;
- when a more local or more extended explanatory domain is required;
- how spatial and temporal organisation contribute to biological persistence;
- and why biological properties must be attributed only where the relevant organisation is established.

Scale is therefore **target-sensitive**. More Scale is not more explanation; the relevant Scale is the spatial and temporal extent required by the explanatory target.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

**Where is the organisation relevant to biological persistence realised across spatial and temporal extents, and what explanatory difference does identifying those extents make?**

### Architectural Role

This Core article establishes the APS meaning of **Scale**:

**Scale is the analytic projection concerned with where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.**

It does not constitute a biological mechanism, causal level, spatial structure, or independent organisational dimension. The material organisation identified under a Scale analysis may include boundaries, configurations, spatial relations, constraints, interactions, trajectories, and environmental couplings. Those relations belong to the biological system or explanandum under investigation; Scale identifies their relevant explanatory extent.

### Spatial-Organisation Comparator

Hermida and Ladyman (2026) provide an important contemporary comparator because they defend the constitutive explanatory importance of spatial structure in biology, but their account and APS Scale are not identical proposals. Hermida and Ladyman address how material spatial organisation contributes to biological phenomena; APS Scale asks where persistence-relevant organisation is realised across spatial and temporal extents.

Where these approaches address the same explanatory target, APS does not assume an additional explanatory contribution merely by redescribing the phenomenon in Scale terminology. Any claimed explanatory gain requires target-matched comparison with the strongest established account of that target.

### Position Within APS

Scale must be understood together with Agency and Process without being converted into an independently acting component of living organisation.

**Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

Agency concerns what that organisation does, Process concerns how its continuity is maintained despite change, and Scale concerns where that persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. These projections can be distinguished analytically because different biological questions give different explanatory priority to activity, continuity, or extent; they nevertheless concern the same materially realised organisation.

## Conclusion

Scale is not size, hierarchy, or an independently acting biological level, nor is it identical to spatial structure. Material spatial relations can contribute constitutively to biological organisation: boundaries, positions, configurations, orientations, shapes, constraints, and physical interactions can all matter to how living systems function and persist. APS Scale asks a different but complementary explanatory question:

**Where is the organisation through which persistence is maintained realised across spatial and temporal extents?**

The answer must be established from the biological case rather than imposed by a predetermined hierarchy. Living organisation performs the causal and coordinative work; Scale makes its relevant extent explicit. Within APS, Scale therefore remains an analytic projection of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation: the projection concerned with **where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents**.

## Introduction

What makes a biological process a function rather than merely something that happens?

Few concepts have been more centralâ€”or more contestedâ€”in biology than biological function. Biologists routinely describe hearts as pumping blood, kidneys as filtering waste, leaves as capturing light, immune systems as defending organisms, and nervous systems as coordinating behaviour. Yet what distinguishes these activities as functions, rather than simply causal processes, has remained one of the enduring questions of the philosophy of biology.

Over the past century, several influential answers have been proposed. Some accounts explain function through evolutionary history, arguing that functions are the effects for which traits were selected. Others understand function as the causal contribution that a component makes within a larger system. More recent organisational approaches locate function within the self-maintaining organisation of living systems. Each of these perspectives captures an important aspect of biological explanation, yet none fully explains why biological functions are intrinsically related to the continued existence of the organisms that enact them.

APS argues that biological function becomes intelligible only when understood relative to viability-oriented organised persistence. A process is functional because it contributes to the ongoing organisation through which a living system actively maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence across changing conditions. Functions are therefore not externally assigned purposes, merely historical evolutionary effects, or isolated mechanistic roles. They are present-tense organisational contributions to the continuity of living organisation.

Understanding function in this way also explains why biological normativity emerges naturally. Because organised persistence can succeed or fail, some organisational contributions preserve viability while others undermine it. Functional organisation therefore establishes intrinsic distinctions between success and failure, maintenance and breakdown, adaptation and dysfunction. These distinctions are not imposed by external observers but emerge from the organisation of living systems themselves.

This account has consequences extending well beyond function alone. By grounding function in viability-oriented organised persistence, APS also explains how mechanisms acquire biological significance, how agency enacts functional organisation, how evaluation operationalises normativity, how semiosis emerges from biologically significant differences, how adaptation reorganises continuity-preserving activity, and why malfunction and diagnosis are objective features of living organisation rather than merely descriptive labels.

This article develops that organisational account of function and examines its implications for biological explanation more broadly. Throughout, APS argues that function is not an isolated biological concept but one expression of the viability-oriented organisation through which living systems actively sustain themselves across continual change.

## Function Beyond Teleology

If biological functions are not externally assigned purposes, what makes them genuine functions?

Since the decline of classical teleology, biology has sought to explain function without appealing to externally imposed purposes or irreducible final causes. Yet abandoning classical teleology did not eliminate the concept of function. Instead, it raised a more fundamental question: what distinguishes a biological function from an ordinary causal process?

Different traditions have offered different answers. Selected-effects accounts explain functions through the evolutionary history of traits, whereas causal-role accounts understand functions as the contributions components make within larger systems. Organisational approaches locate function within the self-maintaining organisation of living systems. Each perspective captures an important aspect of biological explanation, yet each also leaves open the relationship between function and the present organisation of living systems.

APS argues that biological functions become intelligible only in relation to viability-oriented organised persistence. Functions are neither mystical purposes imposed upon living systems nor merely retrospective products of evolutionary history. Nor are they reducible to isolated mechanistic operations considered independently of the organisation to which they contribute.

Within APS, a biological function is:

an organisational contribution to viability-oriented organised persistence.

A process is functional insofar as it contributes to maintaining or re-establishing the organisation through which a living system preserves the conditions of its own persistence across changing conditions. Function is therefore a present-tense organisational property grounded in the ongoing activity of living systems, while evolutionary history explains how such organisations came to exist rather than what presently makes them functional.

APS therefore naturalises biological function without appealing to external purpose or reducing function to historical origin or mechanistic description alone. Function emerges from the organisation of living systems themselves.

## Function and Organised Persistence
How do biological functions preserve living organisation through continual change?

Living systems are never static. Their components undergo continual material turnover, energetic fluctuation, developmental transformation, ecological perturbation, injury, repair, and adaptive reorganisation. Yet despite this continual change, living systems maintain their organisational continuity.

This raises an important biological question. If organisms are constantly changing, in what sense do their functions contribute to persistence rather than simply accompanying change?

APS argues that biological functions contribute directly to the maintenance and re-establishment of organised persistence. A process is functional because it helps preserve the organisational conditions through which a living system remains viable across changing circumstances.

This can be seen across many domains of biological organisation. Metabolism functions because it sustains energetic continuity. Repair functions because it reconstructs degraded organisation. Immune activity functions because it preserves viable persistence under perturbation. Perception functions because it modulates activity relative to conditions that affect continued viability.

These examples illustrate a common organisational principle. Biological functions are not defined by the particular mechanisms they employ but by their contribution to maintaining and re-establishing the organised persistence of the living system. Function therefore concerns organisational contribution rather than isolated mechanical operation.

Living systems persist because different functional relations make different contributions to continuity-preserving organisation. The asymmetries between continuity-supporting and continuity-undermining contributions provide the organisational basis from which biological normativity subsequently emerges.

## Function and Mechanism

If mechanisms explain how biological processes occur, what makes some mechanisms biological functions?

Mechanistic explanation has become one of the defining achievements of modern biology. Metabolic pathways, gene regulatory networks, immune responses, developmental systems, neural circuits, and behavioural processes are all investigated by identifying the mechanisms through which they operate. These explanations reveal how biological activities are materially realised.

Yet an important philosophical question remains. If every biological process can be described mechanistically, what distinguishes those mechanisms that constitute biological functions from those that are merely causal events?

APS argues that mechanistic explanation is indispensable but not explanatorily self-sufficient. Mechanisms explain how biological processes occur. They do not by themselves explain why particular mechanisms contribute to the continued organisation of a living system.

Within APS, mechanisms become biologically functional because they participate in viability-oriented organised persistence. A metabolic pathway, neural circuit, immune response, developmental process, or behavioural pattern is biologically functional insofar as it contributes to maintaining or re-establishing the organisational conditions through which a living system persists across changing circumstances.

Mechanisms therefore acquire biological significance through their organisational roles within living systems. They contribute to viability maintenance, continuity regulation, repair, adaptive reorganisation, evaluation, semiosis, and other activities that preserve organised persistence.

APS therefore does not oppose mechanistic biology. Rather, it situates mechanistic explanation within a broader organisational account of living systems. Mechanisms explain how functional activities are realised, while organisational explanation clarifies why those mechanisms together constitute the viability-oriented persistence of a living organism.

## Function and Temporal Organisation

Can a biological function be understood at a single moment in time?

Living systems exist in continual flux. Their components undergo ongoing material turnover, energetic exchange, developmental transformation, ecological interaction, and adaptive reorganisation. Yet despite this continual change, the functions through which living systems persist exhibit continuity across time rather than existing as isolated events.

This raises an important biological question. If living organisation is continuously changing, in what sense can biological functions remain the same?

APS argues that biological functions are inherently temporal. A process is functional not because of what it achieves at a single instant, but because of its contribution to maintaining or re-establishing viability-oriented organised persistence through time. Function therefore depends upon ongoing organisational continuity rather than static structural identity.

This temporal organisation is expressed through multiple, interacting processes, including continuity maintenance, developmental reconstruction, adaptive reorganisation, ecological coordination, and persistence-preserving regulation distributed across different timescales. Functional organisation therefore extends beyond individual events to encompass the dynamic organisation through which living systems preserve themselves across continual transformation.

Understanding function in this way explains why living systems can remain organisationally continuous despite continual material and environmental change. Biological functions are organised across time because the persistence they sustain is itself an ongoing organisational achievement.

Functions contribute to organised persistence by participating in temporally coordinated continuity-preserving organisation across changing conditions.

## Normativity and Viability

Why do biological functions have standards of success and failure?

One of the distinctive features of biological functions is that they can succeed or fail. Hearts may pump effectively or inadequately, immune systems may preserve viability or permit disease, developmental processes may stabilise or become disrupted, and regulatory systems may maintain continuity or contribute to organisational collapse. Biological explanation therefore depends upon real distinctions between better and worse organisational outcomes.

This raises an important philosophical question. If biology does not appeal to externally imposed purposes or conscious evaluation, where do these standards of success and failure originate?

APS argues that biological normativity emerges directly from viability-oriented organised persistence. Living systems exist under conditions in which continuity may be maintained or lost, regulation may stabilise or degrade, organisation may persist or collapse, and viability may be preserved or undermined. These organisational asymmetries are not imposed by observers; they are intrinsic features of living organisation itself.

Normativity therefore does not depend upon conscious judgement, symbolic representation, external evaluation, or independently imposed standards. It arises because living systems are organised around the continual maintenance and re-establishment of the conditions required for their own persistence. Organisational states matter differently because they contribute differently to continued viability.

Understanding normativity in this way explains why biological functions possess genuine standards of success and failure without invoking external purposes or mental evaluation. Functional organisation is normative because the organisation of life itself establishes real asymmetries between continuity-preserving and continuity-undermining conditions.

## Function and Biological Agency

How are biological functions actively maintained rather than merely possessed?

Biological functions are not static properties of living systems. They must be continually enacted, regulated, modified, and, when necessary, re-established if organised persistence is to continue. This raises an important question. If functions contribute to viability, what organises and sustains those contributions as conditions continually change?

APS answers this question through biological agency. Agency is the ongoing viability-oriented organisational activity through which living systems regulate and coordinate the processes that maintain their own persistence. Functions therefore do not simply exist within organisms; they are continually enacted through the active organisation of living systems.

This organisational activity extends across many domains of biological organisation, including metabolism, behaviour, development, ecological interaction, evaluation, semiosis, and organisational reconstruction. In each case, activity is continuously modulated relative to conditions affecting continued viability.

Agency therefore operationalises both function and normativity. Functional organisation is not passively present but continually regulated, reorganised, reinforced, compensated for, and reconstructed as living systems respond to changing internal and external conditions. Through this ongoing activity, agency preserves the organisational continuity upon which biological function depends.

Agency therefore integrates function, normativity, adaptation, evaluation, semiosis, and continuity-preserving organisation within one unified process of viability-oriented activity. It is through agency that functional organisation becomes an active and continuously maintained feature of living systems rather than a static property of biological structures.

## Function and Evaluation

How do biological functions become operationally significant to living systems?

Biological functions do more than contribute to organised persistence. Living systems must also regulate those functions continuously in response to changing internal and external conditions. This raises an important question. If biological functions establish standards of success and failure, how are those standards translated into the ongoing regulation of living activity?

APS answers this question through biological evaluation. Evaluation is the principal operational expression of biological normativity. Through evaluation, living systems continually differentiate conditions that support continued viability from those that threaten it, thereby modulating activity relative to the requirements of organised persistence.

This evaluative organisation extends across all forms of life. Living systems continually distinguish favourable from unfavourable conditions, stabilising from destabilising activities, and continuity-supporting from continuity-threatening organisational relations. Evaluation therefore enables living systems to regulate their activity in ways that preserve or re-establish the conditions required for continued viability.

Evaluation does not require conscious reflection, symbolic representation, or deliberate judgement. Even the simplest organisms modulate their activity relative to conditions affecting persistence. Biological evaluation is therefore a fundamental organisational process through which agency enacts the normativity inherent in living organisation.

Without evaluation, living systems could not regulate persistence effectively across changing conditions. Evaluation operationalises biological normativity by continuously modulating activity in relation to viability-oriented organised persistence.

## Function, Malfunction, and Breakdown

How can biological functions fail while remaining biological functions?

One of the defining features of biological functions is that they can malfunction. Hearts may pump inadequately, regulatory systems may become unstable, developmental processes may be disrupted, and repair mechanisms may fail to restore organisational integrity. Yet these processes remain recognisably biological functions even when they no longer contribute effectively to continued viability.

This raises an important explanatory question. If biological functions are defined by their contribution to organised persistence, how can they retain their identity when those contributions become impaired?

APS argues that malfunction is an organisational phenomenon rather than merely an observer's judgement. Biological functions are defined by their viability-oriented organisational role. Malfunction occurs when the organisation responsible for sustaining persistence no longer contributes adequately to that role, whether through degradation, disruption, or failure of coordination. The function remains identifiable because the organisational relation persists even though its effectiveness has been compromised.

Breakdown may therefore occur through failed repair, destabilised regulation, degraded coordination, disrupted development, or other failures of organisational integration. These failures reveal intrinsic distinctions between continuity-preserving and continuity-undermining organisational states, viable and non-viable trajectories, and effective and ineffective functional organisation.

Understanding malfunction in this way explains why biological normativity becomes especially apparent when organised persistence is threatened. Malfunction is not simply the absence of successful function or the projection of external evaluative standards. It is the organisational expression of disrupted viability within living systems themselves.

## Function and Adaptation

How do biological functions remain continuous while living systems continually change?

Living systems exist in environments that are constantly changing. Internal organisation is continually modified through development, physiological regulation, ecological interaction, and responses to environmental variation. Yet despite this continual change, living systems preserve the functional organisation required for continued viability. This raises an important biological question. If biological functions are continuously reorganised, in what sense do they remain the same functions?

APS argues that biological functions are adaptive because they are organisational rather than structurally fixed. Living systems preserve continuity not by maintaining identical components or behaviours, but by continually reorganising the activity through which viability-oriented organised persistence is sustained.

This adaptive organisation is expressed through physiological compensation, behavioural flexibility, developmental plasticity, ecological responsiveness, and continuity-preserving reconstruction. As conditions change, functional relations may shift, compensate for degraded organisation, redistribute activity, or reorganise the conditions under which persistence can be maintained. Adaptation therefore preserves organisational continuity through ongoing reorganisation rather than through static structural constancy.

Understanding function in this way explains why biological organisation remains both stable and flexible. Functional continuity does not require organisational immobility. Instead, living systems preserve themselves by continually reconstructing the organisation through which viability is maintained across changing internal and external conditions.
