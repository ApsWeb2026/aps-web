      <h2 id="why-aps-heading">
        <a href="/articles/why-aps/">Why APS?</a>
      </h2>

      <div class="article-text-block">
        <p>
          Biology has achieved extraordinary success in explaining living systems, yet some of its
          deepest conceptual questions remain unresolved. What is life? What distinguishes biological
          organisation from every other organised system? How do concepts such as evolution, function,
          agency, development, and ecology relate?
        </p>

          the organisation and persistence of living systems.

      <h2 id="contemporary-biology-heading">APS in Contemporary Biology</h2>

      <div class="article-text-block">
        <p>
          Contemporary biology succeeds through explanatory plurality. Mechanistic,
          evolutionary, developmental, ecological, informational, physiological,
          mathematical, and organisational approaches ask different questions and
          achieve different kinds of understanding.
        </p>

        <p>
          APS does not seek to replace this plurality with a single theory. It develops
          a substantive and integrative account of living organisation whose explanatory
          claims can be compared with other biological and philosophical approaches.
          Those comparisons require independently applicable methodological controls
          rather than being settled by APS architecture itself.
        </p>
      </div>

    <section class="home-section" aria-labelledby="methodology-heading">
      <h2 id="methodology-heading">Methodology and Explanation</h2>

      <div class="article-text-block">
        <p>
          Biology explains through many successful approaches, but explanations cannot
          be compared simply because they use different concepts, vocabularies, or
          theoretical frameworks. Comparative assessment begins by specifying what is
          being explained and identifying the strongest relevant alternatives.
        </p>

        <p>
          APS therefore distinguishes its substantive account of living organisation
          from the methodology used to assess explanatory claims. Conceptual coherence
          and integration matter, but coherence alone is not evidence. Proposed
          relationships must be specified, materially warranted, and assessed against
          evidence and relevant comparators. The outcome may be explanatory gain,
          complementarity, redundancy, comparator advantage, qualified gain, or no gain
          at all.
        </p>
      </div>

    <section class="home-section" aria-labelledby="organised-persistence-heading">
      <div class="article-text-block">
        <h2 id="organised-persistence-heading">Organised Persistence</h2>

        <p>
          Living systems continually establish, maintain, repair, reorganise, and transform the
          conditions that make biological activity possible.
        </p>

        <p>
          APS describes this continuing achievement as <strong>organised persistence</strong>.
          Organisation here is ongoing activity rather than a static arrangement, and persistence is
          continuity maintained through change rather than changeless endurance.
        </p>

        <p>
          Mechanistic, developmental, ecological, physiological, evolutionary, and organisational
          explanations address different aspects of biological continuity and change. APS proposes
          organised persistence as a candidate comparative explanatory target for investigating how
          their distinctive achievements relate.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <section class="home-section architecture-diagram-section" aria-labelledby="aps-framework-heading">
      <div class="article-text-block">
        <h2 id="aps-framework-heading">Agency–Process–Scale</h2>

        <p>
          APS investigates living organisation through three complementary analytic projections:
          <strong> Agency</strong>, <strong>Process</strong>, and <strong>Scale</strong>.
        </p>

        <p>
          These are not separate biological entities, independent causes, or hierarchical levels. They
          are different explanatory priorities through which the one viability-oriented,
          constraint-closed organisation of living systems can be investigated.
        </p>

        <p>
          Together they provide APS with an analytic architecture for investigating how biology’s diverse
          explanatory achievements may relate while preserving the distinctive questions addressed by
          individual biological disciplines.
        </p>
      </div>

      <div class="aps-framework-content">

> **Where This Article Fits** Having introduced APS in *What Is APS?*, this article explains why such a framework remains worth investigating. It identifies unresolved questions concerning living organisation, asks what an APS organisational perspective might contribute alongside established biological and theoretical approaches, and explains how those possible contributions should be assessed. Readers may wish to read this article immediately after *What Is APS?* before continuing to the broader orientation pathway.

## Section 1 — Introduction. Biology's Extraordinary Achievement

Modern biology has transformed our understanding of life more profoundly than perhaps any previous scientific discipline. Yet the continuing success of biology has revealed an unexpected consequence: the better we understand living systems, the more clearly we can ask what kind of phenomenon life actually is.

Over little more than a century and a half, biology has revealed the mechanisms of heredity, the dynamics of evolution, the organisation of cells, the molecular basis of development, the complexity of ecological interactions, and the biochemical processes that sustain life. Few scientific disciplines have reshaped both human knowledge and practical life so profoundly.

This achievement has been cumulative rather than dependent upon a single revolutionary discovery. Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection unified the diversity of life within a common historical framework. Genetics explained the inheritance of biological traits. Molecular biology uncovered the physical basis of genetic information and cellular function. Physiology, developmental biology, ecology, and systems biology each expanded biology's explanatory reach by revealing new dimensions of living organisation. Together, these fields have produced an extraordinarily rich understanding of how living systems function, develop, reproduce, adapt, and evolve.

The practical consequences have been equally remarkable. Modern biology underpins contemporary medicine, agriculture, biotechnology, conservation, and public health. It informs responses to infectious disease, guides ecosystem management, enables genetic diagnosis and therapy, and contributes to understanding global environmental change. The explanatory power of biology is therefore measured not only by its theoretical achievements but also by its capacity to improve human life and deepen our understanding of the living world.

Importantly, this success has never depended upon a single explanatory tradition. Biological knowledge has advanced through the interaction of multiple complementary approaches. Mechanistic explanations identify how biological phenomena occur. Evolutionary explanations account for their historical transformation. Developmental biology explains the emergence of organismal form and function throughout the life cycle. Ecology situates organisms within wider environmental relationships. Systems biology investigates the coordinated interactions through which complex biological behaviour emerges. Each perspective illuminates aspects of living systems that cannot be fully understood in isolation from the others. As biology has expanded, its explanatory traditions have become increasingly complementary rather than mutually exclusive, creating opportunities for integrative organisational frameworks alongside continued empirical investigation.

Recognising this diversity is essential. APS does not begin from the claim that modern biology has failed or that its established explanatory traditions are inadequate. On the contrary, the continuing success of modern biology provides the very foundation upon which further conceptual work becomes possible. Mature sciences often discover that their greatest achievements reveal new kinds of foundational questions. Biology is no exception.

Indeed, the history of science suggests that intellectually mature disciplines periodically return to questions lying beneath their expanding body of empirical knowledge. Progress in observation, experimentation, and theory frequently reveals new conceptual issues rather than eliminating them. Physics continues to investigate the foundations of space, time, and quantum reality despite its extraordinary predictive success. Likewise, biology continues to ask what distinguishes living systems, what constitutes biological organisation, and what kinds of explanation are required to understand life as a distinctive natural phenomenon. Such questions do not diminish the achievements of biological science; they arise precisely because those achievements have made deeper forms of understanding possible.

It is within this context that the Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework should be understood. APS was not developed in opposition to modern biology, nor as a replacement for its established theories. It emerged from the recognition that the continuing success of biological research has brought foundational questions concerning living organisation into increasingly sharp focus. Before considering how APS approaches those questions, it is therefore necessary to understand why successful sciences continue to revisit their conceptual foundations.

## Section 2 — Why Success Does Not End Inquiry

Modern biology's extraordinary achievements do not bring biological inquiry to an end. On the contrary, they make new kinds of questions possible. As knowledge accumulates, sciences often discover that progress reveals deeper conceptual issues rather than eliminating them. The history of science repeatedly shows that explanatory success and foundational inquiry develop together.

This pattern is familiar across the natural sciences. Physics has achieved remarkable predictive power while continuing to investigate the nature of space, time, gravity, and quantum reality. Chemistry successfully explains the composition and transformation of matter while still examining the conceptual foundations of chemical organisation and emergence. Mature sciences do not cease asking fundamental questions because they have become successful; they ask them because success has clarified what remains to be understood.

Biology occupies a similar position. During the past century, enormous advances have illuminated the mechanisms through which living systems develop, reproduce, adapt, communicate, regulate, and evolve. These achievements have transformed our understanding of life at every level of investigation. Yet they have also sharpened several enduring questions. What distinguishes living systems from other organised physical systems? What makes biological organisation a distinctive object of scientific explanation? What, ultimately, is biology attempting to explain?

These questions are sometimes misunderstood as attempts to replace empirical biology with abstract speculation. In fact, they arise from the opposite impulse. The more completely biological mechanisms become understood, the more clearly one can ask what those mechanisms collectively achieve as aspects of living organisation. The issue is not whether molecular biology, physiology, ecology, or evolutionary theory are successful—they demonstrably are—but whether their combined achievements point towards a broader organisational target that deserves explicit investigation.

This distinction is important because it concerns the nature of explanation rather than the accumulation of evidence. Scientific disciplines routinely distinguish between explaining individual phenomena and clarifying the conceptual framework within which those explanations acquire their coherence. Biology explains countless biological processes with extraordinary precision. A different, though complementary, question asks how those diverse explanations relate to one another as explanations of living systems.

For this reason, foundational questions should not be interpreted as signs of disciplinary weakness. They are characteristic of intellectually mature sciences. Every successful explanatory framework eventually raises questions about its own organising concepts, assumptions, and scope. In biology, questions concerning life, organisation, individuality, function, agency, information, and explanation itself remain active precisely because they concern the conceptual organisation of biological knowledge rather than unresolved empirical observations.

Theoretical biology and the philosophy of biology have long recognised the importance of these questions. They investigate not only particular biological phenomena but also the conceptual structures through which biology understands its subject matter. Their aim is not to compete with experimental biology but to clarify the explanatory architecture that allows diverse biological discoveries to form a coherent scientific understanding of life. Within these fields, discussions of organisation, autonomy, mechanism, function, emergence, and individuality reflect an ongoing effort to understand what kinds of explanation are appropriate for living systems.

It is within this continuing conversation that APS should be situated. The framework did not arise because biology lacked evidence, nor because existing theories had ceased to be productive. Rather, it emerged from the recognition that biology's continuing success has made questions about the organisation of living systems increasingly explicit. Before introducing APS itself, however, one further step is required. We must identify more precisely the nature of the explanatory challenge that these questions reveal.

## Section 3 — The Organisational Target of Biological Explanation

The continuing success of modern biology raises a question that is easily overlooked. If biology can already explain heredity, development, metabolism, physiology, behaviour, adaptation, and evolution with remarkable precision, what remains to be explained? At first sight, the answer might appear to be simply "more biology"—more mechanisms, more genes, more pathways, and more interactions. Yet many of the continuing discussions within theoretical biology suggest that the remaining challenge is of a different kind. It concerns not merely the accumulation of additional knowledge, but the clarification of what biological explanation is attempting to explain when living organisation itself becomes the target.

Every biological discipline investigates some aspect of living systems. Molecular biology explains cellular processes through molecular interactions. Physiology explains the coordinated functioning of organisms. Evolutionary biology explains the historical transformation of populations. Developmental biology explains the emergence and maintenance of organised form throughout the life cycle. Ecology explains the relationships between organisms and their environments. Each contributes indispensable knowledge. Their explanatory success is beyond question.

What is striking, however, is that many successful biological explanations concern systems whose activities, development, regulation, reproduction, ecological relations, and evolutionary histories are aspects of living organisation. This raises a further question: when such explanations bear upon the organisation of living systems, can their relationships be investigated without reducing them to a single explanatory vocabulary or assuming that they share one explanandum?

This question does not ask biology to abandon mechanisms in favour of organisational explanation. Mechanistic explanation remains indispensable because living systems are materially realised through physical and chemical processes. Nor does it seek a master theory capable of replacing evolutionary, developmental, physiological, ecological, or other biological explanations. Different explananda can legitimately require different explanatory approaches.

The issue is therefore conditional rather than universal. Where living organisation itself, or its continuity through change, is the explanatory target, it may be useful to ask how different findings bear upon that target. Where the explananda differ, no such integration should be assumed.

Organisation is already a major subject within theoretical biology. General systems theory, relational biology, autopoiesis, autonomy theory, constraint-based approaches, and organisational accounts of function have all developed substantive ways of investigating it. APS enters this established field rather than claiming to introduce organisation as a previously missing object of inquiry.

Its particular proposal is to investigate whether viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and organised persistence clarify some biological explananda, expose useful relationships among established explanations, or generate assessable questions that existing approaches do not already supply. Whether they do so must be established rather than inferred from APS architecture.

The Agency–Process–Scale framework therefore enters theoretical biology as one candidate organisational perspective. It asks what explanatory work its formulation of living organisation can perform and where, if anywhere, that work differs consequentially from the strongest relevant alternatives.

## Section 4 — Why APS Was Developed

The Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework was developed in response to the explanatory question identified in the preceding section. It does not begin by proposing a new biological mechanism, a new evolutionary theory, or a new account of molecular function. Instead, it begins by asking whether living organisation can be more clearly specified as an explanatory target and what follows if it can.

APS therefore begins from an organisational proposal rather than a new biological mechanism. It asks whether living systems can usefully be investigated as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation whose activity maintains and re-establishes conditions of persistence. This proposal does not imply that the diverse phenomena studied throughout biology are merely expressions of one explanatory process. It provides a candidate organisational target against which relevant relationships among those phenomena can be investigated.

This perspective distinguishes two complementary tasks that can arise within biological explanation. The first is to explain particular biological phenomena through the appropriate empirical sciences, including molecular biology, physiology, developmental biology, ecology, and evolutionary biology. The second, where living organisation itself is explanatorily relevant, is to ask how those explanations bear upon the organisation and continuity of the living system. APS addresses this second task without assuming that it is required for every biological explanandum.

This orientation also explains why APS is organised around three complementary analytic projections:

> **Agency**, **Process**, and **Scale**. These are neither separate components of reality nor independent causal forces. They are complementary analytic projections of one and the same living organisation.

> **Agency** asks what living systems do. It investigates the viability-oriented activity through which organisms maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

> **Process** asks how continuity is maintained despite ongoing material turnover, developmental change, and environmental variation. It examines the dynamic organisation through which organised persistence is sustained.

> **Scale** asks across what spatial and temporal extents, and through what materially implemented relations among them, persistence is organised. It does not treat those extents as explanatory hierarchies.

Taken together, these analytic projections provide a framework for investigating living organisation while preserving the specialised explanatory methods of biology. Evolutionary theory continues to explain historical transformation. Molecular biology continues to investigate biochemical mechanisms. Ecology continues to explain organism–environment relationships. APS asks how such explanations bear upon viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation where that relationship is relevant to the explanandum.

The framework therefore makes a deliberately bounded claim. It does not propose that Agency, Process, and Scale answer every biological question. Rather, they provide complementary analytic projections through which a particular class of questions concerning living organisation can be formulated and investigated. Whether doing so provides explanatory value cannot be determined by the architecture alone.

Seen in this way, APS belongs within the continuing development of theoretical biology. It emerges from biology's existing achievements, engages with established questions concerning organisation and explanation, and seeks to provide a systematic way of investigating them. Its purpose is not to replace biology's explanatory traditions or to assume that they form parts of a single APS explanation, but to determine where an organisational perspective clarifies their relationships, generates useful questions, or identifies additional explanatory work.

## Section 5 — What APS May Contribute

The reason to investigate APS is not that biology lacks successful explanations of mechanisms, evolution, development, regulation, ecology, autonomy, or organisation. Nor is APS justified simply because established biological results can be redescribed using the language of Agency, Process, Scale, viability, or organised persistence.

APS may make several different kinds of contribution, and they should not be conflated.

First, APS may make a **conceptual contribution**. It may help specify biological explananda, distinguish questions that are easily conflated, or clarify relationships among concepts concerned with living organisation. Its distinction between Life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and organised persistence as the continuity of that organisation through change is one such proposal. Conceptual clarification can be scientifically useful, but it does not by itself establish that APS explains a biological phenomenon better than existing approaches.

Second, APS may make a **methodological contribution**. It can provide ways of asking how biological explanations relate without assuming that different vocabularies represent competing theories or that conceptual integration constitutes explanatory gain. APS therefore emphasises specification of the explanandum, comparison with the strongest relevant alternatives, identification of materially implemented dependencies, and explicit allowance for redundancy, complementarity, comparator advantage, or null results. It can also generate questions about living organisation that are open to further investigation.

Third, APS may in some cases make a **biological explanatory contribution**. This is the strongest claim and carries the greatest evidential burden. Such a contribution is established only where, for a specified explanandum, APS identifies explanatory relationships, dependencies, consequences, or discriminating expectations not already supplied by the strongest relevant established explanation.

These three forms of contribution do not entail one another. A useful concept need not produce a better method. A useful method need not produce new biological explanation. And conceptual coherence, integrative scope, accessibility, or the ability to redescribe many biological phenomena in APS terminology does not establish explanatory superiority.

This distinction also clarifies how APS should be tested. **Illustrating APS is not testing APS.** Showing that a biological system can be described in terms of viability, agency, process, scale, constraints, or organised persistence demonstrates applicability of the vocabulary. Testing a biologically consequential APS claim requires a specified explanandum, an adequate comparator, an identifiable difference in explanatory dependency or expectation, and some condition under which the APS claim could fail.

APS should therefore be understood as both an organisational proposal and a research programme for determining what, if anything, follows explanatorily from that proposal. Its value may lie in clarification, methodological organisation, hypothesis generation, or demonstrated biological explanatory gain. Where none of these survives comparison with established alternatives, APS adds no corresponding scientific contribution.

The wider APS corpus develops these questions across life, biological agency, organised persistence, constraint closure, cognition, individuality, development, evolution, social organisation, morality, and biological explanation. Each domain must carry its own evidential burden rather than deriving its scientific status merely from membership in the APS architecture.

## Concluding Reflection

Modern biology continues to expand our understanding of living systems through increasingly sophisticated empirical investigation. At the same time, its continuing success leaves legitimate questions concerning how living organisation should be conceptualised, investigated, and related to the many successful explanations biology already provides. APS represents one attempt to address those questions through an organisational framework centred on viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

Whether APS proves scientifically valuable cannot be settled by the coherence or breadth of its architecture. Its contribution must be assessed by whether it clarifies important explananda, improves explanatory comparison, generates productive and assessable biological questions, or—where stronger claims are made—identifies explanatory dependencies or consequences not already supplied by the strongest relevant alternatives. Where it merely redescribes established biology in APS terminology, no additional explanatory gain has been demonstrated.

The purpose of this article is therefore not to establish that biology needs APS in advance. It is to explain why APS poses questions worth investigating and to make clear what the framework would have to contribute for that investigation to succeed.

## Introduction

Life is remarkable not simply because living systems exist, but because they persist.

Organisms grow, repair themselves, reproduce, develop, regulate their internal conditions, respond to changing environments, and survive continual challenges. Across these transformations they can maintain continuity even though their components, activities, environments, and developmental states continually change.

This persistence is so familiar that it is easy to overlook how much it requires. A living system does not merely remain present through time. It continually maintains and re-establishes conditions under which its organisation can continue. Resources must be acquired, damage repaired, internal activity regulated, development coordinated, and relations with changing environments managed.

APS begins from the organisation that makes such continuity possible.

Within APS, **Life is defined as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. This definition does not identify life with persistence itself. It identifies a form of organisation in which conditions relevant to continued functioning matter to the system's activities, while organisational constraints participate in maintaining one another and the organisation they collectively sustain.

Living organisation is therefore dynamic rather than static. Its components and states may change while organisational continuity is maintained or re-established. APS calls this continuity through change **organised persistence**.

The distinction is important. **Life identifies the kind of organisation under investigation; organised persistence identifies the continuity of that living organisation through change.** Biological agency concerns the viability-oriented organisational activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained.

APS investigates this organisation through the complementary analytic projections of **Agency, Process, and Scale**. These are not components of life, stages in a sequence, or hierarchical levels. They distinguish questions about what living systems do, how continuity is maintained despite change, and across what spatial and temporal extents and relations persistence is organised.

This provides the starting point for the wider APS research programme. It does not require development, ecology, evolution, cognition, repair, resilience, or other biological domains to be redescribed as forms of organised persistence. Instead, APS asks how their findings bear upon living organisation where that relationship is explanatorily relevant, and whether doing so provides explanatory gain beyond the strongest relevant alternatives.

[[box:what-biological-explanation-explains]]

## Why APS Exists

APS was developed in response to a recurring problem within biological explanation.

Modern biology has generated extraordinary knowledge about genes, molecules, cells, physiology, behaviour, development, evolution, ecology, cognition, and many other domains. These fields employ different concepts, methods, and explanatory approaches because they address different biological questions. Their diversity is a source of explanatory strength. Yet it also raises a broader question: how do these different explanatory achievements contribute to our understanding of living organisation?

APS addresses this question by asking:

> What is characteristic of living systems as living systems, and what follows for biological explanation?

APS proposes that **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. This does not identify a particular molecule, mechanism, behaviour, or structural feature as the defining feature of life. It identifies a form of organisation whose continued functioning depends upon activities and constraints that maintain and re-establish conditions of viability.

That organisational account also raises the problem of organised persistence introduced above:

> How is the continuity of living organisation maintained despite change?

When that continuity is the explanatory target, APS investigates how living organisation is maintained or re-established across changing conditions.

This gives APS potentially broad scope without giving it universal explanatory authority. Living organisation provides a domain-wide subject of biological investigation because it concerns what is characteristic of living systems across the biological domain. But not every biological question has living organisation or organised persistence as its explanandum. Particular phenomena may be adequately explained through mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental, ecological, mathematical, informational, or other approaches.

APS therefore does not assume that an organisational account is automatically preferable. Where APS and another approach address the same explanatory target, any claimed advantage must be established through comparison with the strongest relevant alternative. Where the targets differ, the explanations cannot simply be ranked as though they were competing answers to the same question.

APS provides a conceptual framework for investigating relationships among biological findings where those relationships bear upon living organisation or organised persistence. Development, ecology, evolution, cognition, and other domains retain their own questions, methods, explanatory targets, and resources. APS asks how their findings may contribute to an organisational account where that relationship is explanatorily relevant, without requiring their absorption into a single explanatory system.

APS therefore neither replaces existing biology nor seeks to unify biological science under a single mechanism, theory, or obligatory method. Its ambition is to develop and test an organisational account of living systems and to determine, through controlled comparison, where that account provides additional explanatory value.

## The Central Idea of APS

The central framework commitment of APS is straightforward:

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

This is a canonical APS commitment, not a claim that the definition has already been shown to be uniquely correct, necessary and sufficient, or superior to the strongest relevant alternatives.

This definition identifies organisation, rather than persistence alone, as the starting point. Living systems are organised in ways that continually maintain and re-establish conditions required for their continued functioning. Some conditions support that continuation while others undermine it. APS describes this difference as **viability**.

Living organisation is also **constraint-closed**. The processes of life are channelled and regulated by constraints that depend, directly or indirectly, upon the operation of other constraints within the same organisation. The organisation thereby participates in maintaining the conditions through which its own activity remains possible. Constraint closure and viability identify different features of this organisation: closure concerns the mutual maintenance of organisational constraints, while viability concerns the conditions under which the organisation can continue.

Such organisation cannot remain static. Living systems regulate physiological conditions, acquire resources, repair disruptions, develop, modify their activity, and respond to changing circumstances. APS calls this **biological agency**: viability-oriented organisational activity. Agency is therefore not an additional capacity possessed by an otherwise complete living system. It is the present activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained.

Because this activity continues through material turnover, environmental change, development, and disturbance, living systems can maintain organisational continuity while changing. APS calls this continuity through change **organised persistence**. Persistence is therefore not passive endurance or resistance to transformation. Living systems persist through ongoing organisational activity that maintains or re-establishes conditions compatible with continued functioning. Organised persistence is retained as an explanandum-organising and integrative concept; this analytic utility does not by itself demonstrate additional substantive biology, and the tested planarian case did not establish a new biological dependency beyond mature regeneration explanations.

These distinctions allow related concepts to do different explanatory work. **Life** identifies the form of organisation under investigation. **Viability** distinguishes conditions relevant to its continuation. **Constraint closure** concerns the organisation through which constraints participate in maintaining one another. **Agency** identifies viability-oriented organisational activity. **Organised persistence** identifies the continuity of living organisation through change.

Development, adaptation, repair, and resilience can then be investigated without being defined as forms of persistence. Development concerns organised change across the life course; adaptation concerns relations between living systems and changing conditions; repair concerns responses to disruption; and resilience concerns the maintenance or recovery of organisation under perturbation. Where relevant, APS asks how such phenomena bear upon viability and organised persistence.

APS consequently approaches living organisation organisationally without treating organisational explanation as automatically superior to other forms of biological explanation. Analysis of components and mechanisms remains indispensable and may provide sufficient explanations for particular biological questions. Where living organisation or organised persistence is the explanandum, APS asks whether identifying the relevant activities, processes, constraints, and relationships provides explanatory gain beyond the strongest established alternatives addressing that same target.

Living systems are therefore neither adequately characterised as static arrangements of parts nor simply redescribed as things that persist. APS investigates the organisation through which their continued functioning becomes possible.

[[box:description-is-not-explanation]]

## Agency, Process, and Scale

Once living organisation is understood as viability-oriented and constraint-closed, APS asks how that organisation can be investigated without dividing the living system into independent explanatory parts.

It does so through three complementary **analytic projections**:

- **Agency** — what living systems do

- **Process** — how continuity is maintained despite change

- **Scale** — across what spatial and temporal extents, and through what relations among them, persistence is organised

An analytic projection is a distinct explanatory way of investigating one materially realised living organisation. It brings particular relations into explanatory focus without implying that the organisation itself is divided into corresponding independent components, causes, levels, or domains. The distinction among Agency, Process, and Scale therefore belongs to explanatory analysis, while the activities, processes, constraints, dependencies, and spatial and temporal relations investigated through them are biologically real. The philosophical status of this distinction is developed in [Living Reality and Biological Explanation — What Does APS Mean by an Analytic Projection?](/articles/what-does-aps-mean-by-an-analytic-projection/).

This distinction matters because the same biological phenomenon can raise different questions. A response to environmental change may be investigated in terms of what the living system does, how organisational continuity is maintained while conditions change, or which spatial and temporal extents and relations are relevant to explaining the response. These questions differ without requiring three different biological realities.

APS stands for **Agency–Process–Scale**. The name therefore identifies three complementary ways of investigating one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation, not three ingredients from which living organisation is constructed.

Whether an APS analysis contributes explanatory value must then be established relative to the particular explanandum and the strongest relevant alternatives.

### Agency

Agency concerns what living systems do.

Living systems regulate metabolism, acquire and use resources, repair damage, coordinate development, modify behaviour, and respond to changing internal and external conditions. These activities differ enormously among living systems, but they can matter to whether living organisation continues to function.

APS defines **biological agency as viability-oriented organisational activity**. Agency in this sense does not require intelligence, consciousness, planning, or reflective thought. It identifies the activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained in relation to conditions relevant to viability.

Agency is therefore not an additional property possessed by an already complete living system. Nor is it a separate cause acting upon Process or Scale. It is an analytic projection through which the active character of living organisation becomes an explanatory focus.

### Process

Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change.

Living systems undergo continual material turnover, physiological adjustment, development, repair, behavioural change, and interaction with their environments. Their organisation is therefore not a static arrangement preserved by preventing change. Continuity must be maintained or re-established while change occurs.

The Process projection directs attention to this dynamic organisation of continuity. It asks how activities and transformations unfold in ways that maintain, modify, disrupt, or restore living organisation through time.

Process should therefore not be understood simply as the passage of time, nor as a second ingredient added to Agency. Agency and Process are different analytic projections of the same living organisation: Agency foregrounds viability-oriented activity; Process foregrounds the organisation of continuity through change.

### Scale

Biological phenomena occur across physically real spatial and temporal extents. Molecular interactions, cellular activities, organismal physiology, developmental change, organism–environment relations, ecological processes, and evolutionary histories do not all occupy the same spatial or temporal ranges.

APS distinguishes this physical fact from **Scale as an analytic projection**.

Scale asks which spatial and temporal extents, and which materially implemented relations among them, are relevant to the explanatory problem. It does not arrange biology into a hierarchy of levels or assume that larger or smaller scales possess greater causal or explanatory importance.

Relations across spatial and temporal extents must themselves be biologically implemented. A wider-scale pattern does not supersede local causation merely because it occurs over a larger extent. Where processes organised across different extents influence one another, that influence occurs through material interactions and constraints that alter the conditions under which local activities take place.

Scale therefore concerns the spatial and temporal organisation relevant to persistence, while remaining distinct from physical scale itself. Its analytic usefulness is retained, but the tested planarian case did not demonstrate APS-specific substantive gain or novel cross-scale causation.

Taken together, the three projections keep different explanatory questions distinct: what the living system does, how continuity is maintained through change, and which spatial and temporal extents and materially implemented relations are relevant to the phenomenon being explained.

They are different questions asked of one living organisation.

## Continuity Across Biology

The significance of Agency, Process, and Scale becomes clearer when one considers the extraordinary diversity of biological inquiry.

Physiology, development, ecology, evolution, cognition, adaptation, resilience, diagnosis, and social organisation address different phenomena. They employ different methods, ask different questions, and operate with different explanatory resources. APS does not treat this diversity as a deficiency to be overcome. Different biological questions can legitimately require different kinds of explanation.

Living systems nevertheless provide contexts in which many of these phenomena are related. Physiological regulation can affect development; development can alter later capacities and interactions; ecological conditions can modify organismal activity; evolutionary history can help explain present biological organisation. Such relationships are biologically real, but their existence does not make the corresponding disciplines different expressions of a single explanatory process.

APS therefore asks a more limited comparative question:

> How, where relevant, do different biological explanations bear upon living organisation and its continuity through change?

Organised persistence can provide a candidate target for such comparison. Physiology may explain regulatory processes involved in maintaining functioning; development may explain organised transformation across a life course; ecology may explain organism–environment relations; APS formulates evolution as historical transformation of viability-oriented, persistence-sustaining organisation across generations; and APS investigates cognition in terms of the organisation of biological significance across time. These explanations retain their own explananda and standards of adequacy. Their possible relationship to organised persistence is something to be investigated, not assumed. The evolutionary formulation remains integrative: it did not demonstrate additional explanatory power on the tested developmental–evolutionary target.

Agency, Process, and Scale can assist that investigation by directing attention to complementary aspects of the living organisation involved without requiring every biological explanation to employ all three projections. Agency asks what living systems do in relation to viability. Process asks how organisational continuity is maintained despite change. Scale asks which spatial and temporal extents, and which materially implemented relations among them, are relevant to the explanatory problem. The projections do not require every biological explanation to answer all three questions.

APS therefore provides an analytic framework for investigating relationships among explanatory achievements where those relationships bear upon living organisation. Its claims can be assessed using independently applicable comparative controls: comparison must begin with the strongest established explanations of the phenomena concerned and preserve their explanatory targets, evidence, and scope. These controls are not substantive evidence for APS biology or a uniquely APS invention.

The resulting integration is conditional rather than compulsory. Where comparison through living organisation or organised persistence reveals relationships, distinctions, unresolved questions, or additional explanatory dependencies, APS may provide explanatory gain. Where it merely redescribes independently successful explanations in APS terminology, no such gain has been established.

## Cognition in APS

Cognition provides an important test of the distinction between living organisation in general and capacities that arise only within some living systems. APS therefore investigates how cognition can arise within living organisation without identifying cognition with life itself.

Life and cognition are not equivalent. All living systems exhibit biological agency, but biological agency does not by itself establish cognition. APS therefore begins with a distinction between the viability-oriented activity characteristic of life and the additional organisation required for cognitive activity.

A central step is **Biological Evaluation**: the process through which agency generates significance. Conditions encountered by a living system can differ in their consequences for viability, and organised activity can respond to those differences. APS calls the relationally real product of this evaluation **biological significance**.

But evaluation alone is not cognition. Immediate responses, fixed regulatory relations, and viability-sensitive activity can occur without the additional organisation APS associates with cognitive processes. Cognition requires biological significance to be integrated and organised across time so that activity can be modulated in relation to viability-relevant possibilities extending beyond the immediately present condition.

The resulting pathway can be stated in simplified form:

> **Biological Agency → Biological Evaluation → Significance → Integration → Cognition**

This is an explanatory pathway, not a sequence of separate substances, modules, hierarchical levels, or inevitable evolutionary stages. Each distinction identifies additional organisational requirements relevant to the explanatory question being asked.

APS therefore treats cognition as continuous with living organisation without collapsing cognition into life. Biological agency provides the organisational context within which cognition can arise, while cognition involves forms of integrated, temporally extended organisation that are not required for life as such.

This section states APS propositions rather than comparator-validated conclusions. Biological Evaluation and significance remain conditional and untested as sources of additional explanatory gain, while the APS minimal-cognition discriminator remains an open, testable hypothesis.

More elaborate forms of cognitive organisation may contribute to mind, selfhood, reflective agency, and meaning. APS investigates the biological-organisational pathway toward these phenomena while maintaining a boundary between explaining their organisational conditions and claiming to explain subjective experience itself.

## What APS Proposes to Add

APS does not seek to replace existing biology.

Its strongest demonstrated contribution is bounded: it provides analytic and methodological organisation for formulating explanatory targets, relating concepts, and testing proposed gains. Its substantive biological formulations remain APS commitments or hypotheses with proposition-specific evidential status.

APS proposes that **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. From this starting point, it asks how living organisation is enacted through biological agency, how continuity is maintained despite change, and across what spatial and temporal extents and materially implemented relations persistence is organised.

This changes the questions that become available for investigation without determining their answers in advance. APS can ask how conditions relevant to viability differ, how organisational constraints are mutually maintained, how living organisation continues through material and developmental change, and how relations across spatial and temporal extents alter the conditions under which local activities occur.

Organised persistence is important within this account, but APS does not begin by assuming that persistence is the explanatory target of every biological phenomenon. Organised persistence identifies the continuity of living organisation through change. Where that continuity is relevant to an explanandum, APS asks what activities, processes, constraints, and relationships contribute to maintaining or re-establishing it.

This perspective can bring organisational questions into relation with development, ecology, evolution, repair, resilience, cognition, and other biological phenomena. Those domains retain their own explanatory targets, methods, evidence, and standards of adequacy. APS contributes only where relating their findings to living organisation clarifies something that the strongest relevant established explanations do not already provide.

[[box:what-aps-changes-and-what-it-does-not]]

APS therefore changes neither the subject matter of biology nor the validity of its established explanatory approaches. It proposes an organisational account of living systems. What that account contributes must be assessed using independently applicable comparative methods and the strongest relevant established explanations.

Its success depends not on the breadth of phenomena that can be described in APS terminology, but on demonstrated explanatory gain.

## What APS Is Not

Because APS proposes a distinctive account of living organisation, it is useful to clarify what it does not claim.

APS is not a rejection of mechanisms. Mechanistic explanations remain indispensable and may be sufficient for many biological questions. Where living organisation or organised persistence is the explanatory target, APS may ask a further question: how do particular mechanisms contribute to the maintenance, modification, disruption, or restoration of viable organisation? This does not make organisational explanation intrinsically superior to mechanistic explanation. Whether it adds explanatory value depends upon the explanandum and comparison with the strongest relevant established alternative.

APS is not a form of simple holism, nor is it a revival of classical organicism. Its explanatory position is organisational. Living systems contain components and mechanisms, but their biological significance can also depend upon the organisation within which their activities occur. APS investigates such organisational relations without treating the whole as an independent cause or assigning automatic explanatory priority to a larger scale.

APS also does not identify life with cognition, computation, intelligence, consciousness, or information processing. These phenomena may occur within particular living systems and may require explanation in their own right, but none defines life as such. Life does not require cognition; cognition presupposes life.

Nor does APS treat Agency, Process, and Scale as three constituents whose interaction produces life or organised persistence. They are complementary analytic projections of one living organisation: Agency concerns what living systems do; Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change; and Scale concerns across what spatial and temporal extents, and through what materially implemented relations among them, persistence is organised.

These distinctions belong to APS's organisational account of life. They provide claims to be investigated and compared, not an alternative biological substance, mechanism, or explanatory level.

For readers interested in these distinctions, APS provides detailed clarification articles including:

- **Why APS Is Not Holism**

- **Why APS Is Not Organicism**

- **Why Life Is Not Computation**

- **Why Life Is Not Intelligence**

- **Why Life Is Not Active Inference**

Together these articles locate APS more precisely within the wider landscape of contemporary biological explanation.

## Conclusion

APS is an integrative analytic framework for theoretical biology. It investigates living organisation and assesses what an organisational perspective contributes to biological explanation.

Its central proposal is:

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

Living systems continually maintain and re-establish conditions under which that organisation can continue. They regulate activity, acquire resources, repair disruptions, develop, respond to changing environments, and reorganise under changing circumstances. Their components and states may change substantially while continuity of living organisation is maintained or re-established.

APS calls this continuity through change **organised persistence**.

Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections through which this living organisation can be investigated. Agency concerns what living systems do in relation to viability. Process concerns how organisational continuity is maintained despite change. Scale concerns across what spatial and temporal extents, and through what materially implemented relations among them, persistence is organised. These projections distinguish explanatory questions; they are not components, levels, stages, or independent causes.

APS can bring this organisational perspective to questions concerning development, ecology, evolution, cognition, adaptation, repair, resilience, and other biological phenomena. It does not assume that these domains share a single explanandum or that their established explanations must be reconstructed in APS terms. Their relationship to living organisation must be investigated where it is explanatorily relevant.

The same approach informs APS work on function, normativity, purpose, individuality, emergence, and biological explanation. Function and normativity remain open and untested APS propositions; purpose remains a conditional, untested formulation. These concepts are not made explanatory merely by locating them within APS. Their proposed relationships to living organisation must be specified, supported, and, where alternatives address the same target, comparatively assessed.

APS therefore does not seek to reduce biology to a single mechanism, component, theory, or explanatory vocabulary. It proposes an organisational account of Life and asks what explanatory work that account can do.

Its success ultimately depends upon that test: **whether APS reveals explanatory relationships, distinctions, dependencies, or questions that the strongest relevant established explanations do not already provide.**

APS investigates how living organisation works—and what explaining that organisation adds to biology.

This article introduces APS through a sequence of related but distinct explanatory questions.

It distinguishes the organisational proposal APS makes, the analytic projections through which that proposal can be investigated, and the comparative assessment required to determine what explanatory contribution APS actually provides.

## Introduction

APS contains many articles, concepts, pathways, and explanatory domains.

At first glance, this variety can appear overwhelming. The framework includes discussions of persistence, viability, development, ecology, evolution, cognition, adaptation, diagnosis, resilience, repair, normativity, social organisation, and many other topics. Readers often understand individual concepts before they fully understand how those concepts connect. This article is designed to address that problem.

Its purpose is not to introduce new concepts, nor does it attempt to explain every component of the framework in detail. Instead, it serves as a guide to the organisation of APS, helping readers understand the major pathways, domains, and routes through which the framework can be explored.

The organising question is simple:

> Many APS pathways are organised around a recurring explanatory question.

> How do living systems maintain viable continuity through time despite continual transformation?

APS asks how development bears upon continuity through transformation, how ecology bears upon organism–environment relationships, how evolution bears upon historical transformation across generations, how cognition may involve the organisation of biological significance across time, how diagnosis bears upon disruption and recovery, and how social organisation may distribute continuity-supporting activity across collective forms of coordination. These are APS routes of inquiry, not a claim that all domains share one explanandum or have already been comparatively validated through APS.

What initially appears to be a collection of separate biological topics can therefore be navigated through a shared organisational question where that question is relevant. APS is not merely a catalogue of concepts. It is an integrative analytic framework for investigating living organisation and organised persistence without requiring every biological domain to be reconstructed within one unified account.

This gives APS potentially broad scope without giving it universal explanatory authority. Living organisation provides APS with a domain-wide subject of investigation because it concerns what is characteristic of living systems across the biological domain. APS nevertheless does not require every biological question to be reformulated as a question about organised persistence. Particular phenomena may be adequately explained through mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental, ecological, mathematical, informational, or other approaches. Any claim that an APS formulation adds explanatory value must be established against the strongest relevant alternatives addressing the same target.

## The Framework at a Glance

The easiest way to understand APS is to begin with a central organising problem:

> Living systems persist.

Yet they do not persist by remaining unchanged. Organisms grow, develop, adapt, repair damage, interact with environments, reproduce, learn, age, and participate in increasingly complex ecological and social systems. Continuity must therefore be preserved despite continual transformation.

APS treats this achievement as an important explanatory problem in developing its organisational account of living systems. Many, but not all, pathways within the framework can be related to this starting point where continuity through change is relevant to their explanatory target.

Persistence becomes important because living systems must maintain continuity through time. Viability becomes important because continuity must remain compatible with continued existence. Adaptation becomes important because continuity must remain possible under changing conditions, while repair and resilience become important because continuity must survive disruption and recover from perturbation. Development becomes important because continuity must be maintained through transformation rather than despite it.

The framework therefore provides a connected navigational structure. Its domains can be examined for how they bear upon organised persistence while retaining their own questions, methods and explanatory resources. Their relationship is conditional rather than a cumulative hierarchy of necessary layers.

Readers sometimes ask whether APS is primarily about development, ecology, evolution, cognition, diagnosis, or social organisation. APS investigates all of these domains where their findings bear upon living organisation, but it does not assume that they share a single explanandum or that Agency, Process and Scale are equally required in each case. The framework uses a common organisational lens to formulate possible relationships that must then be assessed target by target.

## How APS Is Organised

At the centre of the framework lies a simple explanatory structure.

APS investigates living systems through three complementary analytic projections:

- agency;

- process;

- and scale.

These are not independent theories, nor are they separate branches of the framework. They are three analytic projections through which APS investigates a single viability-oriented organisation: 

**Agency concerns what living systems do.** Organisms regulate conditions affecting their continued viability by acquiring resources, responding to challenges, repairing damage, reorganising activity, and contributing actively to their own persistence. Agency therefore identifies the activity through which viability-oriented organisation is enacted and sustained.

**Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change.** Living systems persist through ongoing activity rather than static existence. Development, metabolism, adaptation, learning, ecological interaction, repair, and reproduction involve processes through which biological organisation is maintained, reorganised, or transformed through time. Process therefore identifies the dynamic organisation through which continuity is maintained despite continual change.

**Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.** Molecular, cellular, physiological, organismal, ecological, developmental, and evolutionary processes operate across different extents and can influence one another through materially implemented interactions and constraints. These are not independent hierarchical levels, and no scale possesses intrinsic causal or explanatory priority. Scale therefore identifies the spatial and temporal extents across which organised persistence is realised.

Together, Agency, Process, and Scale function as complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. They provide a bounded analytic architecture for organising inquiry, not a demonstrated general Explanatory Grammar or a universally required method.

This question supplements rather than supersedes established explanations and earns explanatory standing only through target-matched comparison.

## Organised Persistence

Organised persistence is an important explanandum-organising concept within APS.

Many APS pathways can be investigated in relation to how continuity is maintained or re-established despite continual transformation. Living systems continuously change: components turn over, environments fluctuate, development reorganises structures and capacities, behaviour adapts to changing circumstances, and populations evolve across generations. Yet organisational continuity can remain.

APS uses organised persistence to identify this continuity of living organisation through change. Persistence therefore does not mean static endurance. Within APS it concerns the maintenance or re-establishment of organisational continuity under changing conditions.

This distinction can clarify how biological organisation is investigated. Organisms remain viable not because they avoid change, but because ongoing activities can maintain or re-establish conditions compatible with continued functioning. APS asks whether development, adaptation, repair, resilience, ecological interaction, social coordination and evolutionary transformation bear upon this continuity where the relationship is explanatorily relevant.

Organised persistence currently has demonstrated explanandum-organising and integrative utility, not demonstrated general substantive explanatory gain. In the tested planarian case, it did not establish a new biological dependency beyond mature regeneration explanations.

The remainder of the framework explores the different ways in which organised persistence becomes possible across biological domains.

Its concepts, pathways, and research programmes build upon one another and become easier to understand when encountered in a broadly coherent sequence.

A useful starting point is What Is APS?. That article introduces the central question that motivates the framework and explains why APS begins with the problem of how living systems maintain viable continuity through time despite continual transformation. Readers seeking an initial overview of the framework should begin there.

The next step is Understanding APS. Whereas What Is APS? introduces the framework, the present article explains how the framework is organised. Its purpose is to help readers navigate the major pathways, domains, and conceptual routes that make up APS and to identify where particular questions and research programmes belong within the wider organisation of the framework.

Readers wishing to understand how APS develops and presents its account should then continue to Naturalising Life and How APS Explains Life. Naturalising Life examines how APS develops and stabilises its biological concepts, while How APS Explains Life introduces the organisational account through which APS interprets living systems in terms of viability-oriented organisation and organised persistence. Independently applicable comparative methodology remains separate from substantive evidence for that account.

Readers may then continue to The Core Structure of APS. That article presents the framework's integrated conceptual architecture for navigation and inquiry. It proposes relationships among agency, biological evaluation, significance, integration, cognition, mind, selfhood, reflective agency and meaning while preserving that conceptual coherence is not by itself evidence of substantive biological gain.

From there, readers may continue to APS Architecture Map and How APS Concepts Fit Together, which provide visual and conceptual navigation aids, or proceed directly into specialised domains such as development, ecology, evolution, cognition, diagnosis, resilience, social organisation, and the broader life-to-mind research programme developed in How Did Matter Become Aware of Itself?. APS therefore supports many routes of exploration, but this orientation pathway provides the clearest introduction to the framework as a whole.

## Major Pathways Within APS

Once the central idea of organised persistence is understood, the overall structure of APS becomes much easier to navigate.

The framework is organised into several major pathways. These pathways are navigational groupings rather than biological modules or obligatory components. Each can be examined in relation to a recurring APS question:

> How does viable continuity remain possible through time?

Together they provide a progressively expanding map of how APS relates organised persistence to multiple biological domains. The map does not establish that all domains share the same explanatory target or that their APS formulations have equal evidential status.

Readers can treat these pathways as candidate continuity architectures considered across different spatial and temporal extents and under different conditions. Each retains its own concepts and specialised questions; any contribution to understanding biological persistence must be established rather than inferred from inclusion in the framework.

## Persistence and Temporality

The persistence pathway provides a principal organising route through the framework.

APS begins from the observation that living systems maintain continuity despite continual transformation. Understanding this achievement requires careful attention to persistence, viability, temporality, adaptation, resilience, repair, and continuity itself. This pathway therefore develops the conceptual language through which organised persistence can be understood.

Questions explored within this pathway include:

- What does biological persistence mean?

- How does continuity differ from simple duration?

- Why is viability central to living systems?

- How is continuity maintained under changing conditions?

- What role do repair and resilience play in persistence?

Together these concepts organise APS questions about continuity through change. Persistence identifies the explanatory problem, temporality concerns how continuity is organised through time, and viability concerns conditions relevant to continued functioning. Their role within APS does not make them foundationally indispensable to every biological explanation.

Key articles include:

- **Viability — The Organising Principle of Biological Persistence**

- **Temporal Organisation and Organised Persistence**

- **Scale, Time, and Persistence**

## Development and Organised Persistence

The developmental pathway explores how continuity is maintained through transformation.

This is an important theme within APS because living systems do not simply persist through time. They persist while continually changing. Growth, repair, learning, adaptation, maturation, ageing and ecological responsiveness involve processes unfolding across the lifespan.

APS therefore treats development as more than an early phase of life and investigates it as a candidate continuity architecture. This is an APS formulation whose explanatory contribution must be assessed against the strongest developmental explanations addressing the same target.

The developmental pathway examines:

- morphogenesis;

- developmental integration;

- plasticity;

- regulation;

- resilience;

- ageing;

- developmental niches;

- and developmental continuity.

Together these topics allow APS to investigate how organisms remain viable despite continual transformation. The developmental organisation of life expresses an APS proposal:

> Living systems do not persist despite development.

> They persist through development.

Development can therefore connect persistence as an organising concept with questions about ongoing biological transformation. The tested developmental–evolutionary comparison did not demonstrate additional explanatory gain from the organised-persistence formulation on its locked target.

Key articles include:

## Ecology and Evolution

The ecological and evolutionary pathways extend continuity beyond individual organisms.

Once continuity is understood developmentally, a broader question emerges: how does persistence remain possible within larger ecological and historical systems?

Ecology addresses organism–environment relations relevant to continuity. Organisms do not persist in isolation. Their viability depends upon resources, environmental relationships, ecological niches, symbiotic interactions and multiple forms of coupling with the wider world. APS asks how established ecological explanations bear upon the continuity of living organisation without requiring ecological targets to be redescribed as organised persistence.

Evolution addresses historical transformation across generations. APS formulates evolution as the historical transformation of viability-oriented, persistence-sustaining organisation, while retaining this as an integrative formulation rather than demonstrated general explanatory gain.

APS investigates possible relationships between ecology and evolution where their different explanatory targets bear upon living organisation. Ecology may illuminate present organism–environment relations, evolution historical transformation across generations, and adaptation their differing relations to changing conditions. These formulations do not collapse the domains into a single explanation.

Together these pathways provide APS with routes for investigating how questions of continuity may extend beyond individual organisms across ecological and evolutionary timescales. The tested developmental–evolutionary target yielded no additional APS explanatory gain, and that local null does not determine the status of the framework as a whole.

Key articles include:

- **Ecology as Organised Persistence Across Scales**

- **Evolution as the Historical Transformation of Organised Persistence**

- **Adaptation — How Living Systems Sustain Themselves Through Change**

## Cognition and Semiosis

The cognition pathway develops one of the open research areas of APS.

Approaches differ over how cognition relates to the rest of biology. APS asks whether and how cognition contributes to the organisation of activity across time within living systems.

The APS pathway begins with Biological Evaluation: the proposed process through which agency generates significance. Conditions can differ in their consequences for viability, but the APS account of Biological Evaluation and significance remains conditional and untested as a source of additional explanatory gain.

From evaluation, the framework develops a connected sequence of concepts:

- semiosis;

- meaning;

- information;

- representation;

- cognition;

- intelligence;

- and consciousness.

Rather than treating these topics as isolated philosophical or psychological problems, APS proposes to situate them within a broader account of viability-oriented organisation. The resulting pathway is a conceptual research architecture, not a comparator-validated sequence or an inevitable hierarchy of cognitive development.

The cognition pathway therefore investigates how activity may become increasingly sensitive, flexible and responsive to changing conditions. The APS minimal-cognition discriminator remains an open, testable hypothesis; the pathway does not yet establish general explanatory gain over the strongest relevant alternatives.

## Diagnosis and Resilience

The diagnostic pathway examines what happens when continuity becomes threatened, disrupted, destabilised, or lost.

Many aspects of biological organisation become most visible when systems fail. When continuity is preserved successfully, much of the organisation responsible for that preservation remains hidden. Repair systems often go unnoticed until damage occurs. Regulatory systems become most apparent when conditions change. Resilience becomes visible when continuity is challenged.

APS therefore treats perturbation as potentially explanatorily valuable because disruptions can reveal organisational relationships involved in persistence.

This insight leads to a pathway centred on:

- perturbation;

- malfunction;

- continuity destabilisation;

- resilience;

- compensation;

- recovery;

- repair;

- regeneration;

- and continuity failure.

Within APS, diagnosis is consequently proposed as more than defect identification: it can be investigated as a form of continuity analysis.

The central question is not simply:

> What has gone wrong?

but:

> How has continuity been disrupted, and what organisational capacities remain available for recovery?

This perspective links diagnosis to broader APS questions. Repair may disclose continuity-preserving organisation, resilience continuity-recovering organisation, and failure dependencies that successful functioning can conceal.

Diagnosis therefore provides a candidate route into the organisational architecture of living systems. The tested planarian case did not establish an APS-specific new dependency beyond mature regeneration explanations, and no validated APS-specific diagnostic or biosignature programme is currently claimed.

Key articles include:

- **Diagnosis as Continuity Analysis**

- **How to Diagnose a Biological System**

## Social Organisation

The social pathway extends continuity beyond the level of individual organisms.

Many forms of life persist not only through physiological, developmental, ecological, and cognitive organisation, but also through collective forms of coordination. Communication, cooperation, cultural transmission, institutions, shared practices, and distributed systems of knowledge all contribute to maintaining continuity across groups and populations.

APS therefore investigates social organisation as a candidate continuity architecture through which viability may be supported.

The social pathway explores how persistence may become distributed across interacting agents and how continuity may be supported through collective organisation rather than solely through individual capacities. This perspective provides an APS route into questions concerning cooperation, social learning, cultural inheritance and collective resilience without indiscriminately attributing biological agency to every collective, institution or ecological whole.

Social organisation does not replace individual agency. APS asks how materially implemented collective relations may alter the conditions under which agents maintain continuity across changing circumstances.

## How APS Organises Biological Explanation

At this point, the overall structure of the framework becomes visible.

The persistence pathway organises questions about continuity through change. The developmental pathway investigates continuity through transformation. The ecological and evolutionary pathways address different organism–environment and historical questions. The cognitive pathway develops APS proposals concerning Biological Evaluation, significance and temporally organised activity. The diagnostic pathway examines disruption and recovery, while the social pathway considers materially implemented collective coordination. These pathways have different targets and evidential statuses even where APS relates them to organised persistence.

Each pathway provides a different APS perspective on organised persistence. Their relationships form an integrated architecture for navigation and inquiry around a recurring question:

> How do living systems maintain viable continuity through time?

APS does not reject mechanistic, developmental, ecological, evolutionary, informational or other forms of biological explanation. These approaches can address different questions using their own explanatory resources and may provide sufficient explanations for their respective targets.

Where their questions bear upon living organisation and organised persistence, APS provides an integrative analytic perspective through which their contributions can be related without requiring their reduction to, or reconstruction through, APS. Any comparison should use independently applicable, target-matched controls and the strongest relevant alternatives; that methodology is not substantive evidence for APS biology or a uniquely APS invention.

APS integrates explanatory contributions where such integration is warranted by the question being addressed; it does not assume that explanatory plurality must always be unified.

[[box:the-organisational-grammar-of-aps]]

The framework provides a bounded analytic vocabulary for investigating how diverse biological phenomena may relate to viability-oriented organised persistence where that relationship is relevant to the explanatory question. Its coherence and navigational usefulness do not by themselves demonstrate substantive explanatory gain.

## What APS Changes

APS proposes a distinctive way of organising explanation when living organisation and organised persistence are the central targets.

Rather than beginning only with isolated components, it can begin with continuity. Rather than treating biological domains as interchangeable, it asks where their different findings bear upon living organisation. Rather than treating persistence as static endurance, it investigates continuity through organised transformation.

This shift changes the questions APS makes available. Persistence is treated dynamically rather than statically; stability can be investigated as maintained continuity rather than immobility; development can be examined for its relation to continuity through transformation; ecology for organism–environment conditions; diagnosis for disruption and recovery; cognition for the organisation of biological significance across time; and social organisation for materially implemented collective relations. These remain APS formulations of differing evidential status, not collectively comparator-validated biology.

The result is an integrative analytic framework that can formulate possible relationships among phenomena often studied separately. Whether any such relationship provides additional explanatory value remains target-dependent.

APS therefore organises its general account of living systems around:

- organised persistence;

- viability;

- continuity;

- temporality;

- development;

- adaptation;

- ecology;

- cognition;

- repair;

- resilience;

- social organisation;

- and continuity-preserving organisation across scale and time.

## Conclusion

Understanding APS ultimately means understanding the organisational logic that connects its many domains.

At first glance, the framework may appear to contain diverse and unrelated topics. Persistence, development, ecology, evolution, cognition, diagnosis, resilience, repair and social organisation can seem like separate areas of inquiry. APS proposes that they can be related where each bears upon a recurring organisational challenge:

> How does viable continuity remain possible through time?

The framework therefore possesses a coherent analytic architecture organised around living organisation, organised persistence, viability, Agency, Process and Scale. Within APS, development is investigated in relation to transformation, ecology to organism–environment relations, evolution to historical transformation, cognition to the organisation of biological significance across time, diagnosis to disruption and recovery, and social organisation to collective coordination. Understanding APS therefore means knowing both how these pathways are connected for navigation and where their explanatory targets and evidential statuses remain distinct.

Together these pathways organise APS inquiry into how living systems maintain or re-establish conditions of viability despite continual change. APS is therefore not merely a collection of concepts. It is an integrative analytic framework for theoretical biology whose substantive proposals remain subject to proposition-specific evidence and target-matched comparison.

## Introduction

Every mature scientific framework eventually reaches a point where individual concepts are no longer the primary source of explanatory interest. New ideas have been introduced, relationships among them have been clarified, and a growing body of work has accumulated around a common set of concerns. At this stage a deeper question begins to emerge:

Why do these concepts belong together?

APS has now reached this point.

The framework contains discussions of life, viability, persistence, continuity, development, adaptation, ecology, evolution, cognition, mind, selfhood, meaning, diagnosis, resilience, repair, normativity, agency, process, and scale. Each concept plays an important role. Yet APS is not simply a collection of biological topics gathered under a common label, nor is it a catalogue of independent theoretical claims.

It is an explanatory architecture.

The purpose of this article is to explain that architecture.

What Is APS? introduces the central intuition underlying the framework. How APS Explains Life describes the framework's basic explanatory orientation. Understanding APS surveys its major conceptual domains. This article addresses a different question:

>Why does the framework possess the structure that it does?

The answer begins with a simple biological observation.

Living systems persist.

Yet they do not persist by remaining unchanged. They grow, develop, repair, adapt, learn, age, reproduce, and continually interact with changing environments. Their continuity depends not upon stability but upon the successful organisation of change itself.

APS begins with this phenomenon.

Everything else in the framework emerges from the attempt to understand how such continuity remains possible.

[[box:the-aps-dependency-architecture]]

# Foundations

## The Fundamental Problem of Life

Biological explanation is often organised around particular domains. Development explains transformation. Ecology explains organism–environment relations. Evolution explains historical change. Cognition explains adaptive responsiveness. Physiology explains functional regulation. Diagnosis explains disruption, recovery, and failure.

Each domain appears to address a different aspect of life.

APS agrees that these domains are genuinely distinct. However, it also asks why they repeatedly converge upon similar organisational themes. Why do concepts such as adaptation, repair, resilience, cognition, evaluation, and development recur throughout biology despite their apparent diversity? What underlying problem makes these different forms of organisation necessary?

APS proposes that these phenomena emerge because living systems face a common challenge:

> The maintenance of viable continuity through time.

This challenge provides the architectural centre of the framework.

Development matters because continuity must survive transformation. Adaptation matters because continuity must remain possible under changing conditions. Repair matters because continuity must recover from disruption. Resilience matters because continuity must withstand perturbation. Ecology matters because continuity depends upon environmental relations. Evolution matters because continuity extends across generations. Cognition matters because living systems must respond differentially to conditions affecting continuity.

Viewed separately, these domains can appear unrelated. Viewed through the lens of organised persistence, they reveal different aspects of the same explanatory problem.

APS therefore begins not with a particular biological mechanism or level of organisation but with a question:

> How do living systems maintain viable continuity despite continual transformation?

The remainder of the framework unfolds from the attempt to answer that question.

## Viability and Organised Persistence

The central organising concept of APS is viability-oriented organised persistence.

This idea is easy to misunderstand because persistence is often associated with permanence, stability, or resistance to change. Living systems persist differently. An organism remains recognisably itself despite continual transformation. Development alters its structure, learning modifies its behaviour, repair reorganises damaged tissues, ecological interactions reshape opportunities and constraints, and evolution transforms populations across generations.

Change is not an interruption of biological existence. It is its normal condition.

The explanatory challenge therefore becomes understanding how continuity remains possible despite continual transformation.

APS addresses this challenge through the concept of organised persistence. Organised persistence refers to the active maintenance of viable continuity across time. Persistence is not mere duration. It is the continual regeneration of the organisational conditions required for continued existence.

This distinction changes how living systems are understood. Organisms are not best described as objects that happen to undergo change. They are continuity-preserving organisations whose persistence depends upon the successful organisation of change itself.

Yet persistence alone is not enough.

Many non-living systems persist. What distinguishes living persistence is that continuity must remain compatible with continued existence. Resources must be obtained, damage must be repaired, development must remain coordinated, environmental relations must remain sufficiently supportive, and activity must remain responsive to changing conditions.

This requirement introduces viability.

Viability identifies the conditions under which persistence succeeds or fails. Living systems do not merely continue through time. They continue under organisational constraints that determine whether continuity remains possible. Some conditions support persistence. Others undermine it. Some transformations contribute to continued existence. Others threaten it.

Persistence therefore acquires biological significance because continuity is not guaranteed. Living systems exist under circumstances where persistence can succeed or fail.

Within APS, viability provides the organisational context for propositions about normativity, regulation, adaptation, repair, resilience, and evaluation. These propositions have differing evidential statuses and are not collectively validated by their place in the framework.

Together, these concepts specify the central explanatory problem addressed by APS. Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Organised persistence identifies the problem of how that organisation maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its continuity despite change.

This is a principal explanatory target of APS and the organising target of its general account of living organisation. It does not follow that every biological inquiry must take organised persistence as its target.

Everything that follows in the framework emerges from this foundation.

## Constraint Closure and Organisational Continuity

If viability-oriented organised persistence is the phenomenon APS seeks to explain, a further question immediately arises:

> How is continuity maintained despite continual transformation?

APS addresses this question through the concept of constraint closure.

Living systems are organised through networks of constraints that regulate biological activity. These constraints channel processes, stabilise organisation, and contribute to continuity across time. Yet the constraints themselves are not fixed. They are continually regenerated through the activities of the system. The organisation that sustains continuity is simultaneously sustained by continuity-preserving activity.

Constraint closure therefore helps explain how organised persistence remains possible despite continual material turnover and organisational change. It provides an account of the reciprocal organisation through which living systems maintain themselves through time.

Constraint closure is integral to the APS definition of life, but it is not a separate explanatory centre. APS does not define life as constraint closure alone, nor does it derive the entire architecture from closure alone. Rather, closure matters within APS because it contributes to the maintenance of viable continuity.

Constraint closure is therefore integral to the APS account of living organisation, but it should not be understood as a separate level beneath organised persistence or as something derived from it. APS defines life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Organised persistence identifies how the continuity of that organisation becomes an explanatory problem through time.

The relationship is therefore organisational rather than hierarchical. Constraint closure helps specify how living organisation maintains and regenerates conditions required for its persistence, while organised persistence directs attention to the continuity of that organisation despite material and organisational change.

Constraint closure is therefore best understood as one important organisational solution to the broader challenge of maintaining viable continuity through time.

This ordering helps clarify why APS investigates development, adaptation, repair, resilience, cognition, ecology, evolution, and social organisation within one conceptual architecture. Their inclusion organises inquiry; it does not establish that they share one demonstrated substantive explanation. Closure helps formulate continuity maintenance within APS, while the architecture organises questions arising from persistence.

At this point a deeper question becomes unavoidable: What kind of organisation actively maintains continuity relative to viability?

The answer leads to the analytic architecture through which APS organises inquiry into this problem.

## Explanatory Architecture
### The Agency–Process–Scale Analytic Interface

APS organises its general account of living organisation through three complementary analytic projections:

- **Agency**
- **Process**
- **Scale**

Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

They are not three components from which living systems are constructed, three independent causes, three domains of reality, or three hierarchical levels. Nor does APS propose that every legitimate biological explanation must employ them.

Instead, each projection directs attention to a different explanatory question concerning the same integrated living organisation:

- **Agency** concerns what living systems do.
- **Process** concerns how continuity is maintained despite change.
- **Scale** concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

The distinctions are analytic; the organisation and activities they disclose are biologically real.

Together these relations constitute the Agency–Process–Scale analytic interface through which APS structures inquiry into living organisation and organised persistence. This is a bounded analytic architecture, not a demonstrated general Explanatory Grammar for biology.

This analytic interface belongs specifically to APS. Its organisational usefulness does not establish that Agency, Process, and Scale are obligatory categories of biological explanation generally or that they provide additional substantive explanatory gain. Particular biological questions may be adequately addressed through mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental, ecological, mathematical, informational, or other explanatory approaches.

The current claim is bounded: when living organisation itself is the explanatory target, Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary, target-sensitive ways of organising investigation of how living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their persistence. Whether this organisation adds substantive explanatory capacity requires target-matched comparison; it is not established by the architecture alone.

### Why Agency, Process, and Scale Are Analytic Projections

The terminology matters because Agency, Process, and Scale can easily be mistaken for three things that exist independently within living systems. APS does not make that claim.

A living system does not contain Agency, Process, and Scale as separable components. Nor are they distinct causal forces or levels whose combination produces life. They identify complementary ways of investigating one integrated biological organisation.

APS therefore describes them as **analytic projections**.

**Analytic** indicates that the distinction is made for purposes of investigation and explanation. Living organisation can be examined by asking what the system does, how its continuity is maintained through change, and where that persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

**Projection** indicates that these questions are directed toward the same organisation. Each brings particular relations into explanatory focus without implying that the biological organisation itself is divided into corresponding independent parts.

The term is deliberately preferred to several plausible alternatives.

**Components** would suggest that Agency, Process, and Scale are separable constituents from which living organisation is assembled. They are not.

**Levels** would suggest a hierarchical organisation in which one projection occupies a position above or below another. APS rejects this interpretation.

**Dimensions** can be useful informally, but may suggest independently constituted axes whose combination defines biological reality.

**Categories** would make the distinction primarily classificatory and would understate its explanatory role.

**Perspectives** captures part of the intended meaning but can suggest that Agency, Process, and Scale are optional viewpoints imposed by an observer. APS makes a stronger claim: the distinctions are analytical, but they disclose real features and relations of living organisation.

“Analytic projection” therefore preserves two commitments simultaneously:

> **The distinctions are analytic; the organisation they disclose is biologically real.**

The philosophical status of this distinction is examined in greater depth in [Living Reality and Biological Explanation — What Does APS Mean by an Analytic Projection?](/articles/what-does-aps-mean-by-an-analytic-projection/), which explains how an investigator-selected explanatory differentiation can remain materially and empirically constrained without treating Agency, Process, and Scale as independent divisions of biological reality.

Agency, Process, and Scale consequently belong together without being independent things. Agency concerns the viability-oriented organisational activity of living systems. Process concerns the dynamic organisation through which continuity is maintained despite change. Scale concerns the spatial and temporal extents across which persistence is organised.

Their relationship is therefore neither additive nor hierarchical. They are complementary analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

This terminology also establishes an important limit on APS. Because Agency, Process, and Scale form an analytic interface through which APS investigates living organisation, it does not follow that every biological explanation must be reconstructed through them or that all three are equally useful for every target. Their relevance depends upon the explanatory target, and any claim that this interface provides a preferable account requires target-matched comparative demonstration rather than assertion.

## Agency — What Living Systems Do

Agency concerns what living systems do.

The compact APS definition is:

> **Biological Agency is viability-oriented organisational activity.**

Within the APS account, living systems contribute actively to maintaining and re-establishing conditions required for their persistence. This definition is a current APS commitment; its stronger constitutive application to all life has not been comparatively demonstrated. Cells regulate metabolism, organisms acquire resources, physiological systems maintain organisation, repair responds to damage, and activity can be reorganised as circumstances change.

Agency in this sense does not require intelligence, consciousness, intention, planning, representation, or reflective thought. These capacities may occur in some living systems, but they are not constitutive of biological agency.

Agency is therefore not an additional property layered onto otherwise complete biological organisation. It identifies the viability-oriented activity through which that organisation is enacted and sustained.

As an analytic projection within APS, Agency brings this active character of living organisation into explanatory focus.

## Process — How Continuity Is Maintained Despite Change

Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change.

Living organisation exists through ongoing activity rather than static structure. Metabolism, development, repair, adaptation, learning, reproduction, and ecological interaction unfold through time while continually altering the material and relational conditions of persistence.

APS therefore treats organisation as activity rather than as a snapshot of structure.

Persistence does not require the absence of transformation. Living systems persist through organised transformation: components are replaced, relations change, damage is repaired, developmental states succeed one another, and activity is reorganised while sufficient continuity is maintained.

Process is therefore not merely temporal succession. It identifies the dynamic organisation through which living systems maintain and re-establish continuity despite change.

As an analytic projection within APS, Process brings this temporal and transformational character of living organisation into explanatory focus.

Wider organisational relations do not supersede local causation; they modify the organisational context within which local causal processes operate.

## Scale — Where Persistence Is Organised

Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

Living organisation is not confined to a single spatial or temporal extent. Molecular interactions, cellular organisation, physiological regulation, organismal activity, developmental trajectories, ecological relations, and evolutionary histories occur across different extents while remaining materially related.

APS therefore does not treat Scale as a hierarchy of biological levels. Nor does a wider scale constitute a separate causal agency acting upon processes at a smaller scale.

Cross-scale influence is materially implemented. Organisational relations at wider extents alter the conditions and constraints under which more local processes occur, while local processes materially contribute to the organisation sustained across wider extents. No scale possesses intrinsic causal or explanatory priority merely by virtue of its extent.

Scale consequently identifies neither size alone nor a hierarchy of biological reality. It directs attention to the spatial and temporal organisation of persistence and to the materially implemented relations connecting processes across those extents.

As an analytic projection within APS, Scale brings this spatial and temporal organisation of persistence into explanatory focus.

## Why These Concepts Belong Together

Agency, Process, and Scale belong together because they disclose complementary aspects of one living organisation.

Their relationship is not sequential. Agency does not produce Process, Process does not produce Scale, and the three do not combine to produce life. Nor does APS infer their biological reality from the coherence of the conceptual scheme.

Instead, APS begins with viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and asks complementary questions about it.

What does this organisation do in maintaining and re-establishing conditions of persistence? **Agency.**

How is its continuity maintained despite continual material and organisational change? **Process.**

Where is that persistence organised across spatial and temporal extents? **Scale.**

The three projections therefore belong together because the same living organisation is simultaneously active, processual, and organised across spatial and temporal extent.

Their distinctions are analytic. Their referent is one biologically real organisation.

This is why APS treats Agency, Process, and Scale as a structured analytic interface rather than as three independent parts of an APS ontology.

## The Agency–Process–Scale Analytic Interface

The relationship can be summarised compactly:

- **Agency** — what living systems do.
- **Process** — how continuity is maintained despite change.
- **Scale** — where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

Together they constitute the target-sensitive analytic interface through which APS structures inquiry into living organisation and organised persistence.

This interface identifies how APS analyses living organisation. Its clarity and coherence are analytic and implementation virtues; they do not by themselves establish substantive biological truth or explanatory gain. The framework also proposes relationships among its wider concepts. These are represented in the APS dependency architecture as Dependency Hypotheses requiring independent assessment.

## The APS Dependency Architecture

APS contains a series of proposed relationships extending from living organisation through biological evaluation, significance, integration, cognition, mind, selfhood, reflective agency, and meaning.

These relationships constitute the APS dependency architecture.

The architecture should not be read as establishing the relationships merely by placing concepts in sequence. Each connection represents a **Dependency Hypothesis**: a proposed relationship whose relata, material basis, alternatives, evidential support, limitations, and failure conditions must be independently specified and assessed.

The architecture therefore functions as a structured research programme rather than as evidence for its own claims.

Its principal pathway is represented as:

**Life → Agency → Biological Evaluation → Significance → Integration → Cognition → Mind → Selfhood → Reflective Agency → Meaning**

The arrows identify proposed dependencies. They do not by themselves establish causation, chronology, hierarchy, reduction, or evidential warrant.

In the sections that follow, terms such as *depends upon*, *enables*, *generates*, and *creates the conditions for* should be understood as statements of APS Dependency Hypotheses unless the relevant relationship has been independently established. Their position within the architecture does not itself provide evidential warrant.

## From Agency to Meaning

The Agency–Process–Scale interface supplies the analytic projections through which
APS investigates living organisation. The dependency architecture proposes a
series of relationships through which APS investigates increasingly
sophisticated forms of biological organisation.

The concepts that follow are therefore connected through a structured series
of Dependency Hypotheses. Their sequence expresses the relationships proposed
by APS; it does not by itself establish that those dependencies obtain.

The pathway can be summarised as:

**Life → Agency → Biological Evaluation → Significance → Integration →
Cognition → Mind → Selfhood → Reflective Agency → Meaning**

Each arrow represents a Dependency Hypothesis requiring independent
specification and assessment.

For readability, the sections below sometimes state these proposed relations
directly—for example, that one form of organisation enables, generates, or
depends upon another. Such formulations state APS Dependency Hypotheses; they
do not indicate that the relationship has been established by its place in
the architecture.

The remainder of this section explains the proposed relationships in turn.

## Biological Evaluation

Agency regulates continuity relative to viability. Yet viability introduces an unavoidable asymmetry into biological organisation.

Some conditions contribute to persistence. Others undermine it. Some opportunities support continuity. Others threaten it. Some environmental changes require response. Others are largely irrelevant.

Living systems therefore do not encounter all conditions equally. They distinguish among them. APS refers to this organisational capacity as biological evaluation.

Biological evaluation is the process through which living systems distinguish conditions according to their significance for viability, functioning, and organised persistence. Evaluation does not require conscious judgement, symbolic reasoning, or reflective thought. It emerges whenever organisational activity becomes differentially responsive to conditions affecting continuity.

Within the APS dependency architecture, evaluation marks a proposed transition. Biological Evaluation remains a conditional, untested APS proposition rather than a comparator-validated result.

Living systems no longer merely regulate continuity: they regulate continuity relative to distinctions that matter.

APS proposes that Agency enables Biological Evaluation and that viability-relative asymmetry supplies its organisational basis. This dependency remains open to independent specification and testing.

## Significance

Evaluation alone is not sufficient. The distinction of conditions according to viability immediately introduces another phenomenon. Some conditions now matter.

APS refers to this organisational consequence as significance.

Significance explains how differences become relevant to a living system. A condition is significant when it bears upon viability-oriented organised persistence. Significance is therefore not imposed from outside the system, nor is it a purely subjective interpretation. It emerges through the relationship between living organisation and the conditions affecting its continued existence.

Significance answers a question that evaluation alone cannot answer:

> What exactly is it that living systems distinguish?

They distinguish conditions that matter. APS proposes that significance emerges from Biological Evaluation: Biological Evaluation generates significance. This dependency is conditional and untested, and its statement here does not establish residual explanatory gain.

Within the APS architecture, this proposed transition supplies a route for investigating cognition, mind, selfhood, and meaning. It does not establish those later dependencies or make the sequence foundationally indispensable.

## Integration

Significance explains what matters. Yet significance does not exist as isolated fragments.

Living systems must coordinate multiple sources of significance simultaneously. Information from different domains must be brought into functional relation. Activities must be organised relative to one another. Short-term and long-term concerns must remain sufficiently coherent to support continued viability.

APS refers to this organisational requirement as integration. Integration explains how significance becomes organised into functional unity.

Without integration, significance remains fragmented. With integration, multiple forms of significance become coordinated within a coherent organisational system. Integration therefore performs a crucial architectural role.

APS proposes that Biological Evaluation generates significance and that Integration organises significance. Both are dependency formulations requiring independent evidential assessment.

The transition from significance to cognition becomes possible because significance no longer exists merely as isolated distinctions. It becomes organised within increasingly coherent patterns of activity.

## Cognition

APS hypothesises that, once significance becomes integrated, it can become organised across time. Within this open research pathway, APS understands cognition as the organisation of biological significance across time. The relevant cognition discriminator remains a testable hypothesis, not a demonstrated general result.

This formulation differs from approaches that restrict cognition to brains, nervous systems, symbolic reasoning, or conscious thought. APS begins from the more fundamental organisational problem confronting living systems:

How can significance remain available to guide continuity under changing conditions?

Cognition addresses this problem by allowing significance to persist, accumulate, and influence future activity. Memory, anticipation, learning, adaptive responsiveness, and context-sensitive regulation all emerge through increasingly sophisticated forms of cognitive organisation.

Cognition therefore extends significance beyond the immediate present.

- Biological evaluation generates significance.

- Integration organises significance.

- Cognition organises significance across time.

The architecture now begins to approach questions traditionally associated with mind.

## Mind

Within the APS pathway, cognition allows significance to remain organised through time and mind marks a proposed further organisational achievement.

APS understands mind as the integrated organisation of evaluative cognitive processes through which an agent maintains a coherent relationship with itself and its world.

Mind therefore does not replace cognition. It builds upon it.

Cognition explains the temporal organisation of significance. Mind explains the coherent organisation of significance within an ongoing self–world relationship.

This distinction is important.

Many forms of cognition may remain relatively local, task-specific, or distributed. Mind refers to the emergence of a more coherent organisational perspective through which significance becomes integrated into an enduring pattern of engagement with the world.

APS proposes that mind establishes coherent self–world organisation.

## Selfhood

Coherent self–world organisation introduces a further challenge.

> How is such organisation maintained through continual transformation?

Living systems develop, learn, adapt, age, and reorganise themselves. Yet many retain a recognisable continuity despite these changes.

APS addresses this phenomenon through the concept of selfhood. Selfhood refers to the persistence of coherent self–world organisation through time. If mind explains coherence, selfhood explains continuity.

The distinction mirrors a recurring theme throughout APS.

Continuity does not require stasis. Persistence occurs through transformation.

Selfhood therefore represents the stabilisation of coherent self–world organisation across changing circumstances and timescales.

APS proposes that mind establishes coherence and that selfhood sustains coherence through change. This dependency remains open to independent assessment.

## Reflective Agency

Within the APS pathway, selfhood is proposed to create conditions for another transition: agency can become directed toward itself.

APS refers to this capacity as reflective agency.

Reflective agency emerges when agents become capable of evaluating, interpreting, and reorganising aspects of their own activity. The significance structures guiding behaviour become available for examination, modification, and deliberate redirection.

Reflective agency therefore introduces a new form of organisational flexibility.

Agency no longer operates solely in relation to external conditions. It can also become concerned with its own organisation, commitments, goals, values, and patterns of activity.

The architecture thus becomes increasingly recursive. Agency regulates continuity. Reflective agency regulates the regulation of continuity.

This transition prepares the way for meaning in its fullest sense.

## Meaning

Meaning represents the culmination of the Matter-to-Mind pathway within APS.

Throughout the preceding sections, significance has been progressively organised, integrated, stabilised, and transformed. What began as distinctions relevant to viability has become increasingly coherent, temporally organised, self-related, and reflective.

APS proposes that meaning emerges through this process.

APS understands meaning as organised significance.

This formulation preserves continuity with the earlier stages of the architecture while recognising the distinctive organisational achievements of later stages. Meaning does not appear suddenly at the reflective level, nor is it entirely absent from simpler forms of life. Its roots are already present wherever significance affects organised persistence.

Yet meaning becomes increasingly sophisticated as significance becomes integrated through cognition, mind, selfhood, and reflective agency. Meaning therefore occupies a distinctive position within APS.

It is both rooted in biological organisation and capable of becoming reflective, interpretive, symbolic, ethical, cultural, and existential.

The architecture reaches its culmination here because meaning represents the most highly organised form of significance within the framework.

[[box:the-aps-dependency-architecture]]

The sequence is intended to investigate a pathway from agency to meaning without introducing a separate explanatory system. APS proposes that the pathway can be related to viability-oriented organised persistence; the relationship is not established by conceptual continuity alone.

APS therefore investigates meaning without departing from biology, asking whether increasingly sophisticated forms of organisation can be shown to emerge within the biological world. This remains a research programme rather than a demonstrated collective result.

## Meaning, Value, and Ethics

Meaning is not the end of the APS architecture. It is a major organisational achievement within the Matter-to-Mind pathway, but it also creates the conditions under which new forms of evaluative organisation become possible.

Once significance becomes organised as meaning, some meanings acquire relative stability within agency and become values. In human societies, values become increasingly organised through communication, culture, institutions, and collective life. This gives rise to shared evaluation, social norms, moral evaluation, morality, and ethics.

APS therefore proposes a second major architecture related to the Matter-to-Mind pathway. The Evaluation-to-Ethics Architecture investigates how what matters might become progressively organised into value, social normativity, moral concern, and reflective ethical inquiry.

# Meaning and Extension

The dependency architecture provisionally culminates in meaning. APS also investigates whether related organisational questions can illuminate development, ecology, evolution, resilience, and social organisation. Understanding these proposed extensions requires clarifying the architecture of meaning itself.

## The Architecture of Meaning

The APS dependency architecture culminates in meaning. Yet meaning remains one of the most easily misunderstood concepts within the framework.

Many discussions of meaning begin with human concerns such as language, symbolism, purpose, value, identity, culture, or existential reflection. These are undoubtedly important forms of meaning, but APS argues that they do not represent the beginning of the story. They represent highly developed expressions of organisational processes whose roots extend much deeper into biological life.

APS therefore approaches meaning from the opposite direction. Rather than beginning with symbolic thought and asking how meaning enters biology, it begins with living organisation and asks how increasingly sophisticated forms of meaning emerge within the biological world.

This approach requires a distinction between two related but different forms of meaning: biological meaning and reflective meaning.

These are not separate kinds of reality. They occupy different positions within the same organisational architecture.

Understanding their relationship helps clarify how APS connects life, cognition, mind, selfhood, and meaning without reducing one to the other.

## Biological Meaning

Meaning begins long before reflective thought.

The roots of meaning emerge wherever significance influences the organisation of persistence.

Living systems continuously distinguish conditions according to their consequences for viability. Nutrient gradients, toxins, opportunities, threats, environmental changes, physiological conditions, and ecological relationships all acquire significance because they affect the possibility of continued existence.

Significance therefore introduces the earliest form of meaning within the architecture. This does not imply consciousness, symbolic representation, or interpretation. It simply means that conditions have consequences that matter to the organisation of persistence.

APS refers to this organisational phenomenon as biological meaning.

Biological meaning arises whenever significance becomes incorporated into the regulation of viability-oriented activity. The world is no longer organisationally neutral. Some conditions matter because they contribute to persistence. Others matter because they threaten it.

Meaning therefore begins wherever significance becomes functionally relevant to organised continuity.

This formulation allows APS to speak meaningfully about biological organisation without projecting human mental categories onto simpler forms of life. Biological meaning does not require minds. It requires significance.

Meaning begins because significance matters.

## Reflective Meaning

Biological meaning provides the foundation for more sophisticated forms of meaning, but it does not exhaust them. As significance becomes increasingly integrated through cognition, mind, selfhood, and reflective agency, it becomes available for interpretation, deliberation, evaluation, and revision.

Meaning can now become a topic of concern in its own right.

Living systems capable of reflective agency can ask questions about goals, values, commitments, identities, purposes, futures, responsibilities, and possibilities. Significance becomes organised not merely through biological regulation but through interpretation and self-direction.

APS refers to this organisational achievement as reflective meaning.

Reflective meaning remains continuous with biological meaning because it emerges from the same underlying architecture. Yet it introduces forms of organisation unavailable to simpler systems.

Meaning can now be:

- interpreted;
- communicated;
- negotiated;
- revised;
- symbolically represented;
- culturally transmitted;
- ethically evaluated;
- existentially questioned.

Reflective meaning therefore represents not the abandonment of biological organisation but one of its most sophisticated expressions.

The pathway from biological meaning to reflective meaning is not a transition from biology to something non-biological. It is a transition from simpler to more integrated forms of significance.

## Meaning and Significance

The distinction between significance and meaning plays a central role within APS. The two concepts are closely related but not identical.

> Significance explains what matters.

> Meaning explains how what matters becomes organised.

This distinction helps resolve several recurring confusions.

Biological evaluation generates significance because living systems distinguish conditions according to viability. Significance therefore emerges whenever conditions become relevant to organised persistence.

Meaning emerges when significance becomes organised within increasingly coherent patterns of activity.

The relationship can be summarised as follows:

Significance and Meaning

> Significance = What Matters

> Meaning = Organised Significance

This formulation captures both continuity and difference.

Meaning depends upon significance. Without significance, nothing matters.

[[box:significance-and-meaning]]

Without meaning, significance remains insufficiently organised to support the more sophisticated forms of cognition, mind, selfhood, and reflective agency characteristic of complex living systems.

The distinction also clarifies why meaning occupies two positions within the broader architecture. Its roots appear early because significance already introduces biological meaning.

Its fullest expression appears later because reflective meaning requires cognition, mind, selfhood, and reflective agency.

Meaning therefore possesses both biological depth and reflective sophistication.

## Extending the Architecture

The APS dependency architecture proposes how increasingly sophisticated forms of organisation may be related to viability-oriented organised persistence. APS also investigates the relevance of this architecture to other biological and social targets.

Development, ecology, evolution, diagnosis, resilience, and social organisation all address different expressions of the same underlying challenge: maintaining viable continuity through change.

These domains can therefore be investigated as extension domains of APS where
their explanatory questions bear upon living organisation and organised
persistence. Their inclusion does not imply that their specialist explanations
must be reconstructed through APS or that they instantiate the same
explanatory architecture in every respect.

## Development

Development is often understood as the process through which organisms change across their life histories. APS agrees with this characterisation but places it within a broader architectural context.

Development addresses continuity through transformation.

Organisms do not persist despite developmental change. They persist through it. Growth, differentiation, maturation, regeneration, learning, and ageing all reorganise continuity while maintaining sufficient organisational coherence to sustain viability.

Development therefore illustrates one of the central themes of APS: continuity does not require stasis.

Persistence is achieved through organised transformation.

Within the broader architecture, development can be understood as the progressive reorganisation of significance, activity, and organisation across time.

## Ecology

No living system persists in isolation. Continuity depends upon ongoing relationships with environmental conditions, resources, opportunities, constraints, and other organisms.

Ecology therefore extends the architecture outward. If agency regulates continuity, ecological relationships help determine the conditions under which continuity remains possible.

Significance, cognition, behaviour, adaptation, and development are all shaped by ecological circumstances. Organisms and environments continually influence one another through reciprocal processes of organisation and change.

Ecology therefore reveals continuity as relational.

Persistence depends not only upon internal organisation but also upon the broader networks within which that organisation is embedded.

## Evolution

Development explains continuity within lifetimes. Evolution explains continuity across generations. From an APS perspective, evolution concerns the historical transformation of viability-oriented organisation.

Organisms inherit organisational solutions from previous generations, modify those solutions through variation and adaptation, and contribute to the emergence of new forms of continuity.

Evolution therefore extends organised persistence into historical time.

Within APS, inheritance, adaptation, diversification, and transformation can be examined as historical changes in the organisation of persistence. This is an integrative formulation, not a claim that the same principles have demonstrated additional explanatory power across targets.

Evolution reveals continuity as historical.

APS distinguishes between organisational change and organisational transition.

Many changes occur within an existing organisation of persistence. Regulation, adaptation, repair, and developmental modification may alter the state of a living system while preserving the organisation through which viability is maintained.

Organisational transitions involve a different kind of change. They occur when relations among constraints are reorganised such that a new and relatively stable regime of viability-oriented activity becomes possible.

APS formulates evolution as the historical transformation of viability-oriented, persistence-sustaining organisation, including transitions through which new forms of living organisation emerge and stabilise. This formulation remains current, but the tested developmental/evolutionary target showed no additional explanatory gain; that local null does not establish framework-wide failure.

## Diagnosis and Resilience

The importance of continuity becomes especially visible when continuity is threatened. Injury, disease, dysfunction, environmental disruption, and organisational failure expose the conditions upon which persistence depends. Diagnosis therefore concerns the identification of disruptions affecting viable continuity. Resilience concerns the capacity to recover, reorganise, and restore continuity under changing conditions.

Both concepts illuminate organisational requirements that often remain hidden when systems function normally.

APS consequently uses diagnosis and resilience as possible analytic windows onto organised persistence. Their usefulness for particular targets must be established independently and does not amount to validated APS-specific diagnostics.

## Social Organisation

APS proposes extensions beyond individual organisms.

Human social systems represent highly integrated forms of organised continuity distributed across individuals, institutions, practices, technologies, traditions, and symbolic systems.

Social organisation therefore extends agency, significance, cognition, and meaning into collective domains.

Cultural practices preserve continuity across generations. Institutions coordinate activity across populations. Knowledge systems stabilise significance. Norms and values guide collective behaviour. Shared meanings organise large-scale forms of cooperation.

The continuity challenges addressed by individual organisms reappear at broader scales in increasingly complex forms.

APS investigates whether social organisation can be related to selected organisational themes developed for living systems while preserving the distinct explanatory, historical, and normative requirements of collective life. This is a proposed extension, not a demonstrated reduction of social organisation to biological persistence.

# APS as a Research Program

## Extension Domains

Development can be examined as continuity through transformation; ecology as continuity through relation; evolution as continuity through history; diagnosis and resilience as continuity under disruption; and social organisation as continuity through collective coordination. Together these domains illustrate the breadth of questions that APS seeks to organise.

What begins as an attempt to understand viable continuity within living systems becomes a research programme asking where development, ecology, evolution, cognition, mind, meaning, resilience, and social organisation can be related to organised persistence—and where target differences or evidence set limits on that integration.

# Why the Framework Has This Shape

At first encounter, APS can appear to contain an unusually diverse collection of concepts. Life, viability, persistence, agency, process, scale, evaluation, significance, cognition, mind, selfhood, meaning, development, ecology, evolution, resilience, and social organisation are often treated as distinct subjects within biology and philosophy.

The purpose of this article has been to show that their relationship is neither accidental nor merely thematic.

APS possesses the structure that it does because living systems face a recurring organisational challenge: the maintenance of viable continuity through continual transformation.

This challenge provides the organising problem from which APS proposes a
series of further relationships.

Within the APS dependency architecture, continuity is related to viability;
viability to regulation and agency; agency to biological evaluation;
evaluation to significance; significance to integration; integration to
cognition; cognition to mind; mind to selfhood; selfhood to reflective
agency; and reflective agency to increasingly sophisticated forms of meaning.

These proposed relationships can be summarised as:

**Life → Agency → Biological Evaluation → Significance → Integration →
Cognition → Mind → Selfhood → Reflective Agency → Meaning**

The sequence expresses the architecture of the APS research programme. It does
not establish the individual dependencies merely through their conceptual
ordering. Each connection remains a Dependency Hypothesis answerable to
evidence, alternatives, limitations, and failure conditions.

What initially appears to be a collection of separate concepts can therefore
be investigated as a structured set of proposed organisational relationships.
APS does not begin with cognition and work backwards toward biology, nor does
it begin with meaning and attempt to reduce it to simpler components. It
begins with living organisation and investigates whether increasingly
sophisticated forms of organisation can be related to that biological
foundation through the proposed dependencies.

This is why the framework possesses the shape that it does. Its architecture
expresses a research programme organised around the problem of living
organisation rather than a sequence whose truth follows from its conceptual
coherence.

## Biological Explanation in APS

APS does not claim universal explanatory authority over biology.

Different biological questions may require different kinds of explanation.
Mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental, physiological, ecological,
mathematical, informational, cognitive, and other approaches may provide
adequate and preferable explanations of particular phenomena without being
reconstructed through APS.

APS makes living organisation, and the maintenance of that organisation
despite change, explicit explanatory targets. Organised persistence has current
explanandum-organising and integrative utility for continuity-through-change
questions, but general additional substantive explanatory gain has not been
demonstrated.

This gives APS a potentially broad field of application when living
organisation is the target, without making it foundationally indispensable or
claiming authority over specialist explanations. Relevance and gain remain
target-sensitive.

APS may therefore investigate how findings and explanations from different
biological domains bear upon the general problem of living organisation while
preserving their independent explanatory contributions. Development,
physiology, ecology, evolution, cognition, behaviour, and other domains retain
their own explanatory targets, methods, evidence, and standards of adequacy.

Where relationships among these domains contribute to explaining living
organisation, APS provides an analytic interface for organising inquiry into
them. Comparative assessment is supplied by the independent comparative
explanatory methodology, which can be applied to APS and other frameworks; it
is neither substantive evidence for APS biology nor a uniquely APS invention.
Integration is not assumed to be preferable in every case, and the inclusion
of additional domains or relationships does not by itself constitute
explanatory improvement.

APS therefore occupies a synthetic position without requiring synthesis to
replace analysis or specialist explanation. Its purpose is not to reduce,
absorb, or supersede established biological explanations, but to investigate
whether and how their explanatory contributions can be related when living
organisation or organised persistence is the target.

Whether APS provides additional substantive explanatory capacity for living
organisation cannot be inferred from its breadth, coherence, integration, or
conceptual architecture. It remains an open target-specific question requiring
comparison with the strongest relevant alternatives.

## APS and the Transition from Life to Mind

One of the most enduring questions in science and philosophy concerns how mind emerges within the natural world. Different traditions have approached this problem in different ways. Dualist approaches treat mind and matter as fundamentally distinct forms of reality. Reductive physicalist approaches seek to explain mental phenomena entirely in terms of underlying physical processes. Panpsychist approaches attempt to resolve the problem by locating mind-like properties at the foundations of reality itself. More recently, organisational and enactive approaches have explored the possibility that mind emerges through the distinctive forms of organisation characteristic of living systems.

APS belongs most closely to this latter tradition. Rather than treating mind as a separate substance or reducing it to a collection of physical components, the framework approaches the transition from life to mind as a problem of organisational development. The central question is not how non-living matter suddenly acquires mentality, but how increasingly sophisticated forms of organisation emerge from the challenge of maintaining viable continuity through time.

Within APS, mind is not treated as an isolated phenomenon. It is situated within a proposed dependency architecture linking life, evaluation, significance, cognition, mind, selfhood, reflective agency, meaning, value, morality, and ethics. The framework therefore investigates mind as part of a possible continuous organisational pathway rather than assuming that continuity has already been demonstrated.

This position places APS alongside work in theoretical biology, enactivism, embodied cognition, and organisational approaches to mind that investigates continuity between life and cognition. APS contributes to this discussion by proposing a more explicit dependency architecture through which increasingly sophisticated forms of organisation might be related to viability-oriented organised persistence. The proposal remains open to dependency-specific testing.

APS does not claim a final solution to subjective experience. Questions concerning consciousness, awareness, and first-person experience remain among the most difficult problems in contemporary science and philosophy. The framework's proposal is more limited and more specific. APS investigates a possible biological-organisational pathway toward awareness by identifying candidate relations among agency, evaluation, significance, integration, cognition, mind, selfhood, and meaning. It does not claim that this pathway has been collectively demonstrated.

Readers interested in this question in greater depth should continue to How Did Matter Become Aware of Itself?, which explores the relationship between life, cognition, mind, and awareness as one of the central long-term research programmes of APS.

APS begins from the problem of living organisation.

## Introduction

Every scientific framework begins with a question.

Physics seeks to explain the organisation of matter and energy. Chemistry seeks to explain the transformation of substances. Geology seeks to explain the dynamics of planetary systems. Biology confronts a different challenge because living systems are not merely objects occupying space. They actively maintain themselves, regulate their activities, repair damage, adapt to changing circumstances, and preserve continuity despite continual transformation.

The question is therefore not simply:

> What are living things made of?

Nor is it merely:

> What mechanisms operate within them?

The deeper question is:

> What kind of organisation allows living systems to remain viable through time despite continual change?

APS begins with this explanatory problem.

Rather than treating classification, structure, information processing, computation, mechanism, or other explanatory approaches as competitors that must be displaced, APS asks a particular organisational question: how do living systems maintain viable continuity through continual change? Clarifying this question establishes a principal explanatory target within APS.

This article provides one of the clearest entry points into the APS framework because it introduces two explanatory steps that organise the project. The first identifies viability-oriented organised persistence as a principal APS explanatory target. The second clarifies the analytic architecture through which APS investigates that target.

> **Where this article fits:** *Naturalising Life* explains the APS naturalisation programme and how APS seeks to render biological concepts scientifically intelligible. This article develops the APS account of biological explanation once a principal explanatory target has been identified.

> APS takes viability-oriented organised persistence as a principal explanatory target for understanding living organisation. The present article introduces the explanatory logic through which that target becomes intelligible. Readers seeking the wider conceptual architecture that emerges from this explanatory foundation should continue to *The Core Structure of APS*.

The naturalisation programme relevant to this explanatory approach is developed in *Naturalising Life*, which examines how APS seeks to make its biological concepts scientifically intelligible through naturalisation and conceptual closure.

**## Why Explanation Matters**

Scientific explanation is never simply a matter of accumulating facts. Before explanation can proceed, it must first identify what requires explanation. Different sciences focus on different phenomena because they are organised around different explanatory questions, and biology is no exception.

For much of its history, biology has addressed questions concerning mechanisms, structures, genes, behaviours, populations, development, ecology, and evolutionary change. These and other explanatory practices remain legitimate on their own terms. APS asks an additional organisational question: how do biological phenomena participate in the maintenance and transformation of living organisation?

This question shifts the explanatory emphasis within APS toward organised persistence. Genes, mechanisms, behaviour, development, ecology, and other phenomena may become relevant to this inquiry where their relations to the persistence of living systems are at issue. Their explanatory significance is not thereby exhausted by that relationship.

The result is a distinctive APS explanatory orientation. APS begins with the continuity of living systems and asks how such continuity becomes possible. Within APS, viability orientation characterises the organisation of this persistence, while constraint closure identifies how the conditions supporting that organisation are recursively maintained.

**## Step One — What Needs to Be Explained?**

Every explanatory framework identifies the phenomena and questions to which it gives particular explanatory priority.

APS treats viability-oriented organised persistence as a principal explanatory target for understanding living organisation. Its central question is how living systems maintain and re-establish viable continuity through time despite continual transformation.

Living systems exist under conditions that continually alter the circumstances of persistence. They experience perturbation, resource limitations, injury, developmental transformation, ecological change, ageing, competition, and uncertainty. Yet living organisation can remain continuous through such change.

APS therefore asks:

> How does viability-oriented continuity remain possible despite continual transformation?

This question places continuity at the centre of the APS explanatory project.

Organisms are not static objects. Within the APS account, they are investigated as continuity-preserving processes whose persistence depends upon organisational relationships that must continually be regenerated, maintained, repaired, and adapted across changing conditions. Once continuity becomes the explanatory target, many familiar biological phenomena acquire a new interpretation. Development becomes important because continuity must be preserved through transformation. Adaptation becomes important because continuity must remain viable under changing conditions. Repair becomes important because continuity must survive disruption. Resilience becomes important because continuity must recover from perturbation. Ecology becomes important because continuity depends upon organism–environment relations.

Within this APS inquiry, these phenomena can be investigated through their contributions to organised persistence.

**### Viability and Organised Persistence**

APS does not treat persistence as mere duration.

A rock may persist for thousands of years while remaining largely unchanged. Biological persistence is fundamentally different because organisms persist through ongoing activity. They regulate internal conditions, acquire resources, respond to challenges, repair damage, and continually reorganise themselves in ways that contribute to continued viability.

Persistence therefore becomes an organisational achievement. What requires explanation is not simply that living systems continue to exist, but that they remain viable despite continual change.

APS captures this insight through the concept of viability-oriented organised persistence.

Organised persistence names a principal phenomenon APS seeks to explain: the continuity of living organisation across time. Within the APS account, viability orientation characterises the organisation of this continuity, because living activity is organised around preserving the conditions required for continued existence. Constraint closure helps explain how such continuity is sustained, because the processes of the system contribute to maintaining the constraints that make those processes possible.

Living systems are viability-oriented organised continuities.

APS seeks to explain how such continuities become possible.

**### Development and the Explanatory Target**

One consequence of this perspective is the elevated importance of development.

Biology commonly studies development as a distinct domain among others. APS gives development particular explanatory prominence where continuity through transformation is at issue.

Organisms persist through continual developmental transformation. Growth, repair, regulation, learning, adaptation, ageing, ecological responsiveness, and behavioural change all involve developmental processes operating across different timescales. Within APS, development can therefore be investigated as a major continuity architecture through which organised persistence is maintained.

The developmental organisation of life reveals that living systems do not merely undergo development. They persist through development. This insight helps explain why development occupies such a prominent place within APS. Development is one of the principal forms of temporal organisation through which APS investigates the maintenance and transformation of viable continuity.

**### Perturbation and the Visibility of Organisation**

The APS perspective also changes how perturbation is understood.

Disruptions are often treated as unfortunate complications that interfere with the normal functioning of biological systems. APS instead treats perturbation as explanatorily valuable because it reveals continuity-preserving organisation that may otherwise remain hidden.

Repair becomes visible when damage occurs. Resilience becomes visible when continuity is threatened. Regulation becomes visible when conditions change. Adaptation becomes visible when organisms encounter novelty. Perturbation therefore exposes organisational relationships that are often concealed during ordinary functioning.

For this reason, APS regards vulnerability not as an accidental feature of life but as one of the clearest windows into biological organisation. Systems reveal what they depend upon, how they preserve continuity, and what capacities they possess for recovery when those capacities are tested.

The first step of APS can therefore be summarised quite simply. APS identifies viability-oriented organised persistence as a principal explanatory target for understanding living organisation: how living systems maintain continuity despite continual transformation, perturbation, and environmental change.

**## Step Two — How Does APS Organise Explanation?**

Identifying organised persistence as a principal explanatory target within APS is only the first step.

Once that target has been identified, a second question immediately emerges:

> How does APS organise explanation around it?

This question lies at the heart of the APS framework. APS answers it through Agency, Process, and Scale. Together they provide a structured analytic architecture within APS: complementary analytic relationships through which APS investigates viability-oriented organised persistence.

Agency, Process, and Scale are not merely three topics within biology, nor are they requirements imposed upon biological explanation generally. They are analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Agency concerns what living systems do; Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change; Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

APS therefore adopts an explicitly organisational perspective. It asks how activities, processes, constraints, relationships, and interactions are organised in ways that maintain, re-establish, or transform viable continuity. Mechanisms, components, structures, historical relations, and other explanatory resources retain their independent importance; APS relates them to organised persistence where doing so contributes additional explanatory understanding.

This leads directly to the central explanatory architecture of APS.

Within APS, living systems are investigated through the organisation of Agency, Process, and Scale in relation to viability-oriented organised persistence. This analytic architecture structures the APS account without being proposed as the necessary or exhaustive architecture of biological explanation generally.

The remainder of the framework develops this explanatory architecture in increasingly detailed ways.

**### Mechanism and the APS Explanatory Question**

APS does not reject mechanistic explanation.

Mechanisms remain indispensable within biology. Organisms regulate physiological conditions through mechanisms, repair damage through mechanisms, adapt behaviour through mechanisms, develop through mechanisms, and reproduce through mechanisms.

The question is therefore not whether mechanisms matter.

The question for APS is whether a mechanistic account by itself answers the particular organisational question APS poses about viable continuity.

APS asks questions about organised persistence that are not always identical to the explanatory targets of particular mechanistic accounts.

Mechanistic explanations can establish how particular activities occur. APS additionally asks how relevant mechanisms participate in the organisation through which viability is maintained, re-established, or transformed.

A repair mechanism may restore damaged tissue. A regulatory mechanism may stabilise physiological conditions. A behavioural mechanism may guide adaptive activity. Where the explanandum concerns organised persistence, APS asks how such mechanisms contribute to the continuing organisation of the living system.

This organisational question supplements rather than invalidates mechanistic explanation. Its relevance depends upon the explanandum.

**### Agency, Process, and Scale**

APS investigates organised persistence through three analytic projections:

- Agency;

- Process;

- Scale.

Agency concerns what living systems do. Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change. Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

These are not separate components, causal sources, or levels. They are analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation rather than components of reality.

Their explanatory value lies in the different perspectives they provide upon the same organised living system. APS relates them where doing so clarifies how living organisation is enacted, maintained, re-established, or transformed.

They therefore constitute the central analytic architecture used by APS for this inquiry, not obligatory dimensions of biological explanation generally.

**### Developmental Organisation and Continuity**

The importance of development becomes even clearer at this stage.

Where organised persistence is the explanatory target within APS and Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections, developmental organisation becomes a major target of investigation where continuity through transformation is the explanandum.

Development involves transformation across time and can coordinate physiological, behavioural, ecological, and organisational processes relevant to continuity. Within APS, developmental organisation provides a way of investigating how persistence remains viable through transformation rather than despite it.

This insight extends far beyond embryology or growth. Repair, adaptation, learning and ageing can each involve developmental processes across different temporal extents. APS therefore investigates important forms of continuity as developmentally organised.

APS consequently treats developmental organisation as a major continuity architecture where persistence through transformation is the explanandum.

**### Ecological and Organism–Environment Relations**

Biological continuity depends upon materially realised organism–environment relations.

Living systems depend upon continual interaction with their environments. Resources must be acquired, opportunities may be exploited, risks must be managed, and environmental conditions must remain sufficiently supportive for viability to be maintained.

Organism–environment relations therefore become important to the APS explanation of organised persistence.

APS rejects the image of organisms as self-contained entities operating independently of their surroundings. Living systems are materially situated in and coupled to environments that shape resources, constraints, perturbations, and opportunities relevant to viability. These organism–environment relations can therefore matter to development, adaptation, and persistence without implying that the wider ecological context is itself a viability-oriented biological agent.

APS therefore includes ecological as well as organisational relations where they are required to explain the persistence of the living system under investigation. Within this APS inquiry, explaining organised persistence may require attention to how continuity depends upon interacting organism–environment relations.

**### Repair, Resilience, and Organised Continuity**

A particularly revealing consequence of the APS approach concerns repair and resilience.

Repair and resilience are often studied as specialised biological topics. APS gives them particular explanatory importance where they reveal how continuity is preserved under conditions of challenge and disruption.

Repair can show how continuity is restored. Resilience can show how continuity recovers. Regulation can show how continuity is stabilised. Adaptation can show how continuity is reorganised. Where these processes contribute to maintaining or re-establishing viable organisation, they can illuminate persistence as an active organisational achievement rather than passive endurance.

This helps explain why APS places such emphasis upon perturbation. Disruptions can reveal organisational dependencies relevant to the maintenance of viability. When systems are challenged, relationships contributing to persistence may become visible in ways that ordinary functioning can conceal.

Within APS, repair and resilience can therefore illuminate organisational dependencies relevant to viable continuity beyond their immediate biological contexts.

**## From Biological Explanation to the Wider Framework**

The first step identifies a principal APS explanatory target. The second specifies the analytic architecture through which APS investigates it.

Together these steps establish the explanatory orientation of the wider framework.

Once continuity becomes the explanatory target and organised persistence becomes the explanatory focus, domains that are often treated separately can be related within APS where their explananda bear upon the organisation and transformation of living persistence.

Within APS, development, ecology, evolution, cognition and social organisation can each be investigated in relation to different forms or transformations of organised persistence. Their inclusion in this shared architecture does not establish that APS has demonstrated additional explanatory gain for each domain, nor does it replace their independently established biological explanations.

Within APS, these domains can, where relevant, be compared through a recurring explanatory concern:

> How is viability-oriented continuity preserved across changing conditions and timescales?

The wider APS framework develops this question across multiple domains of biological organisation. Where organised persistence is relevant to the explanandum, each domain may contribute a different perspective and identify different mechanisms, processes, scales, and relationships bearing upon continuity.

Within APS they can be related through a shared analytic architecture organised around a recurring biological concern:

> the maintenance of viable continuity through continual transformation.

The full dependency architecture linking life, agency, biological evaluation, significance, integration, cognition, mind, selfhood, reflective agency, and meaning is developed in *The Core Structure of APS*. **## What APS Changes in Explanatory Emphasis**

Every explanatory framework highlights some features of the world while treating others as secondary.

The proposed contribution of APS lies not simply in the concepts it introduces but in the explanatory organisation it proposes for relating biological phenomena. By placing viability-oriented organised persistence at the centre of its own explanatory inquiry, APS changes the explanatory emphasis through which familiar biological phenomena are interpreted and connected within the framework.

Many traditional approaches begin with structures, mechanisms, genes, behaviours, populations, or informational processes and then attempt to explain life through them. APS begins with a different question:

> How does continuity remain viable through time?

Once this question becomes central within APS, the explanatory emphasis changes.

Within this APS perspective, persistence is investigated not as static endurance but as an active organisational achievement. Stability is investigated not as immobility but as the regulated preservation of continuity through change. Development becomes especially relevant where continuity through transformation is the explanandum. Ecology becomes relevant where organism–environment relations bear upon the conditions of persistence. Repair and resilience can provide windows into continuity-preserving organisation when disruption and recovery are under investigation.

This shift does not discard existing biology.

APS seeks to relate and, where explanatory gain results, integrate it.

Mechanisms remain important. Genes remain important. Evolution remains important. Physiology remains important. Within APS, these phenomena can additionally be investigated through their contributions to organised persistence without implying that this exhausts their explanatory significance in biology.

APS therefore offers an organisational reconstruction centred on continuity rather than components alone. The resulting framework can relate domains often studied separately while preserving the independent explanatory contributions generated within them.

**## Continuity Architectures Across the Living World**

One consequence of the APS perspective is the recognition that continuity can be investigated through multiple interacting architectures.

Within APS, development, ecology, evolution, cognition and social organisation can be investigated as different contexts in which the organisation or transformation of continuity is relevant.

These domains differ enormously in their mechanisms, timescales, and forms of organisation. Within APS, they can nevertheless be related through a recurring explanatory concern.

Within APS, each can be investigated in relation to the organisation and transformation of viable continuity through time.

This insight helps explain why APS repeatedly returns to concepts such as organised persistence, viability, temporal organisation, adaptation, resilience, repair, and continuity. These are not isolated topics scattered throughout the framework. Within APS, they recur because each can bear upon questions concerning the organisation and transformation of viable continuity.

Within APS, life's persistence is investigated as organised continuity.

The various domains of APS explore how this organisation occurs across different biological domains. The framework therefore has a cumulative conceptual structure: different domains can contribute distinct perspectives on continuity-preserving organisation while their independent explanatory status remains separately assessable.

**### From Development to Social Organisation**

The developmental and social synthesis articles illustrate this cumulative structure particularly clearly.

Within APS, development is investigated in relation to continuity through the trajectory of individual organisms, while social organisation is investigated in relation to ways interactions among organisms, populations, institutions, and technologies can alter conditions relevant to biological and collective continuity.

Neither domain replaces the other. They address different explanatory contexts and spatial and temporal extents at which relations relevant to persistence may be investigated.

The developmental organisation of life examines viability through transformation, while the social organisation of life examines how collective organisation can modify conditions relevant to persistence across larger historical and organisational extents.

Together they motivate a bounded APS proposition:

> relations relevant to organised persistence can become distributed across interacting biological and social contexts without implying that every participating system is itself a viability-oriented biological agent.

This proposition guides inquiry within the wider framework where such distributed relations are relevant to the explanandum.

**## The APS Perspective**

APS can therefore be summarised as an explanatory reorientation.

It begins with a central APS question:

> How do living systems maintain viable continuity despite continual transformation?

It then develops an organisational framework for investigating that question.

A principal explanatory target is viability-oriented organised persistence.

The analytic architecture is provided by Agency, Process, and Scale as analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

The wider explanatory domains investigate different forms and transformations of organised persistence without requiring their independent biological explanations to be reduced to this APS architecture.

Development, ecology, evolution, cognition, physiology, diagnosis, and social organisation can therefore be related within APS where their connection to organised persistence provides genuine explanatory understanding.

APS approaches living organisation through continuity, organisation, viability, and persistence. These commitments define its explanatory orientation rather than prescribing the necessary structure of biological explanation generally.

Within APS, life is characterised as:

## APS as an Organising Framework

APS is not intended to replace existing biological research programmes or established biological explanations. It provides an integrative analytic framework for investigating questions concerning living organisation, viability, continuity, and organised persistence while remaining compatible with independently established mechanistic, developmental, evolutionary, ecological, physiological, and other biological explanations.

Its principal demonstrated contribution at the present stage is bounded analytic and methodological organisation: clarifying explanatory targets, distinguishing questions, exposing dependencies for assessment, and connecting research pathways without treating conceptual integration itself as evidence of biological truth or explanatory superiority.

Within those limits, APS aims to provide:

- an explicit organisational account of life within the framework;

- a coherent analytic structure for investigating biological organisation;

- ways of relating questions concerning mechanism, function, development, evolution, ecology, cognition, and diagnosis without collapsing their distinct explanatory targets;

- and an integrative architecture through which claims about organised persistence can be formulated and subjected to further comparative and empirical assessment.

Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections within this architecture. They are not universally necessary dimensions of biological explanation, and no projection has intrinsic causal or explanatory priority.

Organised persistence is a principal APS explanatory target rather than a fourth projection. It identifies the problem of how living organisation maintains continuity through material, structural, developmental, and environmental change. Whether APS provides additional explanatory gain for a particular organised-persistence target must be established by target-matched comparison rather than inferred from the coherence of the framework.

Comparative explanatory methodology remains independently applicable. Its standards can be used to assess APS, neighbouring frameworks, and established biological explanations alike; they do not constitute substantive evidence for APS biological claims.

The purpose of the architecture map is therefore not to prescribe a single route through APS but to make the structure of the framework visible so that readers can identify pathways most relevant to their interests.

As the framework continues to develop, additional pathways may emerge and existing research pathways may be revised as their individual propositions are assessed.

Within APS, organised persistence remains a principal explanatory target.

Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections through which APS investigates living organisation where those projections are relevant to the explanandum.

The various domains of APS provide research contexts in which questions concerning continuity, organisation, maintenance, transformation, and extension can be investigated without assuming that every domain requires the same explanatory categories or that conceptual integration demonstrates explanatory superiority.

## Key Point

APS is best understood as an integrative analytic framework connecting multiple research and explanatory pathways rather than as a collection of independent topics or a universal explanatory grammar for biology.

Persistence, development, ecology, evolution, cognition, diagnosis, social organisation, and philosophy of biology raise different explanatory questions. APS provides a structured way of relating those questions through its concern with living organisation and organised persistence while preserving their target differences, proposition-specific evidential status, and the independence of established biological explanations.

This architecture map is therefore a guide to the APS research programme: what its major pathways investigate, how they are related within the framework, and where claims require further empirical or comparative assessment.

APS can initially appear conceptually demanding because many important ideas appear together throughout the framework. Agency, Process, Scale, viability, constraint closure, persistence, adaptation, development, cognition, and organisation recur across different articles and explanatory contexts. ## The Central Question of APS

At its core, APS investigates a question about living organisation:

> How is a living system organised such that maintaining and re-establishing the conditions of its own persistence becomes a consequence of its own activity?

Established biological approaches address many aspects of living systems. Mechanistic, evolutionary, dynamical, informational, control-theoretic, organisational, behavioural, developmental, ecological, and other explanations may provide substantive, sufficient, or preferable accounts for particular biological explananda.

APS does not reject or subordinate those explanations. It makes living organisation itself an explicit target of inquiry and asks how viability, activity, constraint, continuity, and organised persistence can be related within that target.

The resulting framework is therefore an APS account of living organisation. Its conceptual integration does not establish that other biological explanations depend upon APS, that APS supplies their foundation, or that APS provides additional explanatory gain for every biological target.

## One Organisational Reality, Multiple Explanatory Perspectives

APS makes a canonical commitment about life:

> Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

This organisation consists in the ongoing modulation of constraints through which a system actively maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence.

Different APS concepts formulate different questions about this organisation. They do not all have the same role, and their inclusion within the framework does not make each one necessary for every biological explanation.

For example:

- **Viability** identifies conditions relevant to continued functioning and persistence.

- **Biological agency** is viability-oriented organisational activity.

- **Constraint closure** identifies an APS organisational commitment concerning the reciprocal maintenance of constraints involved in living organisation.

- **Process** asks how continuity is maintained despite change.

- **Scale** asks where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents and materially implemented relations relevant to the explanandum.

Agency, Process, and Scale have a specific architectural role within APS. They are complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of the same viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

They are not separate entities, components, levels, stages, dimensions, or independent causes. Nor are they universally or equally required for every biological explanation. Their relevance depends upon the explanatory target.

They distinguish different explanatory questions without dividing biological reality into components, levels, stages, or independent causes. Organised persistence is a principal APS explanatory target rather than a fourth projection.
  </p>

</div>

The purpose of these distinctions is not to multiply biological explanations. It is to make explicit which organisational question is being asked and to avoid treating differences of target, perspective, spatial or temporal extent, or explanatory purpose as though they automatically represented competing explanations.

The broader APS research pathways connecting biological agency with Biological Evaluation, significance, cognition, mind, selfhood, reflective agency, meaning, and social organisation contain propositions with different evidential status. Their inclusion within the wider architecture does not establish every proposed dependency or transition.

## Four Questions APS Keeps Distinct

APS distinguishes several questions that can otherwise become conflated. These questions are useful for navigating the framework, but they should not be interpreted as universally necessary stages of biological explanation.

### 1. What Is at Stake? — Viability-Oriented Organisation

Living systems depend upon an organisation of processes and constraints that must remain within conditions compatible with continued functioning and persistence.

APS describes this in terms of **viability-oriented organisation**.

Viability is not an additional substance or property layered onto an already existing system. Within APS, it identifies the viability-relative conditions under which the organisation of a particular living system can continue functioning and persist.

Viability therefore helps specify what is at stake in questions concerning organised persistence.

### 2. What Does the Living System Do? — Agency

Living systems do not persist passively.

They regulate, repair, reorganise, compensate, adapt, and otherwise modify their activity under conditions relevant to viability. APS uses **biological agency** in the compact sense of viability-oriented organisational activity.

Agency does not require consciousness, intelligence, intention, planning, or choice. It is not an additional component added to life; it is an analytic projection concerned with what living systems do.

Agency therefore directs attention to the activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained.

### 3. What Organisational Commitment Concerns Constraint Maintenance? — Constraint Closure

Living systems depend upon constraints whose organisation is itself maintained through system activity.

Within APS, **constraint closure** identifies the reciprocal organisation through which relevant constraints participate in maintaining and re-establishing the organisation that sustains them.

Constraint closure is therefore part of the canonical APS characterisation of life. Its inclusion within APS should not be taken to establish that every biological explanandum requires a constraint-closure explanation or that established biological explanations are incomplete without it.

### 4. How and Where Is Persistence Organised? — Process and Scale

Living organisation is dynamically maintained through time and materially realised across spatial and temporal extents.

Within APS:

- **Process** asks how continuity is maintained despite change.

- **Scale** asks where persistence is organised across the spatial and temporal extents and materially implemented relations relevant to the explanandum.

Process and Scale are not co-constitutive biological components or causal levels. They are analytic projections used when the relevant explanatory target requires attention to ongoing transformation, temporal organisation, spatial extent, or materially implemented relations across extents.

No projection has intrinsic explanatory priority, and a specified explanandum may require one, two, or all three APS projections.

## Why the Same Concepts Recur

Readers often notice that the same APS concepts appear in multiple contexts.

Agency may appear in discussions of regulation, adaptation, developmental reorganisation, ecological responsiveness, or continuity maintenance. Persistence appears in discussions of viability, resilience, diagnosis, evolution, development, and cognition.

This recurrence does not require the concepts to perform identical explanatory work in every context. The same living organisation can support different explanatory questions depending upon the explanandum.

For example:

| Question | APS Concept or Projection Emphasised |
|---|---|
| What conditions are relevant to continued functioning? | Viability |
| What does the living system do? | Agency |
| How are relevant constraints reciprocally maintained? | Constraint closure |
| How is continuity maintained despite change? | Process |
| Across which extents and materially implemented relations is persistence organised? | Scale |

These concepts therefore need not compete with one another. They distinguish questions that may be relevant to the same biological organisation.

Their recurrence across APS should likewise not be interpreted as evidence that every APS concept is required for every biological explanation.

## Why APS Uses Organisational Triads

APS frequently employs conceptual groupings such as:

- Agency – Process – Scale

- Persistence – Adaptation – Evolution

- Evaluation – Semiosis – Cognition

These groupings do not all have the same architectural or evidential status.

Agency–Process–Scale has a specific role as three complementary analytic projections of living organisation. Other triads can function as heuristic or research groupings connecting related questions and propositions.

Their purpose is to preserve distinctions that might otherwise be collapsed. They do not establish additional biological components, theoretical layers, universal explanatory requirements, or demonstrated dependency relations merely by placing concepts together.

For example, Agency and Process distinguish questions about activity and continuity through change. Scale draws attention to the extents and materially implemented relations relevant to the explanandum. Persistence, adaptation, and evolution distinguish related but non-identical questions about continuity, response to changing conditions, and historical transformation.

APS therefore uses such groupings as analytic aids rather than as evidence that biological explanation must conform to a fixed conceptual template.

[[box:analysis-vs-synthesis-two-directions-of-explanation]]

## Diagnosis, Classification, and Organisational Questions

APS has developed a research interest in diagnostic questions concerning living organisation.

Classification asks whether a system belongs to a specified category. Diagnostic inquiry can instead ask what organisational conditions are present, how they are materially realised, and how they contribute to a specified explanandum.

Within APS, questions may include:

- Is the relevant organisation viability-oriented?

- How is its activity materially and organisationally grounded?

- Does the activity contribute to maintaining or re-establishing conditions relevant to persistence?

Such questions can direct attention toward organisation rather than relying only on behavioural, complexity-based, intelligence-based, or historical classifications.

This does not establish a validated APS-specific diagnostic method. Whether an APS diagnostic formulation identifies additional explanatory relations must be assessed for the particular target and against the strongest relevant comparator.

Perturbation, repair, compensation, resilience, and breakdown may provide useful contexts for investigating organisational dependencies, but their usefulness does not itself demonstrate distinctive APS diagnostic capacity.

## Present Organisation and Historical Explanation

APS distinguishes questions concerning present organisation from questions concerning historical origin and transformation.

For some explananda, it can be useful first to specify whether and how a system is presently organised in a viability-oriented manner and then separately ask how that organisation arose, developed, or changed historically.

This is an explanatory distinction rather than a universal methodological order.

Evolutionary history can explain the origins and transformations of traits, organisations, capacities, and populations. Present organisational contribution is a different explanatory question. A trait's evolutionary history does not by itself determine its present viability-relative organisational contribution, just as present function does not eliminate the relevance of historical explanation.

Within APS, evolution is formulated as the historical transformation of organised persistence. This provides an integrative APS perspective on relations among persistence, variation, inheritance, selection, development, ecology, and historical change.

That formulation neither replaces established evolutionary explanations nor establishes additional explanatory gain merely by integrating these questions within APS. The relative contribution of an APS formulation must be assessed against target-matched evolutionary comparators.

## Development, Cognition, and Social Organisation

APS also develops research pathways concerning development, cognition, and social organisation.

### Development

Development provides an important domain for investigating how living organisation continues through transformation. Growth, differentiation, repair, plasticity, learning, adaptation, and ageing can raise questions about how continuity is maintained or re-established while biological organisation changes.

Within APS, organised persistence provides one way of formulating those questions. It does not follow that every developmental explanandum receives additional explanatory gain from that formulation.

### Cognition

APS investigates a proposed pathway from biological agency and Biological Evaluation toward increasingly integrated forms of biological significance and cognition.

These propositions do not constitute a demonstrated developmental sequence. Individual dependencies require independent assessment, and cognition should not be inferred merely from the presence of life, agency, regulation, information, or adaptive behaviour.

Within APS, cognition is investigated in relation to the organisation of biological significance across time and to activity involving viability-relevant possibilities that is not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation.

The cognition pathway therefore remains a proposition-specific APS research programme rather than an established consequence of the framework's general architecture.

### Social Organisation

APS also investigates how evaluative and organisational processes may become distributed through relations among individuals, groups, institutions, cultures, and materially realised social systems.

This extension does not require attributing biological agency indiscriminately to populations, institutions, cultures, ecosystems, or other collective entities.

Nor does social organisation represent a higher causal level within APS. Questions of Scale remain target-relative and concern physically realised extents and materially implemented relations.

The social pathway therefore investigates possible extensions of APS concepts while retaining the distinctions among biological agency, individual evaluation, collective organisation, social normativity, and moral legitimacy.

## From Biological Evaluation to Ethics

One APS research pathway investigates possible dependencies connecting Biological Evaluation, Significance, Meaning, Value, Human Values, Shared Evaluation, Social Norms, Moral Evaluation, Morality, and Ethics.

Within this architecture, **Biological Evaluation** concerns the process through which agency generates significance. **Significance** is a relationally real product of biological evaluation.

The later transitions in the pathway introduce additional questions concerning meaning, value, shared evaluation, social norms, moral justification, and reflective ethical inquiry.

These transitions do not have identical evidential status, and the sequence should not be read as a demonstrated causal or developmental progression.

## What APS Changes — and What It Does Not

APS changes the explanatory questions that become explicit when living organisation itself is made the target of inquiry.

It proposes that **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation** and examines how that organisation is enacted and maintained through biological activity. The corresponding problem of **organised persistence** concerns how living organisation maintains and re-establishes continuity through change.

This does not require biological explanation to begin from APS or to reconstruct other explanatory approaches within it.

Mechanistic, evolutionary, dynamical, computational, informational, control-theoretic, organisational, developmental, ecological, and other approaches may provide substantive, sufficient, or preferable explanations for particular biological questions.

APS therefore does not change the scientific status of biology or its material continuity with chemistry and physics. It proposes no special life substance, separate physical laws, non-natural causal forces, or distinct realm of living matter.

Its distinctive commitments concern how living organisation is characterised. Within APS:

- **Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity.**

- **Biological normativity is viability-relative asymmetry.**

- **Biological function is present-tense viability-relative organisational contribution.**

- **Purpose concerns viability-oriented organisation without requiring intention.**

Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation rather than independent components, causes, dimensions, levels, or a hierarchy.

These commitments provide an analytic framework within which biological questions can be formulated and compared. Their coherence, integration, biological specificity, or breadth does not by itself establish explanatory superiority over alternative approaches.

Where APS and another framework address the same explanatory target, their relative contribution requires target-matched assessment.

## A Simple Way to Hold APS Together

The conceptual architecture of APS can be summarised around a small number of related commitments and questions.

> APS characterises life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and investigates organised persistence: how living organisation maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own continuity through change.

Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections through which relevant aspects of that organisation can be investigated.

Other APS concepts and research pathways address more specific questions concerning viability, constraint organisation, adaptation, development, evolution, cognition, diagnosis, social organisation, normativity, function, purpose, significance, meaning, and ethics.

These concepts are not all equivalent, do not all have the same evidential status, and are not universally necessary for every biological explanation.

Seen in this way, APS can be navigated as an integrative analytic framework whose concepts are connected by their relation to living organisation while remaining distinguishable by target, function, and evidential status.

## Why APS Preserves Multiple Concepts

Living systems support many different explanatory questions.

Questions about present activity differ from questions about historical origin. Questions about continuity through change differ from questions about spatial extent. Questions about biological agency differ from questions about cognition. Questions about social normativity differ from questions about moral legitimacy.

APS preserves these distinctions rather than forcing them into a single explanatory category.

This produces conceptual complexity, but the complexity should not be justified simply by asserting that living systems themselves are complex. The relevant test is whether a distinction clarifies an explanandum, identifies an explanatory relation, or contributes something not already supplied by the relevant comparator.

Accordingly, APS concepts should be retained and developed where they improve analytic precision or survive appropriate comparative assessment, not merely because they form part of an integrated conceptual system.

## Conclusion

The purpose of this article has been to clarify why APS employs multiple concepts without treating them as multiple independent foundations or universally necessary dimensions of biological explanation.

Viability, biological agency, constraint closure, Process, Scale, development, adaptation, evolution, cognition, diagnosis, and social organisation perform different functions within the framework.

Agency, Process, and Scale have a particularly specific architectural status: they are complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of the same viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. They are analytic projections rather than components of reality.

Organised persistence is a principal APS explanatory target rather than a fourth projection. It concerns how living organisation maintains and re-establishes continuity through change.

Other APS pathways—including those concerning cognition, meaning, social organisation, morality, and ethics—contain propositions and proposed dependencies whose evidential status must be assessed individually.

APS therefore provides an integrative analytic framework for relating questions about living organisation without claiming that conceptual integration establishes biological truth, general explanatory superiority, or the inadequacy of independently established explanations.

The central orienting question can be stated simply:

> How is viable continuity organised and maintained through change?

Different APS concepts approach that question from different explanatory perspectives. Their value depends upon the clarity and explanatory contribution they provide for specified targets.

## Introduction — Why Naturalisation Matters

Biology employs concepts whose scientific interpretation remains contested. Purpose, agency, cognition, significance, meaning, and mind are prominent examples. They occur in scientific and philosophical discussions of living systems, yet disagreement persists about what they refer to, how broadly they should be applied, and what explanatory work they can legitimately perform.

The sources of these disagreements differ. Purpose can become entangled with intention, design, or final causes. Agency is sometimes restricted to conscious choice. Cognition can be tied to brains, nervous systems, representation, or particular behavioural capacities. Meaning can be treated as exclusively linguistic or psychological. Mind raises further questions concerning cognition, selfhood, awareness, and subjective experience.

Substantial empirical evidence does not automatically resolve such disputes because empirical disagreement is not always the only issue. Sometimes researchers disagree about the target, boundaries, or meaning of the concept through which evidence is being interpreted.

APS treats this as a methodological possibility rather than a general diagnosis of biological controversy. Some disputes may indeed involve conceptual ambiguity or conflation; others may primarily concern evidence, explanatory targets, theoretical commitments, operational criteria, or genuine biological differences.

*Naturalising life*, as the term is used here, concerns the attempt to formulate difficult biological concepts in terms that locate them within natural, materially realised living organisation and make their claims sufficiently explicit for scientific assessment.

This does not imply that naturalisation is a prerequisite for all scientific explanation, that every contested concept should be preserved, or that a naturalised APS formulation is thereby correct.

A second methodological idea, **conceptual closure**, concerns the degree to which the terms, relations, boundaries, and explanatory target of a concept have been specified clearly enough for systematic inquiry and comparison.

The central proposal of this article is therefore deliberately bounded:

> Naturalisation and conceptual closure can contribute to moving difficult biological concepts from conceptual ambiguity toward scientifically assessable claims.

Whether those claims are empirically supported, explanatorily useful, or preferable to alternatives remains a separate question.

[[box:what-naturalisation-does-and-does-not-establish]]

## Conceptual Difficulty in Biology

Scientific progress depends upon empirical investigation, but scientific inquiry also requires concepts through which observations, hypotheses, models, and explanations are formulated.

Conceptual difficulty can arise when the same term is used for different phenomena, when boundaries remain unclear, when assumptions imported from another domain are left unstated, or when researchers address different explananda while appearing to disagree about a single claim.

This does not mean that persistent scientific controversies can generally be explained by conceptual instability. Nor does it establish that conceptual clarification will resolve them. It means only that conceptual status can itself become a legitimate object of methodological analysis.

Purpose illustrates the problem. In biological contexts, purposive language may refer to intention, evolved function, goal-directed regulation, organisational contribution, or merely convenient shorthand. Those alternatives should not be treated as equivalent.

Agency raises a related issue. Human agency often implies deliberation or intention, whereas biological agency can be formulated more minimally. APS uses the compact definition:

> **Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity.**

This formulation is intended to specify an APS biological target without requiring conscious intention.

Cognition raises a more difficult boundary problem. Neural, behavioural, computational, enactive, basal, biogenic, and other approaches do not necessarily identify the same target or use the same criteria. Evidence of regulation, information processing, adaptive responsiveness, or learning-like behaviour therefore cannot by itself settle what should count as cognition.

Meaning and significance require similar care. Within APS, **Biological Evaluation is the process through which agency generates significance**, and **Significance is a relationally real product of biological evaluation**. Meaning belongs to a later and more demanding research pathway and should not be collapsed into significance simply because both concern organism–world relations.

These examples illustrate the methodological problem addressed here. Before competing claims can be compared effectively, it can be necessary to specify what concept is being used, what phenomenon it identifies, and what evidence would count for or against the resulting claim.

That is a problem of assessability rather than a guarantee of resolution.

## Naturalisation as a Methodological Proposal

In its broad philosophical use, naturalisation can refer to attempts to understand apparently exceptional or problematic phenomena within the natural world.

Within this article, the term is used more narrowly. **APS naturalisation** is a methodological effort to formulate difficult biological concepts in relation to materially realised living organisation without either treating them as non-natural exceptions or assuming that they can simply be eliminated in favour of lower-scale descriptions.

This is not an argument against reduction, mechanism, evolutionary explanation, physicalism, functional analysis, or other established approaches. Those approaches may provide sufficient or preferable explanations for particular explananda.

Naturalisation instead asks a different question:

> Can the phenomenon be formulated in biologically tractable terms such that the resulting claim becomes explicit enough to investigate?

That formulation may involve identifying the relevant organisation, activity, constraints, relations, spatial and temporal extents, or viability conditions. Which of these matters depends upon the explanandum.

Naturalisation should therefore not be confused with biological validation. A concept may be given a coherent naturalistic formulation and nevertheless prove empirically unsupported, explanatorily redundant, too broad, too narrow, or inferior to an existing account.

Nor does naturalisation require preservation of every inherited concept. Sometimes analysis may show that a term conflates several phenomena or contributes no explanatory gain. In such cases, revision, restriction, decomposition, or abandonment may be appropriate.

The methodological value of naturalisation lies in making the claim clearer—not in protecting the concept from criticism.

The sequence distinguishes six operations.

First, a **difficult biological concept** is identified. The difficulty may concern ambiguity, anthropomorphic inheritance, normative conflation, uncertain boundaries, explanatory underspecification, or some combination of these.

Second, **conceptual problem identification** asks exactly what is unclear. This is important because different problems require different remedies. Ambiguity is not the same as empirical inadequacy, and disagreement about boundaries is not the same as disagreement about mechanism.

Third, **naturalisation** seeks a biologically tractable formulation.

Fourth, **conceptual closure** specifies the relevant terms, relations, exclusions, boundaries, and explanatory target with sufficient precision for assessment.

Fifth, this work should yield a **scientifically assessable claim**: a claim explicit enough that evidence could, in principle, support, restrict, revise, or undermine it.

Sixth, substantive claims face **independent empirical and comparative assessment**. If an APS formulation claims explanatory gain, its contribution must be assessed against the strongest target-matched comparator rather than inferred from conceptual coherence.

This final step is essential. The methodological sequence is successful when it makes a claim more assessable. It is not successful merely because it makes a concept fit neatly within APS.

## Conceptual Closure as Clarification for Assessment

Conceptual closure does not mean finality.

Scientific concepts remain revisable, and some productive concepts retain contested boundaries for long periods. Closure should therefore not be treated as a binary state in which a concept becomes permanently fixed.

Within APS, conceptual closure is better understood as **sufficient specification for inquiry**.

A concept has greater closure when its intended target is clearer, its relevant relations are explicit, its exclusions can be stated, and its role can be distinguished from neighbouring concepts.

This is not the same as definition alone. A formal definition can still be operationally unclear or explanatorily unhelpful.

It is not the same as consensus. Researchers can agree on terminology while disagreeing about the phenomenon.

It is not the same as explanation. A concept can be sufficiently clear for inquiry without the corresponding phenomenon being fully explained.

And it is not the same as evidential closure. A well-specified claim may turn out to be false.

The value of conceptual closure is therefore methodological. It makes disagreement more precise. Researchers can ask whether they are disputing the same target, whether criteria distinguish positive from negative cases, whether alternative accounts are genuinely rivals, and what evidence bears upon the claim.

Conceptual closure, in this bounded sense, contributes to **scientific assessability**.

## Naturalisation, Conceptual Closure, and Scientific Intelligibility

Scientific intelligibility is a broader and more difficult notion than conceptual closure.

A phenomenon can be observable and experimentally tractable while remaining theoretically contested. Conversely, a concept can be clearly defined while contributing little explanatory understanding.

For that reason, this article does not define scientific intelligibility as an automatic product of naturalisation plus conceptual closure.

A more defensible relationship is:

**naturalisation**  
→ can improve biological tractability

**conceptual closure**  
→ can improve conceptual specification

**together**  
→ can contribute to scientifically assessable formulation

**independent inquiry**  
→ determines what evidential and explanatory standing the resulting claim deserves

Scientific intelligibility can therefore increase as concepts, evidence, models, mechanisms, comparative explanations, and empirical results become better coordinated. Naturalisation and conceptual closure may contribute to that process, but they neither exhaust it nor guarantee its outcome.

This distinction preserves the useful insight in the APS naturalisation programme without turning a methodological proposal into a general theory of scientific progress.

## APS as a Programme of Integrative Naturalisation

With those limits in place, APS can reasonably be interpreted as containing a recurring programme of **integrative naturalisation**.

Across several domains, APS attempts to reformulate concepts inherited from philosophical, psychological, or everyday discourse in terms appropriate to living organisation.

The important qualification is that these reconstructions do not all have the same scientific status.

Some are canonical APS commitments. Others are proposed extensions, research pathways, or claims requiring further empirical and comparative assessment.

### Life

APS proposes:

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

This is the canonical APS characterisation of life.

Its explanatory sentence is:

> This organisation consists in the ongoing modulation of constraints through which a system actively maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence.

Within APS, **organised persistence** is a principal explanatory target: how living organisation maintains and re-establishes continuity through material, structural, developmental, and environmental change.

This formulation does not establish that organised persistence is a uniquely APS problem or that other biological accounts are incomplete unless reconstructed through APS.

### Agency

APS naturalises agency without requiring consciousness or intention.

Its compact formulation is:

> **Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity.**

Agency is therefore what living systems do within the APS architecture. It is not an additional substance, module, level, or mental property inserted into biological organisation.

This formulation can be compared with alternative accounts of agency. Its coherence within APS does not itself establish its comparative adequacy.

### Purpose

APS treats purpose without invoking external design or conscious intention.

Within the framework:

> **Purpose concerns viability-oriented organisation without requiring intention.**

This formulation should not be expressed as purpose “emerging” automatically from life, agency, or a biological imperative.

Instead, APS proposes that purposive biological organisation can be investigated in viability-relative organisational terms. Whether this formulation captures all scientifically legitimate uses of purpose is a separate comparative question.

### Cognition

Cognition requires a stronger boundary.

Life does not require cognition; cognition presupposes life.

APS therefore does not identify cognition with regulation, information processing, adaptive responsiveness, evaluation, or agency alone.

Within the current APS research programme, biological agency becomes cognitive when integrated biological significance modulates activity across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities in ways not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation.

This formulation is intended to distinguish cognition from more general biological regulation while retaining continuity with living organisation.

It remains a substantive research proposition requiring operational, empirical, and comparative assessment.

### Significance and Meaning

APS distinguishes significance from meaning.

> **Biological Evaluation is the process through which agency generates significance.**

> **Significance is a relationally real product of biological evaluation.**

These formulations concern viability-relative relations through which conditions become significant for organised activity.

Meaning belongs to a later research pathway involving further integration. It should therefore not be defined simply as “significance-for-an-agent,” nor treated as an automatic consequence of biological evaluation.

This distinction is especially important for the Matter-to-Mind programme because it prevents biological significance, cognition, mind, and reflective meaning from collapsing into one another.

## Agency, Process, and Scale

Naturalisation does not add a second architecture alongside Agency, Process, and Scale.

Agency, Process, and Scale are **analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

Within APS:

- **Agency** asks what living systems do.

- **Process** asks how continuity is maintained despite change.

- **Scale** asks where persistence is organised across the spatial and temporal extents and materially implemented relations relevant to the explanandum.

They are analytic projections rather than components of reality.

They are not interacting ontological principles, causal levels, developmental stages, or universally necessary dimensions of biological explanation.

No projection has intrinsic causal or explanatory priority. Their use is target-relative.

Naturalisation and conceptual closure have a different function. They concern how a difficult concept or claim can be formulated clearly enough for scientific assessment.

The relationship is therefore methodological rather than architectural equivalence:

- Agency, Process, and Scale distinguish complementary questions about living organisation.

- Naturalisation and conceptual closure help specify difficult concepts and claims.

- Empirical and comparative assessment determine what scientific standing those claims deserve.

This prevents conceptual methodology from being mistaken for ontology and prevents the APS analytic architecture from being re-described as interacting biological causes.

## Illustrative Applications

The following examples show how the naturalisation approach can be used within APS. They are illustrations of conceptual reconstruction, not demonstrations that the APS formulations are uniquely correct.

### Purpose

Purpose is difficult partly because the term can suggest intention, design, foresight, or final causes.

APS narrows the target by treating purpose as **viability-oriented organisation without requiring intention**.

The naturalisation step removes the requirement for a conscious or external goal. Conceptual closure then requires clarification of how purpose differs from function, agency, normativity, adaptation, and mere persistence.

The resulting APS claim is more explicit and therefore more assessable. Whether it provides explanatory gain over established accounts remains a comparative question.

### Agency

Everyday agency often implies conscious decision-making.

APS instead defines **biological agency as viability-oriented organisational activity**.

This makes the intended biological target explicit while excluding intention as a necessary condition.

The next question is evidential rather than definitional: whether this formulation discriminates relevant cases, clarifies biological explanation, and contributes beyond established accounts of autonomy, regulation, adaptivity, behaviour, or organisation.

### Cognition

Cognition illustrates why naturalisation must not become indiscriminate expansion.

Evidence of adaptive regulation, signal discrimination, memory-like effects, or information processing does not automatically establish cognition.

APS therefore introduces a stronger proposed condition involving integrated biological significance, temporal organisation, and viability-relevant possibilities not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation.

Naturalisation here does not simply broaden cognition across life. Its role is to make the proposed boundary explicit enough to test.

### Significance and Meaning

Meaning presents a particularly important conceptual risk because biological significance can easily be redescribed as meaning without demonstrating the additional organisation required.

APS therefore begins with **Biological Evaluation** and **Significance** rather than treating meaning as primitive.

Significance concerns viability-relative relations generated through biological evaluation. Later questions concern how significance becomes integrated into cognition, mind, selfhood, reflective agency, and forms of meaning.

Naturalisation here is therefore a process of differentiation as much as integration. It prevents neighbouring concepts from being collapsed merely because they form part of a connected research programme.

Taken together, these cases show what the methodological proposal can legitimately do. It can clarify targets, remove inappropriate inherited assumptions, specify relations among concepts, and produce claims capable of further assessment.

It cannot determine the outcome of that assessment in advance.

## From Life Toward Mind

APS develops a broader research pathway concerning the organisational route from life toward cognition, mind, selfhood, reflective agency, and meaning.

The canonical pathway is:

**Life**  
↓  
**Agency**  
↓  
**Biological Evaluation**  
↓  
**Significance**  
↓  
**Integration**  
↓  
**Cognition**  
↓  
**Mind**  
↓  
**Selfhood**  
↓  
**Reflective Agency**  
↓  
**Meaning**

This pathway should not be interpreted as a demonstrated causal or developmental sequence.

Its arrows represent proposed dependencies within an APS research architecture. Individual transitions retain their proposition-specific evidential status.

The pathway is useful because it distinguishes questions that are easily conflated. Life and agency do not imply cognition. Significance is not identical to meaning. Cognition is not identical to mind. Selfhood does not require reflective agency, and none of these distinctions by itself solves the problem of subjective awareness.

Naturalisation contributes by asking how each proposed concept can be formulated in biologically tractable terms.

Conceptual closure contributes by asking whether the relevant target, boundaries, dependencies, and exclusions have been specified clearly enough for assessment.

Neither process demonstrates the dependency pathway itself.

The Matter-to-Mind programme therefore remains a research programme rather than an accomplished derivation of mind from life.

Its purpose is to investigate how increasingly demanding forms of organisation might be related while preserving the evidential burden associated with each proposed transition.

APS deliberately stops short of claiming that this programme solves subjective experience. The boundary is explanatory rather than evasive: clarifying the biological-organisational pathway toward awareness is not equivalent to explaining why or how subjective experience exists.

## Naturalisation and Existing Biological Explanation

The naturalisation programme should not be interpreted as a demand that established biological explanations be reconstructed within APS.

- Mechanistic explanations can identify causal organisation.

- Evolutionary explanations can reconstruct historical transformation.

- Developmental explanations can account for growth, differentiation, plasticity, and developmental change.

- Ecological explanations can address organism–environment relations, populations, communities, and environmental dynamics.

- Dynamical, computational, informational, physiological, control-theoretic, organisational, and other approaches can likewise provide substantive explanations for appropriately specified targets.

APS does not acquire priority over these explanations merely because it formulates questions about living organisation.

Naturalisation and conceptual closure can instead be used to ask whether a contested APS concept has been specified clearly enough to compare with those alternatives.

That is especially important when APS claims additional explanatory contribution.

A claim of explanatory gain requires:

- a specified explanandum;

- reconstruction of the strongest relevant comparator;

- neutral formulation of the competing or complementary claims;

- evidence that the APS account contributes something not already supplied by the comparator;

- and willingness to accept rivalry, complementarity, target-difference, redescription, residual gain, or no-additional-gain outcomes.

Conceptual integration cannot substitute for that assessment.

## Scientific Intelligibility as an Open Methodological Question

The relationship among naturalisation, conceptual closure, and scientific intelligibility should remain open to investigation.

This article proposes that naturalisation and conceptual closure **can contribute** to scientific intelligibility by making difficult concepts more tractable and claims more assessable.

It does not establish that these operations constitute a universal epistemology of biology or a general account of scientific progress.

Some concepts become scientifically productive while retaining contested boundaries. Some disputes are resolved primarily by empirical discovery rather than conceptual reconstruction. Some concepts are replaced rather than stabilised. Others divide into several more precise concepts.

The history and philosophy of biology therefore provide no reason to assume a single route to conceptual progress.

APS contributes a narrower methodological proposal: when conceptual ambiguity, inherited anthropomorphism, conflation, or explanatory underspecification obstructs assessment, naturalisation and conceptual closure may help expose exactly what is being claimed.

That proposal is itself assessable.

Its value should be judged by whether it improves conceptual precision, supports discriminating tests, clarifies relations among explanations, or enables more rigorous comparative assessment.

If it does not, its methodological contribution is limited.

## What Naturalisation Contributes to APS

Naturalisation is therefore best understood as one methodological strand within APS rather than as its epistemological foundation.

It contributes to the framework by encouraging explicit questions such as:

- What phenomenon is the concept intended to identify?

- Which inherited assumptions are being retained or rejected?

- How is the phenomenon materially realised?

- What distinguishes the concept from neighbouring concepts?

- What would count as a positive, negative, or borderline case?

- What explanatory target does the concept address?

- What evidence would support or undermine the claim?

- Does the formulation add anything to the strongest existing explanation of that target?

These questions complement the wider APS concern with organised persistence and the Agency–Process–Scale analytic architecture.

They do not replace established scientific methodology.

Their role is to make APS propositions—and potentially other difficult biological propositions—more explicit, criticisable, and assessable.

That is a stronger methodological standard than simply asking whether the concepts form a coherent system.

## Conclusion

Many important biological concepts remain contested despite extensive empirical investigation. Such disputes can involve evidence, theoretical commitments, explanatory targets, operational criteria, conceptual ambiguity, or combinations of these.

APS proposes naturalisation and conceptual closure as two methodological resources for addressing the conceptual part of this problem.

**Naturalisation** seeks biologically tractable formulations of difficult phenomena.

**Conceptual closure** seeks sufficient specification of terms, relations, boundaries, exclusions, and explanatory targets for systematic assessment.

Their legitimate outcome is not guaranteed truth or explanatory superiority.

It is **scientific assessability**.

Once a claim becomes assessable, substantive scientific work remains. Evidence may support it, restrict it, transform it, or undermine it. Comparative analysis may show explanatory gain, complementarity, target difference, redescription, or no additional gain.

This distinction is particularly important within APS because the framework connects concepts across life, agency, Biological Evaluation, significance, cognition, mind, selfhood, reflective agency, meaning, social organisation, morality, and ethics.

Conceptual connection is not equivalent to demonstrated dependency.

Likewise, Agency, Process, and Scale are not interacting ontological principles. They are complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of the same viability-oriented, constraint-closed living organisation.

Naturalisation therefore does not provide an ontological or epistemological foundation from which the rest of APS follows.

Its more defensible contribution is methodological:

> **to help move difficult biological concepts from ambiguity toward formulations clear enough to be criticised, compared, and tested.**

That contribution also defines the appropriate relationship between *Naturalising Life* and the wider Matter-to-Mind programme. APS can investigate how concepts such as significance, cognition, mind, selfhood, reflective agency, meaning, and awareness might be biologically formulated without claiming in advance that their proposed dependencies have been demonstrated.

The resulting programme remains open-ended.

Its success depends not on whether increasingly many concepts can be incorporated into APS, but on whether the resulting claims become clearer, more discriminating, empirically tractable, and capable of surviving appropriate comparative assessment.

## Introduction: What is Biology Trying to Explain?

Biology explains an extraordinary range of living phenomena. Genetics explains inheritance. Physiology explains how organisms coordinate their activities. Developmental biology explains how living forms arise and change. Evolutionary biology explains adaptation and diversification, while ecology explains the relationships between organisms and their environments.

These are powerful achievements, but they do not all ask the same questions or explain life in the same way. A genetic explanation of inheritance differs from a physiological explanation of regulation, an evolutionary explanation of adaptation, or an ecological explanation of interdependence. This diversity is not a weakness. It reflects the many different features of living systems and the different methods needed to investigate them.

Yet an important question remains. How do these different explanations relate as explanations of living systems?

The question is not whether biology needs one theory to replace its existing disciplines. Nor is it whether genes, mechanisms, evolution, information, or organisation should be declared the single foundation of life. Each can make an important explanatory contribution. The deeper question is whether their relationships can be clarified without reducing them to one another.

This question remains open despite biology’s extraordinary empirical success. Scientific discoveries continue to reveal how living systems work, develop, evolve, and interact, but they also invite reflection on what makes these phenomena distinctively biological and how different kinds of biological explanation fit together. The continuing discussion of what life is shows that this foundational question has not become obsolete (Nicholson 2025).

The Agency–Process–Scale Framework begins here. APS accepts biology’s explanatory diversity and does not offer another theory intended to replace it. Instead, it asks what different biological approaches contribute to understanding a striking feature of life: living systems persist despite continual change.

This article explores that comparative proposal. It begins with the different answers biology gives to different explanatory questions, then considers the continuity of living organisation through change. From there, it asks whether organised persistence can provide a useful target for comparing explanatory contributions without treating it as the single explanandum of biology or reducing established explanations to APS terminology.

## Biology Has More Than One Kind of Answer

Biological explanations differ because they address different questions. A molecular biologist may ask how a particular gene is expressed. A physiologist may ask how body temperature is regulated. A developmental biologist may ask how tissues become organised. An evolutionary biologist may ask how a trait arose, while an ecologist may ask how organisms persist within changing environments.

These questions are related, but they are not interchangeable. Explaining how a structure presently works is different from explaining how it evolved. Describing the molecular interactions involved in a process is different from explaining its contribution to the functioning of an organism. Investigating an organism’s internal regulation is different from investigating its ecological relationships.

No single kind of explanation answers every biological question. This is partly because living systems are extraordinarily complex, but it is also because scientific explanations are selective. They identify particular patterns and relationships according to the question being asked. Different investigations can therefore provide legitimate explanations without offering identical accounts of the same phenomenon (Mitchell 2002; Potochnik 2017).

Biology’s explanatory diversity should not be treated as a problem that must be eliminated. Mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental, ecological, organisational, and other approaches have achieved their success by concentrating on different features of the living world. Their differences allow biology to explain more, not less.

But accepting this diversity does not mean that biological explanations are unrelated. Genes function within cells, cells participate in organisms, organisms develop and act within environments, and these relationships are transformed through evolution. Explanations directed at one of these concerns may therefore intersect with explanations directed at another.

The challenge is to understand these relationships without forcing every explanation into one theoretical form. We can ask what each approach explains especially well, where its contribution meets those of other approaches, and what broader features of living systems become clearer when these contributions are considered together.

APS accepts this challenge as a question of comparison rather than unification. It does not ask which biological approach should become the foundation of all the others. It asks how their different achievements contribute to understanding living organisation.

## An Enduring Biological Question

Questions about what makes biological explanation distinctive are not new. Throughout its history, biology has approached living beings through different combinations of activity, structure, function, regulation, development, and historical change. These approaches did not all seek the same kind of answer, but each contributed to the continuing effort to make life scientifically intelligible.

Aristotle understood living beings through their characteristic activities, capacities, and forms of organisation. His biology differed greatly from modern science, but it established an enduring idea: explaining an organism involves more than listing the materials from which it is composed. It also involves understanding how those materials participate in the organised activities characteristic of its form of life (Aristotle 1937).

In the nineteenth century, experimental physiology brought a different explanatory priority to the foreground. Claude Bernard investigated the regulated internal conditions that enable organisms to continue functioning despite changes in their surroundings. His work helped establish that living continuity depends on active physiological regulation rather than passive material stability (Bernard 1865).

Darwin transformed biological explanation in another way. Natural selection explained adaptation and diversification historically, showing how present biological forms can be understood through descent, variation, and differential reproduction. Evolutionary explanation did not replace physiological or developmental explanation. It addressed a different question: how living forms and their relationships have changed over generations (Darwin 1859).

Later advances in genetics, molecular biology, development, ecology, and systems biology greatly expanded biology’s explanatory reach. They did not produce one universally accepted answer to what biology explains. Instead, they revealed more clearly the variety of questions involved in understanding living systems. The history of biological thought is therefore not a simple progression toward a single explanatory foundation, but a continuing development of different ways of making life intelligible (Mayr 1982).

This history matters because it changes how the opening question should be approached. We need not assume that Aristotle, Bernard, Darwin, and contemporary biology were all trying to explain exactly the same thing. Their differences are part of the evidence. Biology has repeatedly advanced by developing new forms of explanation while retaining questions that other approaches address especially well.

The task is therefore not to select one historical tradition as the final answer. It is to ask how these different explanatory achievements relate—and whether living systems present a continuing feature that makes their comparison scientifically productive.

## Persistence Through Change

Living systems persist, but they do not do so by remaining unchanged. Their materials are continually replaced, their activities vary, and their relationships with their surroundings shift. Organisms grow, develop, repair damage, regulate internal conditions, and respond to circumstances that cannot be held constant.

Even the apparent stability of an organism is therefore an achievement. A living cell must continue exchanging matter and energy with its surroundings. A plant must adjust its growth and physiology as light, water, temperature, and other conditions change. An animal must coordinate metabolism, movement, repair, and behaviour while its internal and external circumstances vary.

Process-oriented biology draws attention to this dynamic character of life. Organisms are not simply structures that happen to undergo change. Their continuity depends on continuing processes that rebuild, regulate, and reorganise them. What requires explanation is not change alone, but how continuity is sustained through change (Nicholson and Dupré 2018).

This continuity is not the preservation of every material component or structural detail. Nor does it require an organism to return continually to one fixed state. Development can transform an organism profoundly while preserving the continuity of its living organisation. Repair may restore functioning through arrangements that differ from those present before damage. Responses to new conditions may alter patterns of activity while maintaining viability.

Persistence also takes different forms across time. During an individual life, living organisation must continually maintain or re-establish the conditions of its functioning. Across generations, evolution transforms inherited and developmental organisation rather than preserving it unchanged. Continuity and transformation are therefore not opposites. Biological persistence often depends on the capacity for organised change.

APS calls this continuity **organised persistence**: the continuity of living organisation through change. The term does not refer to mere survival, static endurance, or anything that happens to last. Living systems can maintain continuity despite extensive material turnover, developmental transformation, physiological variation, and changing environmental conditions.

Organised persistence should not be confused with the APS definition of Life. APS defines:

> **Life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

The distinction is important. The definition of Life identifies the organisation APS proposes as characteristic of living systems. Organised persistence identifies the continuity of that living organisation through change. It is therefore a phenomenon requiring explanation, not a second definition of Life and not itself the activity through which continuity is maintained.

This distinction gives the opening question a more focused comparative form. APS does not assume that every biological explanation has organised persistence as its explanandum. It asks instead whether relating different explanatory achievements to the continuity of living organisation can clarify what each contributes and where important explanatory differences remain.

## The APS Proposal

APS advances a comparative proposal: in appropriate cases, biological explanations can be compared by asking what each contributes to understanding the continuity of living organisation through change.

This does not mean that every biological discipline is secretly explaining organised persistence. Genetics, physiology, development, evolution, ecology, and other fields address distinct questions and phenomena, and their explanations must first be understood on their own terms. APS does not reduce them to one theory or judge them by a single method.

The comparative question arises only after those explanatory targets and achievements have been identified. APS then asks whether relating them to organised persistence reveals explanatory relationships, differences, or omissions that are not already clear within the established explanations themselves.

For any biological approach, comparison therefore begins by asking:

- What is the explanatory target?
- What does the approach explain especially well?
- What, if anything, does relating that explanation to organised persistence add?

A mechanistic explanation might reveal how a particular biological process works. An evolutionary explanation might show how a trait or organisation arose and changed historically. An ecological explanation might clarify how an organism’s continued functioning depends on its relationships with its surroundings. APS does not replace any of these explanations. It asks how their different contributions relate to the persistence of living organisation.

Organised persistence is therefore a proposed comparative target, not a new biological mechanism. Whether it proves useful depends on the explanatory work it enables: whether comparison reveals relationships, distinctions, or unresolved questions that are not already adequately represented by the strongest established explanations. If it merely redescribes those explanations in APS terminology, no explanatory gain has been established.

APS also begins from biological agency. Living systems do not merely undergo change; through their activities they regulate, repair, respond, and reorganise. Biological agency is the viability-oriented organisational activity through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their persistence (Spencer 2026).

This does not imply conscious intention or decision-making. A cell regulating its internal conditions and a plant adjusting its growth are not assumed to deliberate about future outcomes. Their agency lies in the organised activity through which conditions affecting viability are actively managed.

The APS proposal can therefore be stated compactly:

> Biology offers many legitimate explanations of living phenomena. APS compares their contributions by asking how each helps explain the organisation through which living systems persist despite change.

This proposal does not settle the question posed by the article’s title. It provides a way of investigating it.

## Agency, Process, and Scale

APS investigates living organisation through three complementary analytic projections. These distinguish explanatory questions without dividing biological reality into three components, causes, or levels.

**Agency asks what living systems do in relation to viability.** Living systems regulate their activities, respond to changing conditions, repair damage, acquire and use resources, and reorganise under changing circumstances. APS describes biological agency as viability-oriented organisational activity. Agency does not require consciousness or deliberate choice.

**Process asks how organisational continuity is maintained through change.** Living systems persist through continuing material and organisational activity. Metabolism, regulation, development, repair, and other processes involve change while contributing, in different circumstances, to continuity of living organisation. Process therefore concerns the organisation of continuity through change; it should not be equated simply with time.

**Scale asks across what materially realised spatial and temporal extents, and through what relations among them, persistence is organised.** Biological entities, activities, processes, constraints, and relations occur across physically real spatial and temporal extents. Scale, as an analytic projection, asks which of those extents and relations are relevant to the explanatory problem. It should therefore be distinguished both from physical scale itself and from a hierarchy of biological levels.

Consider a plant responding to water shortage. Agency asks what the plant does in relation to changing conditions: for example, regulating water loss, altering growth, or reallocating resources. Process asks how continuity of living organisation is maintained or re-established while the plant and its conditions change. Scale asks which materially realised spatial and temporal extents and relations—such as cellular activity, whole-plant coordination, environmental interaction, development, or evolutionary history—are relevant to the particular explanation being sought.

Agency, Process, and Scale are **analytic projections of one living organisation**. Their differentiation belongs to explanatory analysis; it does not divide the living system into three independently existing parts. The activities, processes, constraints, and spatial and temporal relations they disclose are biologically real, while their separation into Agency, Process, and Scale serves investigation and explanation.

Analytic differentiation therefore does not imply ontological separation.

This threefold enquiry helps APS compare explanations without forcing them into one form. A molecular explanation may clarify a mechanism, a physiological explanation may clarify coordinated functioning, an ecological explanation may clarify environmental dependency, and an evolutionary explanation may clarify historical transformation. Agency, Process, and Scale help identify how these different contributions bear on organised persistence.

The APS name therefore identifies an explanatory practice rather than a division of reality. It keeps three questions together:

- What is the living system doing?
- How is continuity being maintained or re-established through change?
- Across what spatial and temporal extents, and through what relations among them, is that persistence organised?

## What Difference Does This Make?

APS does not give biology a new set of facts. Its contribution lies in changing how explanatory questions are related. It asks what becomes visible when established biological explanations are considered in relation to organised persistence.

### Mechanism

A mechanistic explanation asks how a phenomenon is produced through organised entities and activities (Machamer et al. 2000). It might explain how a membrane channel regulates ion movement, how a signalling pathway initiates a cellular response, or how physiological processes coordinate temperature regulation.

APS preserves these explanations and also asks:

> How does this mechanism contribute to maintaining or re-establishing viable organisation?

This additional question does not alter or automatically improve the mechanistic explanation. For some explanatory problems, the mechanism may already provide everything required. Where the explanandum concerns living organisation, however, it may be useful to ask how the identified mechanism bears on viability or organised persistence. APS contributes only if that additional question reveals an explanatory relationship not already adequately represented by the mechanistic account.

### Evolution

Evolutionary explanation asks how biological traits, populations, and forms of organisation have changed historically. Natural selection, inheritance, variation, drift, and other evolutionary processes explain patterns that cannot be understood from present functioning alone.

APS also asks:

> What organisation is being transformed, and how does that transformation alter the conditions under which living systems can persist?

This does not make evolution a process of preserving organisms unchanged. Evolution concerns the historical transformation of living organisation across generations. Established evolutionary theory explains that transformation through variation, inheritance, selection, drift, and other relevant processes. APS asks whether relating such historical transformation to organised persistence clarifies something about the organisation being transformed that the evolutionary explanation does not already make explicit.

### Systems Biology

Systems biology investigates how interacting components and processes generate coordinated biological behaviour. It examines networks, dynamics, regulation, and control rather than treating biological components in isolation (Kitano 2002).

Where the explanatory problem concerns living organisation, APS may additionally ask:

> How does this coordination bear on the maintenance or re-establishment of viable organisation?

Not every organised network is alive, and not every stable pattern is biologically significant. But neither does identifying a relation to viability automatically improve a systems explanation. The comparative question is whether making that relation explicit contributes something not already captured by the systems account.

### Biological Autonomy

Organisational approaches ask how living systems maintain themselves through mutually dependent processes and constraints. They have shown why biological organisation cannot be understood simply as a collection of externally assembled parts (Moreno and Mossio 2015).

APS overlaps with this concern while asking a distinct comparative question:

> What does biological autonomy contribute to understanding organised persistence, and how does it relate to mechanistic, evolutionary, developmental, and ecological explanations?

The convergence is important, but the approaches are not identical. Biological autonomy is a substantive account of living organisation. APS uses comparison to clarify what this and other approaches contribute to a broader explanatory enquiry.

### Ecology

Ecological explanations investigate relationships between organisms and their surroundings. Access to water, energy, nutrients, other organisms, and suitable physical conditions can all affect whether living organisation continues to function.

APS also asks:

> How do these relationships alter conditions relevant to the organised persistence of the living systems involved?

This question does not imply that an ecosystem is itself a biological agent. It asks how environmental relationships become materially involved in the persistence of organisms and other living systems.

These examples show where APS seeks explanatory gain. It does not repeat each explanation in new vocabulary, and it does not claim that organised persistence supplies what existing biology has failed to discover. Instead, it provides a common point of comparison.

That comparison can reveal several things:

- different explanations may address different aspects of the same biological problem;
- explanations that use similar terms may nevertheless ask different questions;
- present functioning and evolutionary history make distinct contributions;
- environmental relationships may condition agency without replacing it;
- detailed mechanisms may acquire biological significance through their contribution to viable organisation.

The value of APS therefore depends on demonstrable explanatory gain. Comparison must clarify relationships, distinctions, unresolved questions, or possible lines of investigation beyond what the strongest established explanation already provides. If organised persistence merely redescribes existing explanations in APS terminology, no explanatory gain has been established.

APS is best understood as an invitation to test a question:

> Does comparing biological explanations through organised persistence help us understand living systems more clearly?

## An Invitation to Explore

The question “What is biology trying to explain?” does not require biology to choose one theory, method, or explanatory foundation. Living systems present different phenomena at different spatial and temporal extents, and biology has developed correspondingly different ways of investigating them.

APS begins from this diversity. It does not claim that every biological discipline is explaining exactly the same thing. It asks whether comparing their distinctive achievements in relation to organised persistence can produce greater explanatory clarity.

This proposal remains open to investigation. Its value depends on whether organised persistence clarifies relationships among biological explanations, exposes unresolved questions, and supports productive research. APS should therefore be assessed by what it helps biology explain—not simply by the coherence of its terminology.

Readers wishing to continue can follow three immediate paths:

- **What Is APS?** introduces the purpose and scope of the framework.
- **Naturalising Life** explains how APS approaches life through viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.
- **The Core Structure of APS** develops the relationship among Agency, Process, and Scale.

These articles lead into the wider APS programme, including evolution, development, cognition, individuality, ecology, and biological explanation. Each extends the enquiry begun here while addressing a more specific domain or problem.

Readers interested in the wider scientific and methodological setting of this proposal can also follow the APS-THEORY research programme. Its three studies examine the historical identity of theoretical biology, the functional boundary between theoretical biology and philosophy of biology, and a neutral method for comparing dependency claims in biological explanation. Together, they provide an independent test of the explanatory setting within which APS makes its own proposals.

APS therefore proposes organised persistence neither as biology's final or exclusive explanation nor as a universal explanandum to which every biological explanation must be reduced. It offers organised persistence as a candidate comparative target: a way of asking whether the distinctive achievements, relationships, and limits of biological explanations become clearer when considered in relation to the continuity of living organisation through change. Its value must be established by the explanatory contribution that comparison actually makes.

## Explanatory Architecture

This article develops the following explanatory architecture:

**Biological explanatory plurality**  
→ biology contains multiple successful explanatory approaches directed toward different questions and phenomena

**The comparison problem**  
→ explanatory diversity raises the question of how different explanatory achievements can be related without reducing them to a single theory, method, or vocabulary

**Living organisation through change**  
→ organised persistence identifies the continuity of living organisation through change

**The APS comparative proposal**  
→ organised persistence is treated as a candidate comparative target rather than assumed to be the universal explanandum of biology

**Agency, Process, and Scale**  
→ provide complementary analytic projections through which the living organisation relevant to a comparison can be investigated

**Comparator control**  
→ established explanations must first be understood according to their own explanatory targets, strengths, and scope

**Explanatory contribution**  
→ APS contributes only where comparison through organised persistence clarifies relationships, distinctions, unresolved questions, or possible lines of investigation beyond what the strongest established explanation already provides

**Failure condition**  
→ if comparison merely redescribes established biological explanations in APS terminology, no explanatory gain has been established

The architecture therefore moves from **explanatory plurality to controlled comparison**, not from plurality to theoretical unification. Organised persistence provides a proposed point of comparison whose value must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

**In summary:**

> **Biology's explanatory diversity does not require unification, but it does invite comparison. APS succeeds only where that comparison makes an additional explanatory contribution.**

## Introduction - What This Article Does

The Agency–Process–Scale framework begins with a particular account of life. It treats living systems as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisations whose continued existence depends on maintaining the conditions of their own persistence. From that starting point, APS develops an explanatory architecture organised around three complementary analytic projections: Agency, Process, and Scale.

That move needs to be made carefully. A definition of life does not by itself explain particular biological phenomena. Nor does the APS architecture determine in advance which variables, mechanisms, dependencies, spatial extents, or temporal relations matter in any particular case. Moving from an account of living organisation to biological explanation therefore requires additional steps.

APS can organise inquiry by relating a specified biological explanandum to the activity sustaining viability, the processes through which that activity occurs, and the spatial and temporal extents across which persistence is organised. These are not three independent causes of biological phenomena. They are analytic projections of one living organisation, and their relevance depends on the explanatory problem.

The distinction between organising explanation and providing biological warrant is fundamental. APS can help specify what is being asked, expose relationships that merit investigation, and connect otherwise separated explanatory questions. Whether those relationships actually obtain in a biological system is a further matter. That requires evidence.

**Where This Article Fits**

*This article occupies the transition between the conceptual foundations of APS and its comparative methodology of explanation.*

*[What Is APS?](/orientation/what-is-aps/)* introduces the framework and its basic commitments. The foundational articles examine life, biological organisation, Agency, Process, Scale, and Organised Persistence in greater detail. This article asks the next question: **if those commitments are provisionally adopted, what follows for explanatory inquiry?***

*The answer is not that APS supplies completed explanations in advance. Its architecture helps move from an account of living organisation towards specified explanatory questions and candidate biological dependencies. The methodology articles then take over, asking how such claims should be tested, compared with existing explanations, and assessed for explanatory gain.*

## From an Account of Life to an Explanatory Problem

APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

Within APS, this identifies the kind of organisation from which explanatory inquiry begins. Living systems persist only while organised activities continue to maintain, repair, regulate, reproduce, or re-establish conditions compatible with continued existence.

This starting point has important affinities with organisational approaches to biology, particularly work on biological autonomy and closure of constraints (Moreno and Mossio 2015; Montévil and Mossio 2015). These traditions provide independent scientific and philosophical context for treating living systems as organised and self-maintaining rather than as static collections of components. They do not, however, independently establish the complete APS account of life or its explanatory architecture.

Living systems are materially realised. Their components interact through particular causal structures and biological processes. Molecular, mechanistic, physiological, developmental, ecological, evolutionary, and other domain-specific explanations therefore retain their own explanatory roles. APS does not replace them. It asks whether particular explanatory questions can sometimes be related more explicitly to the organised persistence of the living system being investigated.

## Why Living Organisation Places Demands on Explanation

Once life is treated as organised persistence rather than material constancy, APS asks how inquiry into such an organisation should be structured.

Living systems remain organised while undergoing continual change. Components turn over, physiological states fluctuate, development transforms the organism, environments change, and lineages persist through reproduction and inheritance. An explanatory architecture concerned with such systems must therefore be capable of asking about activity, transformation, and the spatial and temporal organisation of persistence.

Within APS, Agency, Process, and Scale provide those complementary perspectives. This is an architectural proposal, not a claim that every biological explanation must take the same form.

The distinction is important. APS's account of life motivates explanatory questions; it does not determine their answers.

## Agency, Process, and Scale as Analytic Projections

**Agency** concerns the viability-oriented activity through which living organisation sustains conditions of persistence.

**Process** concerns how organised biological change occurs.

**Scale** concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

These are complementary analytic projections of one organisation. They are not components into which an organism can be decomposed, stages through which explanation must proceed, hierarchical levels, or independent causal powers.

A particular explanandum may foreground one projection, require two, or require relations involving all three. Their relevance must be established for the explanatory target rather than assumed from the APS architecture itself.

This is the first boundary between architecture and biological explanation: **APS supplies questions and distinctions; biological investigation determines which are relevant and what answers they receive.**

For the detailed APS treatment of spatial and temporal extent, see [Scale — How Life Is Coordinated Across Space and Time](/articles/scale-how-life-is-coordinated-across-space-and-time/).

## An Explanatory Architecture, Not an Explanatory Grammar

APS therefore does not provide a universal explanatory grammar into which every biological phenomenon must be fitted.

Its architecture performs a more limited function. It helps ask whether a specified phenomenon is adequately understood in relation to viability-oriented activity, biological change, and the spatial and temporal organisation of persistence.

That distinction protects explanatory plurality. A molecular mechanism does not become incomplete merely because it can be redescribed in APS terminology. A physiological explanation need not invoke every APS projection. An evolutionary explanation does not become an APS explanation simply because persistence across generations can be identified.

The explanatory target comes first.

Only after that target has been specified can APS ask which projections, relationships, and dependencies are relevant to explaining it.

## From Architecture to Domain-Specific Explananda

An APS investigation should therefore begin by specifying the **explanandum** rather than by searching for Agency, Process, and Scale.

The target might concern maintenance of a physiological variable, regeneration after injury, developmental stability, behavioural adjustment, persistence under environmental change, or another biological phenomenon. Different explananda legitimately require different explanatory resources.

Once the target is clear, APS can ask more discriminating questions. Which activities make a viability-relevant difference to the phenomenon? Through what processes is the relevant change produced or maintained? Across which spatial and temporal extents is the organisation required for the phenomenon realised?

These questions can organise investigation without predetermining its outcome.

The sequence is therefore:

**specified explanandum → relevant analytic projection(s) → candidate explanatory relationships**

rather than:

**Agency → Process → Scale → explanation.**

## Relations, Dependencies, and Material Organisation

An explanatory architecture can identify relationships worth investigating. It cannot establish those relationships merely by representing them.

Suppose an APS analysis suggests that a biological phenomenon depends upon interactions extending across cellular, tissue, organismal, or temporal extents. That suggestion becomes biologically explanatory only when the relevant relations can be specified materially.

What interacts with what? Through which processes? Under what conditions? Over what timescale? What would be expected to change if the proposed dependency were altered? Contemporary causal and mechanistic approaches already provide powerful resources for addressing questions of this kind (Machamer, Darden, and Craver 2000; Woodward 2003).

APS does not replace those resources. Its possible contribution is to help identify dependencies that matter because of their relation to the organised persistence of the system under investigation.

The same restriction applies across the three projections. Calling an activity viability-oriented identifies its APS significance but does not specify its material mechanism. Identifying a process does not establish its contribution to persistence. Likewise, the occurrence of phenomena at different scales does not establish causal coupling between them.

Scale itself does not coordinate or cause biological activity. Such work is performed by materially realised interactions, constraints, and processes.

The explanatory sequence must therefore proceed beyond architecture:

**candidate relationship → specified biological dependency → material implementation → evidence.**

## Continuity Across Biological Time and Scale

A concept linking the APS account of life to this explanatory architecture is **Organised Persistence**.

Living systems persist through change. Their material constituents turn over, their states fluctuate, and their developmental and environmental circumstances alter. Persistence therefore does not ordinarily mean preservation of an identical material state. It concerns continuity of living organisation through change.

Within APS, Organised Persistence helps organise questions about what is maintained, restored, transformed, or reproduced; through which processes this occurs; and across which spatial and temporal extents the relevant organisation persists.

This gives the concept an explanandum-organising and integrative role. It does not make Organised Persistence an additional causal entity, nor does it establish that explanations formulated through it are superior to mechanistic, physiological, developmental, ecological, or evolutionary explanations.

Its value must instead be assessed in relation to particular explanatory targets.

For the fuller treatment, see [Persistence — Organised Continuity Through Time](/articles/persistence-organised-continuity-through-time/).

## Architecture Does Not Guarantee Explanation

This boundary becomes especially important when APS moves from conceptual architecture to empirical investigation.

A proposed dependency must be supported by evidence appropriate to the kind of relation being claimed. Causal, mechanistic, organisational, developmental, historical, and other dependencies need not require identical forms of evidence.

Perturbation can provide evidence about a proposed dependency when the intervention and resulting change bear appropriately on that dependency. Such evidence must still be interpreted relative to the claim, experimental design, and relevant alternatives.

This logic is not uniquely APS. The possible APS contribution lies in helping identify which dependencies merit explicit investigation because of their relation to organised persistence.

But a successful APS redescription is not yet a successful APS explanation.

If APS reorganises established knowledge without revealing a relevant dependency, resolving an explanatory difficulty, generating a useful discrimination, or otherwise changing what can be explained, its contribution may be conceptual, integrative, methodological, or heuristic rather than substantively explanatory.

Those are legitimate contributions. They should not be confused with explanatory gain.

## From Explanatory Architecture to Comparative Method

Biology is explanatorily plural. Different fields address different questions using different methods and explanatory resources, and relations among those explanations need not be simple (Marchionni 2008; Gijsbers 2016).

APS can investigate whether explanations directed at different aspects of living systems can be related through their bearing on organised persistence. Such integration can be scientifically useful, but integration is not automatically explanatory gain.

Showing that APS can describe or organise a phenomenon is therefore insufficient. Where additional explanatory capacity is claimed, the relevant question becomes comparative:

**What does the APS-guided account contribute relative to the strongest relevant alternative addressing the same explanatory target?**

Target matching comes first. Approaches directed at different explananda should not be ranked as though they answered the same question, and a weak or obsolete comparator cannot establish a meaningful advantage.

The detailed comparison procedure belongs to [How Should Scientific Explanations Be Compared?](/articles/how-should-scientific-explanations-be-compared/). The important point here is architectural: **APS explanation becomes a comparative question once explanatory gain is claimed.**

## APS as a Research Programme

The transition from life to explanation therefore changes the scientific role of APS.

A framework can remain principally conceptual if it organises ideas. A research programme must expose those ideas to investigation.

For APS, the resulting sequence is:

**living organisation → specified explanandum → relevant Agency–Process–Scale projection(s) → proposed dependency → material implementation → evidence → strongest relevant comparator where required → bounded result**

Not every investigation will require every projection. Not every proposed dependency will survive testing. Not every comparison will reveal explanatory gain.

Negative and null findings are therefore informative. They can show where APS contributes clarification or integration without additional explanatory capacity, where an established explanation is already sufficient, where a proposed dependency lacks support, or where an APS claim requires restriction or revision.

The scientific standing of APS cannot be established simply by extending its vocabulary into additional areas of biology. It depends upon the cumulative results of controlled investigations.

For the methodology of framework testing, see [How Should a New Scientific Framework Be Tested?](/articles/how-should-a-new-scientific-framework-be-tested/).

## What Would Count as Explanatory Gain?

APS treats explanatory gain as **target-relative and comparator-relative**.

Conceptual clarification, integration, methodological guidance, and heuristic organisation can all be genuine scientific contributions. None automatically establishes substantive explanatory superiority.

For a specified target, stronger explanatory gain would require showing that an APS-guided analysis adds relevant explanatory capacity relative to an appropriately strong alternative—for example by identifying and supporting a dependency required for the target that the comparator does not capture.

A null result is equally legitimate. If the strongest comparator already explains the target and APS contributes only redescription, no additional explanatory gain has been demonstrated for that target.

Neither result should automatically be generalised to the framework as a whole.

The full criteria are developed in [What Counts as Explanatory Gain?](/articles/what-counts-as-explanatory-gain/), while [What Happens When APS Is Tested Against Its Strongest Comparators?](/articles/what-happens-when-aps-is-tested-against-its-strongest-comparators/) shows how proposition-specific comparison can produce different outcomes.

## A Bounded Example: Cognition

Cognition illustrates the transition from architecture to biological warrant particularly clearly.

Within APS, cognition is not synonymous with life, biological agency, or regulation. The framework proposes a more specific boundary involving the integration of biological significance across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities.

The important point here is methodological rather than a full account of cognition. APS architecture can formulate a candidate distinction. Biological cases must then establish whether the proposed dependencies are materially realised and whether an adequate non-cognitive explanation is sufficient.

Cognition therefore exemplifies the larger sequence:

**architectural distinction → biological application → discriminating evidence → comparative assessment.**

The substantive cognition architecture and its empirical boundary are treated in [Cognition — Where Does It Belong in Biology?](/articles/cognition-where-does-it-belong-in-biology/).
