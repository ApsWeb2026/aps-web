Information is one of the most widely used and ambiguously defined concepts in biology. Genes, nervous systems, signalling pathways, and ecological interactions are all commonly described informationally.

APS distinguishes carefully between:
- statistical information,
- signalling relations,
- biological meaning,
- and viability-oriented organisation.

In Shannon information theory, information concerns uncertainty reduction and signal structure. APS accepts the usefulness of these formal frameworks while rejecting the assumption that statistical information alone explains biological meaning or life itself.

In APS, information becomes biologically significant only within systems already organised around viability-oriented persistence.

A difference counts as informational for a system only if it participates in:
- evaluation,
- regulation,
- semiosis,
- or persistence-maintaining activity.

For example:
- a nutrient gradient matters to a bacterium because it affects viability,
- hormonal signals matter because they regulate persistence-supporting activity,
- and neural signalling matters because it contributes to coordinated organismal organisation.

Information is therefore not foundational in APS.

Evaluation precedes information because systems must first differentiate between viability-supporting and viability-undermining conditions.

Semiosis precedes representation because differences become biologically meaningful before they become symbolically or representationally structured.

APS therefore treats information as organisationally derivative rather than ontologically primary.

Living systems do not exist because information flows through them.

Rather, information becomes possible because living systems already exist as viability-oriented, persistence-maintaining organisations.

Information must therefore be understood through the broader organisational grammar governing biological explanation:
- agency,
- process,
- scale,
- viability,
- evaluation,
- semiosis,
- cognition,
- and persistence.
