## Conventional framing

Developmental plasticity is often understood as the capacity of organisms to produce different developmental outcomes under different environmental conditions.

Classical approaches frequently emphasise flexibility, environmental responsiveness, or phenotypic variability. Plasticity may therefore be treated as a modification of an otherwise relatively fixed developmental programme.

## APS reframing

APS interprets developmental plasticity in relation to developmental organisation rather than treating it merely as phenotypic variation.

Plasticity concerns regulated differences in developmental trajectories or outcomes associated with differences in internal or external conditions. These differences may involve morphology, physiology, behaviour, developmental timing, or other features of living organisation, depending on the biological system concerned.

Plastic responses can contribute to viability under particular conditions, but viability preservation is not part of the definition of plasticity. Plastic responses may be beneficial, neutral, costly, maladaptive, or incompatible with continued viability. Their significance must therefore be established for the biological case concerned.

Developmental plasticity likewise need not involve every possible form of physiological, behavioural, ecological, or morphogenetic change. The relevant developmental reorganisation and the conditions to which it responds must be identified for the target under investigation.

## Plasticity and Organised Persistence

Developmental plasticity can contribute to organised persistence when alternative developmental trajectories or outcomes help maintain or restore viable organisation under changing conditions.

This relation is not universal. Living systems need not depend on plasticity for every form of persistence, and the occurrence of a plastic response does not establish that the response preserves viability.

Plasticity may nevertheless provide target-specific explanatory information about how viable organisation is maintained under particular perturbations or environmental conditions. Where it does so, its contribution to organised persistence should be demonstrated from the biological case rather than inferred from plasticity itself.

Developmental plasticity is therefore neither opposed to stability nor intrinsically a mechanism of persistence. Its explanatory relation to stability and persistence depends on the developmental system and target concerned.

## Plasticity and Regulation

Developmental plasticity and developmental regulation are related but non-identical concepts.

Regulatory processes may enable, constrain, coordinate, or stabilise plastic developmental responses. Plasticity, in turn, concerns conditional differences in developmental trajectories or outcomes rather than regulation as such.

In some systems, regulation may support plastic reorganisation under changing conditions. In others, regulatory processes may canalise development or restrict the range of possible responses. The relation between regulation and plasticity must therefore be established for the particular developmental phenomenon rather than treated as a universal continuity-maintaining partnership.

## Plasticity and Ecology

Environmental and ecological conditions can be important sources of variation to which developmental systems respond.

Temperature, nutrition, resource availability, interactions with other organisms, social conditions, or other environmental differences may contribute to plastic developmental outcomes in particular systems. Some plastic responses may therefore require organism–environment relations for their explanation.

Developmental plasticity is not, however, inherently ecological, nor does a plastic response necessarily arise through organism–environment coupling. Relevant conditions may also be internal, and environmental influence alone does not establish that the environment forms part of the developmental organisation producing the response.

The explanatory extent should therefore include ecological relations when those relations are required to explain the plastic developmental outcome concerned.

## Plasticity and Evolution

Developmental plasticity can be evolutionarily significant, but its evolutionary significance must be established for the evolutionary case concerned.

Plastic developmental responses can affect phenotypic expression and may alter relations between organisms and their environments. Variation in plasticity can also become relevant to inheritance, selection, adaptation, or evolutionary change where the required population-level and historical relations are demonstrated.

These possibilities do not establish a general developmental sequence from plasticity to adaptation or evolutionary transformation. Nor does plasticity by itself connect development, ecology, adaptation, and evolutionary continuity.

APS therefore treats developmental plasticity as a possible contributor to evolutionary explanation rather than as an intrinsic evolutionary mechanism or universal bridge between biological domains.

## Key Point

Developmental plasticity is the regulated capacity to produce different developmental trajectories or outcomes in response to differences in conditions. Its consequences for viability, organised persistence, ecology, adaptation, or evolution must be established for the biological case concerned.
