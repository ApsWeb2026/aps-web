Meaning is often treated as dependent upon:
- language,
- symbolic representation,
- or conceptual interpretation.

APS rejects this assumption.

In biological systems, meaning begins more fundamentally through viability-oriented organisation itself.

Differences become meaningful when they matter to:
- persistence,
- regulation,
- organisational coherence,
- or viability-oriented activity.

A nutrient gradient matters to a bacterium because it affects persistence.

A stress signal matters to a plant because it alters viability conditions.

An immune signal matters because it contributes to distinguishing tolerated from damaging states.

Meaning therefore emerges through evaluation before symbolic representation or linguistic cognition arise.

APS treats semiosis as the process through which differences become biologically meaningful within evaluative organisation.

Meaning is thus grounded in:
- evaluation,
- semiosis,
- regulation,
- and persistence-maintaining activity.

This allows APS to distinguish biological meaning from:
- Shannon information,
- symbolic encoding,
- abstract representation,
- or formal signal transmission alone.

Information may participate in meaningful biological organisation, but informational structure alone does not generate biological significance.

Meaning arises because living systems exist as viability-oriented organisations for which some conditions matter differentially relative to persistence.

APS therefore treats meaning as:
- organisationally grounded,
- biologically objective in principle,
- and prior to explicit representation.

Representation, language, and symbolic cognition may extend meaning into more sophisticated forms, but they do not create meaning from nothing.

Meaning must therefore be understood through the broader organisational grammar governing biological explanation:
- agency,
- process,
- scale,
- viability,
- evaluation,
- semiosis,
- cognition,
- and persistence.
