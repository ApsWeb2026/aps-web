## Conventional Framing

A constraint is often understood as a limitation imposed upon a system. In physics and mathematics, constraints are commonly treated as boundary conditions or restrictions that reduce the possible behaviour of a process.

Under this framing, constraints may be:

- externally specified
- relatively fixed
- understood primarily as limitations

This understanding is useful in many scientific contexts, but it does not fully capture the role constraints play in living systems.

## The APS Reframing

In APS, a constraint is a materially realised organisational relation that selectively alters the possibilities available to ongoing activity without fully determining outcomes.

Constraints therefore do not merely restrict activity. They can enable organised processes by limiting, channelling, or stabilising some possibilities while leaving others available.

A membrane alters the possibilities for exchange. Enzymes alter the possibilities and rates of chemical transformation. Vascular organisation alters the conditions under which transport occurs. Behavioural organisation can alter the conditions under which an organism interacts with its environment.

Constraints are therefore neither external rules imposed upon biological matter nor complete determinants of what happens.

They are materially realised relations through which the possibilities of ongoing activity are selectively structured.

## Constraints and Biological Organisation

Living organisation depends upon materially realised relations that constrain ongoing activity.

Such constraints can contribute to:

- stabilising activity
- coordinating processes
- maintaining enabling conditions
- regulating transformation
- and preserving possibilities for continued functioning

Their explanatory significance lies not simply in the existence of structures or components, but in what those materially realised relations do within an organisation.

APS therefore asks how particular constraints alter the local conditions under which biological processes occur and how those relations participate in the organisation whose persistence is being explained.

This does not mean that every constraint necessarily contributes successfully to viability.

Constraints may be altered, degraded, dysfunctional, insufficient, or organised in ways that impair continued functioning. Their biological significance must therefore be assessed relative to the organisation and explanandum under investigation.

## Constraints Are Dynamically Maintained

In living systems, many biologically important constraints are themselves produced, maintained, repaired, modified, or re-established through ongoing activity.

Cell membranes require continuing maintenance. Enzymatic systems depend upon material and energetic processes. Physiological organisation is continually sustained despite turnover and perturbation. Behavioural organisation may alter conditions relevant to continued functioning.

Constraints therefore cannot generally be understood as static structures imposed upon otherwise passive processes.

Processes may contribute to maintaining constraints, while those constraints alter the possibilities available to the processes through which they are maintained.

This reciprocal dependence is central to understanding constraint closure.

It also shows why the explanatory role of a constraint cannot be captured solely by describing its structure at a single moment.

## Constraints Across Spatial and Temporal Extents

Biological constraints are materially realised across different spatial and temporal extents.

A constraint operating locally can alter the conditions under which nearby processes occur. Those local conditions may themselves depend upon organisation realised over wider spatial extents or maintained over longer durations.

APS does not interpret these relations as a hierarchy of biological levels or as wider domains superseding local causation.

Rather, materially implemented constraints alter the local conditions under which biological activity occurs, while the organisation relevant to persistence may extend across different spatial and temporal extents.

Scale therefore concerns **where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents**.

The explanatory relevance of a particular extent depends upon the biological target under investigation. No spatial or temporal domain possesses automatic explanatory priority.

## Constraint Closure

Constraint closure concerns relations of mutual dependence among constraints within an organisation.

In a constraint-closed organisation, constraints participate in maintaining conditions relevant to the continued operation or regeneration of other constraints, while themselves depending upon constrained processes and organisational relations for their continued realisation.

Closure does not mean material or energetic isolation from the environment. Living systems remain materially and energetically open.

Nor should constraint closure be treated as an independently sufficient criterion for life, biological agency, normativity, function, or organised persistence.

Within APS, life is **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

Constraint closure identifies an indispensable organisational relation within that living organisation. Closure considered abstractly does not exhaust the explanation of what makes the organisation biological or how its persistence is maintained through change.

## Constraints and Normativity

Constraints participate in biological organisation under conditions that are asymmetric with respect to viability.

Some changes remain compatible with continued functioning. Others impair, degrade, or terminate the organisation through which persistence is maintained.

APS understands biological normativity in terms of this **viability-relative asymmetry**, rather than external judgement, mental evaluation, or imposed goals.

Constraints can be biologically consequential because altering them changes the possibilities available to ongoing activity and may thereby affect conditions relevant to continued functioning.

A damaged membrane, disrupted metabolic relation, or failed regulatory process can matter biologically because of its consequences for the organisation's viability.

Constraints do not independently generate normativity.

Their normative significance arises within viability-oriented living organisation, where differences in activity and condition can have different consequences for continued functioning and persistence.

## Constraints and Organised Persistence

Constraint relations contribute to explaining how living organisation continues despite material turnover, environmental change, perturbation, and transformation.

Their contribution is not that they preserve an unchanged state.

Rather, materially realised constraints can maintain, alter, or re-establish the conditions under which ongoing activity remains possible.

This makes constraints important to the APS account of organised persistence: the continuity of living organisation through change.

Constraint relations are therefore part of the organisation through which persistence is achieved, not independent causes operating upon an otherwise separate biological system.

Their explanatory contribution must be established relative to the particular organisation, process, spatial or temporal extent, and explanandum under investigation.

## Summary

In APS, a constraint is a materially realised organisational relation that selectively alters the possibilities available to ongoing activity without fully determining outcomes.

Constraints can limit, channel, stabilise, or enable activity by changing the conditions under which processes occur.

In living systems, biologically important constraints are often dynamically maintained through the same organisation within which they operate. Relations of mutual dependence among such constraints are central to constraint closure.

Constraint closure is indispensable to the APS account of living organisation, but closure considered abstractly does not by itself establish life, biological agency, normativity, or organised persistence.

APS therefore situates constraints within one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation whose ongoing activity maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence across spatial and temporal extents.
