**Where This Article Fits**

*This article belongs to the **History of Ideas** topic within the Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework. It examines how ecology transformed biological explanation through the development of distinct concepts, methods, and investigative traditions.*

*It complements *Aristotle and the Foundations of Biology*, *Theophrastus and the Foundations of Plant Science*, *and the History of Ideas articles on evolution, scientific knowledge, and technology. It distinguishes the emergence of academic ecology from earlier and independently developed ecological understanding, including Indigenous knowledge traditions considered on their own terms.*

*Its primary contribution is historical rather than theoretical. By examining changes in ecological explanation and disagreements about biological organisation, it provides context for APS questions concerning material processes, explanatory units, and scale, without suggesting that ecology anticipated APS or demonstrating APS's explanatory superiority.*

## Introduction: What Did Ecology Change?

Living organisms exist through relationships with their surroundings. Plants depend upon light, water, nutrients, and other organisms, while animals encounter environments structured by food availability, climatic conditions, habitat characteristics, and interactions within and among species. Human societies have long developed sophisticated understandings of these relationships through observation, cultivation, hunting, gathering, environmental management, and the transmission of knowledge across generations. Ecology did not discover the dependence of organisms upon their environments, nor was academic science the first setting in which such relationships were systematically understood.

Ecology's historical significance lies instead in the development of diverse scientific traditions that made relationships among organisms and their environments increasingly explicit objects of investigation, comparison, measurement, experimentation, modelling, and causal explanation. This development was neither uniform nor inevitable. Natural history, plant geography, evolutionary biology, physiology, population studies, animal ecology, and investigations of energy and material circulation contributed different questions and methods, sometimes reinforcing one another and sometimes producing substantial disagreements about what should be explained and how.

The history of ecology is consequently better understood as a changing constellation of investigative practices and explanatory commitments than as a succession of discoveries culminating in one comprehensive understanding of nature. The distinction matters because describing an organism's occurrence in a particular environment is not equivalent to explaining its distribution; identifying a regularity in population change does not necessarily reveal the mechanisms responsible; and recognising interactions among species does not establish that the resulting community constitutes an integrated biological individual. These questions became increasingly explicit as ecology developed, although different ecological traditions answered them in different ways.

The central historical question is therefore how ecological investigation changed what could be observed, tested, and explained. Answering it requires attention both to the methods that opened new possibilities for investigation and to the disagreements that exposed their limitations.

## Ecological Knowledge Before Ecology

Knowledge of organism–environment relationships long preceded ecology's emergence as a recognisable academic discipline. Agricultural, pastoral, hunting, and gathering practices have depended upon detailed understandings of seasonal variation, soils, water, vegetation, animal movements, and the consequences of environmental intervention. Such knowledge could be refined through repeated observation, practical experimentation, and intergenerational transmission, although its purposes, organisation, and standards of justification varied across societies.

Ancient natural history provides one documented tradition in which environmental relationships received sustained attention. Aristotle examined animals in relation to their habitats, habits, reproduction, and ways of life, while Theophrastus investigated differences among plants associated with locality, soil, climate, cultivation, and other conditions. These investigations established questions that later ecologists would also find important, but they should not be represented as modern ecology in an undeveloped form. Their explanatory categories and intellectual purposes belonged to different historical settings, and their significance is not exhausted by their resemblance to subsequent scientific practices.

Indigenous ecological knowledge requires an equally careful historical distinction. Such knowledge is not simply an early stage of academic ecology, nor a collection of observations awaiting scientific validation. It can involve sustained relationships among environmental understanding, practical responsibility, cultural continuity, and obligations to particular places. The Australian examples considered here illustrate the importance of these relationships, without representing the diversity of Indigenous knowledge traditions globally.

Victor Steffensen's Fire Country (2020), for example, presents cultural burning through an Indigenous account of environmental understanding, responsibility, and relationships with Country. Research involving Martu collaborators has investigated how landscape burning interacts with vegetation succession and habitat heterogeneity, while also recognising that these practices belong to cultural and historical relationships extending beyond their measurable ecological effects (Bird et al. 2016). The significance of Martu burning therefore cannot be reduced to the production of a particular vegetation pattern or landscape mosaic.

Bawaka Country and colleagues (2015) develop a related but distinct challenge to conventional research assumptions. Their collaborative account places relationships with Country at the centre of knowledge production and questions the separation of human investigators from the environments they study. The argument is methodological and ontological as well as ecological: Country is not treated merely as a physical setting containing objects available for external observation. This work should be considered on its own terms rather than translated into the language of ecosystems or contemporary organisational biology.

These examples make it necessary to distinguish the history of ecological understanding from the narrower institutional history of academic ecology. The distinction does not imply that the two histories were isolated. Scientific institutions have interacted with other knowledge traditions through collaboration, exchange, exclusion, and appropriation, and these relationships form part of the history of how ecological knowledge acquired authority. Nevertheless, identifying the emergence of academic ecology requires attention to its particular concepts, methods, and institutions, without making those developments the measure of all earlier or contemporary environmental understanding.

## Natural History, Plant Geography, and Environmental Relationships

The expansion of European natural history brought increasingly extensive records of organisms, their geographical distributions, and the conditions under which they occurred. Exploration, collecting, botanical gardens, herbaria, correspondence networks, and natural-historical publications enlarged the geographical scope of comparison, although these practices were frequently embedded in imperial and colonial relationships. The resulting collections contributed to taxonomy and biogeography, but the accumulation of specimens was not itself sufficient to explain why particular organisms occurred in some places and not others.

Humboldt and Bonpland's *Essai sur la géographie des plantes* (1805) was an important contribution to the comparative investigation of vegetation and environmental conditions. By coordinating geographical observation, environmental measurement, and visual representation, their work made patterns of plant distribution more accessible to systematic comparison and causal investigation. Nevertheless, identifying relationships between vegetation and environmental gradients did not establish every mechanism responsible for the observed distributions. The distinction between representing a pattern and explaining its production would remain important throughout the development of ecology.

The history of botanical exploration in Australia illustrates a related development, while also revealing the unequal circumstances under which scientific knowledge was assembled. European collectors and colonial botanical institutions produced extensive records of plants and their localities, contributing to systematic botany and the study of geographical distributions. Their work nevertheless took place in environments already known and managed through Indigenous traditions, and the authority to classify, interpret, and publish environmental knowledge was not equally distributed. A satisfactory history must therefore examine not only the information accumulated by botanical institutions, but also the social and political circumstances of its production.

Natural history and plant geography helped establish important observational and comparative foundations for ecology. They did not, however, form a single continuous route to modern ecological science. Subsequent developments involved new research questions, specialised methods, institutional changes, and disagreements about the appropriate subjects of biological explanation.

## Ecology Becomes a Scientific Discipline

During the nineteenth century, developments in evolutionary theory, physiology, field investigation, and scientific specialisation created new opportunities for studying organism–environment relationships. Ernst Haeckel's formulation of Ökologie in Generelle Morphologie der Organismen (1866) is an important landmark in the history of ecological terminology, linking relationships among organisms and their surroundings to the wider explanatory concerns of evolutionary biology. The appearance of a term, however, did not establish a fully formed discipline. Ecology acquired its institutional and methodological identity through a more extended and uneven history of research programmes, teaching, professional organisation, publication, and scientific disagreement.

### Warming and the comparative study of plant communities

Johannes Eugenius Warming's *Plantesamfund* (1895) was an important contribution to the systematic study of plant communities. His ecological investigations subsequently reached a wider English-language readership through *Oecology of Plants* (1909). Warming examined vegetation in relation to environmental conditions, plant adaptations, habitats, and recurrent forms of association, bringing together questions previously pursued through plant geography, morphology, and natural history.

The significance of this work was not merely that it described plants in different habitats. It helped establish comparative investigation of plant communities as a coherent scientific undertaking in which the relationships between vegetation and environmental conditions could be examined across different settings. The history of the work's publication and translation also illustrates the international circulation of ecological ideas, rather than a development confined to one national tradition.

### Cowles and the dynamics of vegetation

Henry Chandler Cowles's 1899 investigation of the vegetation of the Lake Michigan sand dunes provides a particularly instructive example of changing field methods. Cowles examined vegetation in relation to dune topography, exposure, sand movement, and environmental change, treating plant associations not as fixed features of a landscape but as formations undergoing transformation. By comparing different dune environments, he sought to understand the processes through which vegetation became established, changed, and was replaced.

This approach made succession a historical and causal problem susceptible to systematic field investigation. Its methodological importance also lay in the difficulties it exposed. Differences among sites may suggest a sequence of development, but spatial arrangements do not automatically establish temporal histories. Such an inference requires assumptions about the comparability of sites, their environmental conditions, and the processes through which their vegetation developed. Cowles's work thus illustrates both the explanatory possibilities of ecological field investigation and the need to distinguish observed patterns from reconstructed processes.

Taken together, Warming and Cowles demonstrate that ecology's development involved changes in how vegetation was investigated, not simply an increase in descriptive information. Plant communities and their environmental relationships became subjects of comparative, dynamic, and increasingly causal inquiry, although the nature and degree of integration within those communities remained open to disagreement.


## Populations, Communities, and Mathematical Explanation

The development of mathematical population ecology introduced new ways of investigating relationships among population growth, competition, predation, and resource availability. Alfred Lotka's *Elements of Physical Biology* (1925) and Vito Volterra's mathematical investigations, including his 1926 analysis of interacting species, demonstrated how assumptions about population processes could be expressed formally and their consequences examined. Their contributions were closely related, although the historical relationship between them was not one of simple collaboration or a single shared discovery. In a published exchange in 1927, Lotka drew attention to his earlier treatment of overlapping mathematical results, and Volterra acknowledged that priority while maintaining the significance of his own investigations.

The explanatory value of these approaches lay partly in making assumptions explicit and deriving consequences that were not immediately apparent from descriptive observation. Mathematical models could identify conditions under which particular population trajectories were possible, but the validity of their formal results did not establish that the same assumptions adequately represented every natural population. As Sharon Kingsland's historical account demonstrates, mathematical modelling and field-based ecological investigation developed through complex interactions and disagreements. The history of Lotka and Volterra consequently illustrates both the possibilities of formal ecological explanation and the importance of distinguishing mathematical derivation from empirical adequacy.

A different controversy concerned the organisation of ecological communities. Frederic Clements's *Plant Succession* (1916) developed a strongly developmental interpretation of vegetation change, emphasising recurrent sequences and the organised character of plant communities. Henry Allan Gleason's "The Individualistic Concept of the Plant Association" (1926) challenged the inference that recurrent plant associations necessarily constituted sharply bounded, organism-like units. His alternative emphasised individual species' environmental responses, opportunities for dispersal, and historical circumstances in explaining the composition of vegetation.

The significance of this disagreement was not simply that one author favoured communities while the other favoured individual organisms. Both addressed patterns of vegetation, but they differed over the processes and organisational relationships needed to explain those patterns. Clements's approach directed attention towards regularities in succession and community development, whereas Gleason questioned whether such regularities justified attributing strong unity to the associations concerned. Their disagreement exposed a fundamental problem that continues to arise in biology: the existence of a recurrent pattern does not, without further evidence, establish the kind of material integration responsible for it.

Neither interpretation should be made into a universal account of ecological communities. The historical importance of the controversy lies in the competing explanatory questions it made explicit and in the distinction it drew between observable associations and the organisational claims used to interpret them.

## Animal Ecology and the Ecological Niche

Plant ecology was only one of the traditions through which ecological explanation developed. Animal ecology brought questions of habitat requirements, feeding relationships, behaviour, population interactions, and the functional positions of organisms within communities into more sustained investigation. The changing uses of the ecological niche concept provide a particularly revealing example of how ecological ideas could organise different explanatory problems without acquiring a single, permanently settled meaning.

Joseph Grinnell's study of the California thrasher (1917) connected the occurrence of a species with habitat conditions and ecological relationships affecting its distribution. His work became an influential reference point for investigations of the circumstances under which organisms occur and persist, although it should not be described as the first recognition of such relationships or as the uncontested origin of the niche concept.

Charles Elton's *Animal Ecology* (1927) placed greater emphasis upon feeding relationships and the functional positions of organisms within ecological communities. The contrast with Grinnell is useful, provided it is not exaggerated: environmental requirements, species distributions, and biological interactions are often inseparable in actual ecological investigation. What differed was the explanatory emphasis placed upon particular relationships.

G. Evelyn Hutchinson's "Concluding Remarks" (1957) introduced an influential formal treatment of ecological conditions through multidimensional representation. This approach made it possible to express questions about environmental suitability and population persistence in more explicitly quantitative terms, while also distinguishing potential conditions from those realised in the presence of ecological interactions and constraints. Its significance was not the replacement of every earlier niche conception, but the development of a formal representation suited to particular kinds of ecological analysis.

The history of the niche therefore illustrates both the productivity and the ambiguity of ecological concepts. A concept may connect investigations of distribution, environmental requirements, functional roles, and persistence without those investigations becoming identical. Historical differences among Grinnell, Elton, and Hutchinson should consequently be understood as changes in explanatory emphasis and formalisation, rather than as a simple succession of definitions.

## Experimentation and the Testing of Ecological Models

The growing use of controlled experimentation provided opportunities to investigate ecological relationships under conditions in which selected variables could be manipulated and their consequences observed. G. F. Gause's investigations during the 1930s were particularly important because they connected mathematical accounts of population interaction with experimental studies of competition and predation. His *The Struggle for Existence* (1934), together with contemporary experimental publications, examined the behaviour of microorganisms under specified laboratory conditions and assessed the relationship between observed population changes and theoretical expectations.

Experiments involving species of *Paramecium* investigated the consequences of growing populations separately and in mixed cultures. The results demonstrated that competitive outcomes depended upon the biological characteristics of the organisms and the circumstances of their interaction, including growth conditions and the effects of the surrounding medium. Collaborative research by Gause, Nastukova, and Alpatov further showed that differences in population growth and tolerance of conditioned media could affect the relative performance of competing species. These investigations therefore offered more than a simple demonstration that one species could exclude another: they helped identify conditions under which different competitive outcomes might arise.

The relationship between experimentation and mathematical theory was correspondingly selective. Gause reported results consistent with aspects of Volterra's competition models, but his predator–prey experiments did not uniformly reproduce the fluctuations predicted by the mathematical treatment. The historical achievement lay in making theoretical relationships experimentally investigable, while also revealing the limitations of particular assumptions and experimental arrangements. Laboratory findings could support, qualify, or challenge mathematical explanations, but their generalisation to natural ecological systems required additional evidence.


## Ecosystems, Energy, and Material Processes

The emergence of ecosystem ecology provided another approach to the problem of identifying appropriate ecological units. In "The Use and Abuse of Vegetational Concepts and Terms" (1935), Arthur Tansley criticised organism-like interpretations of vegetation and advanced the ecosystem concept as a means of considering organisms together with the physical conditions and processes with which they interact. This proposal was more than an enlargement of the geographical area under investigation. It changed how the relations among biological and physical components could be organised for explanatory purposes without requiring that an ecosystem possess the individuality of an organism.

Raymond Lindeman's "The Trophic-Dynamic Aspect of Ecology" (1942) subsequently demonstrated the explanatory possibilities of investigating trophic relationships through energy transfers. His approach made it possible to connect ecological processes through quantitative accounts of energy flow, extending inquiry beyond inventories of species or descriptions of vegetation. Tansley and Lindeman thus contributed in related but distinguishable ways: the former helped reformulate the conceptual object of ecological investigation, while the latter developed a quantitative approach to particular processes occurring within ecological systems.

These contributions should not be represented as the culmination of an inevitable progression towards systems thinking. Ecosystem ecology subsequently developed through disagreements over stability, boundaries, integration, and the appropriate use of systems concepts. The histories produced by Joel Hagen and Frank Golley illuminate different aspects of this development, including the influence of research institutions, methodological commitments, and the intellectual circumstances in which ecosystem approaches gained authority.

The broader historical lesson is that conceptual reorganisation and empirical explanatory achievement must be evaluated separately. The introduction of an ecosystem concept opened possibilities for investigation, while particular studies of energy and material relationships demonstrated what could be explained through selected methods. Neither contribution established that ecosystems are organisms or that every ecological problem is best addressed at the ecosystem scale.

## Ecology Across Places, Institutions, and Knowledge Traditions

The history of ecology cannot be adequately confined to a small number of European and North American theorists. Its investigations depended upon environments, organisms, research institutions, and intellectual exchanges distributed across many regions, although the available historical scholarship remains uneven in its coverage of these developments. The examples considered here are therefore representative rather than comprehensive.

Megan Raby's *American Tropics* (2017) provides an important account of the Caribbean setting in which tropical biological research developed. Field stations, scientific travel, institutional funding, colonial relationships, and changing research priorities all influenced the questions that could be investigated and the authority accorded to particular kinds of evidence. Tropical environments were not merely places where theories developed elsewhere were applied; field investigations could generate new problems and expose limitations in existing generalisations.

The international development of ecology also depended upon botanical institutions, agricultural investigations, vegetation studies, conservation programmes, and environmental monitoring. These activities differed in their purposes and methods, and a fuller global history would require closer examination of the particular institutions and investigators involved. A selective historical account should acknowledge that limitation rather than presenting a small number of examples as a complete international synthesis.

Indigenous ecological knowledge also challenges histories centred exclusively on academic institutions. As the Australian examples discussed earlier demonstrate, ecological investigation may intersect with longstanding relationships to Country, cultural responsibilities, and different understandings of knowledge production. These relationships cannot be adequately represented solely through their measurable ecological consequences or contributions to academic research.

These examples cannot be assimilated into a single narrative of the growth of Western ecological science. They demonstrate that environmental knowledge may be organised through different intellectual and practical commitments, and that relationships between Indigenous traditions and academic research have involved collaboration as well as unequal authority and exclusion. Recognising these differences is not an obstacle to writing the history of ecology; it is necessary to understanding the limits and circumstances of the disciplinary history being told.

## What Changed in Biological Explanation?

The historical cases examined here identify several related but distinguishable changes in ecological explanation. Their significance lies not in demonstrating that each successive approach superseded its predecessors, but in showing how different investigative practices made particular questions more tractable.

Humboldt's comparative plant geography connected vegetation patterns with measured environmental gradients, allowing relationships across different localities to be represented and examined systematically. Warming and Cowles subsequently helped establish vegetation and its transformation as subjects of comparative field investigation, with Cowles's dune studies showing how ecological succession could be approached through hypotheses about changing environmental processes. These developments extended the explanatory possibilities of natural history, while retaining the problem of distinguishing observed distributions from the causes and histories responsible for them.

Mathematical population ecology made assumptions about population interactions explicit and enabled investigators to examine their consequences formally. Gause's experiments demonstrated how some such relationships could be investigated under controlled conditions, although neither formal models nor laboratory findings established universal principles without further evidence. Animal ecology and the development of niche concepts similarly expanded the range of questions concerning environmental requirements, species interactions, functional roles, and population persistence, while showing that explanatory concepts could remain productive despite differences in their interpretation.

The disagreements among Clements, Gleason, and Tansley revealed that the identification of ecological patterns and the attribution of organisational unity were separate questions. Communities and ecosystems could serve as useful subjects of investigation without thereby being shown to possess the integration characteristic of individual organisms. Lindeman's trophic-dynamic approach added another possibility by making energy relationships quantitatively investigable, demonstrating how explanatory progress could arise through new methods applied to particular processes rather than through the adoption of a comprehensive theory of ecological organisation.

Taken together, these developments made the selection of explanatory units, methods, and scales increasingly explicit. A local account of vegetation change, a model of population interaction, and an investigation of ecosystem energy flow address different questions and require different forms of evidence. Their value cannot be established merely by ranking them as more or less comprehensive descriptions of nature.

The history also reveals that ecological explanation has been shaped by the circumstances in which knowledge was produced. Research institutions, geographical settings, colonial relationships, and interactions with Indigenous knowledge traditions influenced both the questions investigated and the forms of evidence recognised as authoritative. Ecology's historical transformation therefore involved changes in scientific concepts and methods alongside changes in the social organisation of inquiry.

## Ecology and APS: A Bounded Comparison

The history of ecology is relevant to the Agency–Process–Scale framework because ecological investigations frequently concern organisms, environmental conditions, processes, and relationships operating across different spatial and temporal extents. APS may offer additional questions about viability-oriented activity, materially realised organisation, and organised persistence, particularly where the activities of living systems must be understood in relation to changing environmental conditions.

Such relevance does not establish theoretical identity or explanatory superiority. The disagreements concerning plant communities and ecosystems show why ecological association should not automatically be interpreted as organisational unity, while the success of a population model does not depend upon its incorporating every physiological process occurring within the organisms concerned. An APS interpretation would need to identify a specific explanatory problem, compare the strongest available account, and demonstrate what additional understanding its concepts provide.

Ecology's history is valuable independently of APS. Its principal contribution to the present framework is methodological: it provides historically grounded examples of why explanatory units, material processes, and scales must be justified in relation to particular questions and evidence, rather than inferred from a preferred conceptual vocabulary.

## Conclusion

Ecology transformed biological explanation by developing new ways to investigate organism–environment relationships. Its distinctive contributions arose through different combinations of comparative observation, field investigation, mathematical modelling, experimentation, and the conceptual reorganisation of ecological systems.

These developments did not constitute a single sequence of discoveries leading towards one comprehensive conception of biological organisation. Their history was marked by disagreements over what constituted an ecological unit, which processes deserved explanatory priority, and how observational, mathematical, and experimental evidence should be interpreted. Such disagreements were not incidental obstacles to progress; they helped expose assumptions that might otherwise have remained implicit and encouraged the development of alternative approaches.

A satisfactory historical account must also recognise that the academic discipline emerged within wider histories of environmental knowledge, including traditions that developed outside European scientific institutions and that cannot be evaluated solely through their contributions to those institutions. The history of ecology is therefore both a history of changing explanatory practices and a history of the circumstances under which particular forms of ecological understanding gained scientific authority.

Ecology's enduring intellectual significance lies in this expansion of explanatory possibilities. It made organism–environment relationships subjects of increasingly differentiated investigation without resolving them into one universal method, one privileged scale, or one conception of biological organisation. That history provides a valuable basis for assessing contemporary biological frameworks, whose contributions must likewise be judged by the explanations they enable and the evidence through which those explanations can be defended.

### Central Question

How did ecology transform biological explanation by making organism–environment relationships subjects of systematic observation, comparison, modelling, experimentation, and causal investigation?

### Architectural Role

This **Core article in the History of Ideas cluster** examines the development of ecological explanation through distinct, sometimes competing scientific traditions.

Its contribution is historical and methodological, not definitional. It neither establishes APS's canonical concepts nor presents ecology's history as a progression towards APS.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

The article draws upon natural history, plant geography, evolutionary biology, physiology, and experimental investigation. It also recognises Indigenous and other longstanding ecological knowledge traditions as independently developed forms of environmental understanding.

These traditions are historically related but do not constitute successive stages of a single intellectual development.

### Related or Subsequent Explanatory Developments

The article connects with population and community ecology, niche theory, ecosystem ecology, experimental and quantitative ecology, and contemporary environmental research, including collaborations involving Indigenous knowledge.

These approaches address different explanatory problems rather than forming a sequence of progressively superior theories.

### Related Explanatory Questions

- What distinguishes ecological description from causal explanation?
- When do ecological associations warrant claims of organisational unity?
- How should models, experiments, and field evidence be integrated?
- What determines the appropriate explanatory unit and spatial or temporal scale?
- How do scientific institutions and differing knowledge traditions influence ecological explanation and its authority?

### Explanatory Boundaries

Ecology did not originate environmental understanding, and its history was neither linear nor intellectually uniform.

Ecological communities and ecosystems are not automatically biological individuals or agents. Mathematical formalisation, experimental control, and systems representation do not independently establish explanatory superiority.

Indigenous ecological knowledge is not treated as a precursor to academic ecology. Historical affinity with APS establishes neither conceptual equivalence nor distinctive explanatory gain.

### Position Within APS

The article provides historical context for distinguishing **Agency, Process, and Scale**: viability-oriented organismal activity, materially realised ecological relationships, and the explanatory extents appropriate to particular questions.

These are complementary analytical perspectives, not independent causal levels. Ecological association does not itself establish biological agency, organisational unity, or individuality.

Any distinctive APS contribution must be demonstrated against established ecological explanations rather than inferred from conceptual resemblance.
