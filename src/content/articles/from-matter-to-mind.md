**Where This Article Fits**

*This is an evolving companion article to [How Did Matter Become Aware of Itself?](/articles/how-did-matter-become-aware-of-itself/), it uses two worked comparisons to examine how proposed connections in the APS account can be tested. Each comparison specifies an explanatory target, assesses what the evidence and existing accounts support, and revises the APS claim in light of the result.*

## Introduction

How did matter become aware of itself?

Read as an evolutionary question, “How did matter become aware of itself?” calls for a historical account like an explanation of how an eye evolved. Such an account would identify the capacities that changed, reconstruct their history in particular lineages, and explain how those changes occurred. This article takes up a question that such a history depends on: what capacities would we need to trace, and what evidence would justify attributing them to an organism? The worm and macaque comparisons examine two parts of that problem. They ask what it takes to use a learned outcome to guide action and what evidence would distinguish monitoring a memory from being aware of oneself as remembering.

A bacterium responding to a chemical gradient, a worm learning to approach an odour, and a person wondering whether they recognise a face bring different aspects of the problem into view. What makes a condition matter to a living system? How does experience change what an organism does? What would show that an organism can monitor an aspect of its own activity or recognise it as its own? A scientific account must specify the capacity at issue and identify the processes that support it. Claims about how that capacity develops or arose in a lineage require evidence of their own. Whether and how such capacities are accompanied by subjective experience is a further question.

APS provides a philosophical framework for this inquiry by taking *organised persistence* as its biological starting point. It begins with the activity through which living systems sustain their organisation through change and asks what further forms of organisation support cognition and self-awareness. The concepts are chosen for the distinctions they allow us to make. *Significance* concerns why a condition matters to an organism in relation to its continued functioning, while *integration* concerns how several such conditions jointly shape its activity. *Cognition* concerns how integrated significance enables possibilities beyond the immediate situation to guide activity. *Self-awareness* raises a further question about how an aspect of the organism’s own activity can become available to it as its own.

Together, these precisely defined and interrelated concepts form an *explanatory grammar*. Their definitions give each concept a distinct role, and their relationships let us formulate claims about what one capacity may depend on. Making the explanatory grammar explicit has value in its own right. It allows readers to assess the starting point, trace what follows from the definitions, and identify where a proposed biological dependency requires independent evidence. For example, the APS definition of cognition includes integrated biological significance. Whether a particular cognitive capacity depends on a specific form of integration is an empirical question about the organism. The wider explanatory architecture is developed in [How Did Matter Become Aware of Itself?](/articles/how-did-matter-become-aware-of-itself/).

This article examines two points in that architecture. A proposed test asks whether a worm can adjust its response to an odour when the food it has learned to expect changes in value. An existing experiment provides a basis for asking whether rhesus macaques use the availability of a memory to decide whether to attempt a test. The first comparison asks what flexible use of a learned outcome would tell us about cognition. The second asks how far the evidence supports memory monitoring and what further grounds would be needed to attribute self-awareness. The comparisons have yet to establish explanatory gain for APS. They show where existing accounts already support an inference and where further evidence or comparison is needed.

### How Explanations Connect Across Biology

Consider a worm that changes how it moves after encountering an odour with food. A mechanistic explanation asks how that experience changes the influence of sensory neurons on motor circuits and how those changes affect steering and turning. An evolutionary explanation asks how the capacity for such learning arose in a lineage and under what conditions selection might have favoured it. An organisational account asks how the activity contributes to the worm’s continued functioning. APS starts from this last question and asks how the significance of food, the animal’s prior experience, and the circumstances in which it encounters the odour jointly shape what it does.

Each approach gives investigators a way to decide what to look for. Circuit recordings and measures of navigation can help relate processes at different scales. Comparisons across species can help constrain an account of the capacity’s origins. APS makes its own starting point and conceptual dependencies explicit, so readers can see why it asks how food matters to the worm and what further evidence would show that the worm can act on the current value of an outcome it learned to expect. The explanations can inform one another where the evidence establishes a connection between them. See [Explanatory Grammar](/articles/explanatory-grammar/), [Scale — How Life Is Coordinated Across Space and Time](/articles/scale-how-life-is-coordinated-across-space-and-time/), and the glossary entry on [Scale](/glossary/scale/).

The scope of an explanatory grammar can differ from the scope of a particular explanation. In the worm case, neurobiology supplies the specialist concepts needed to explain how sensory neurons influence motor circuits, while behavioural studies distinguish changes in steering and turning. Connecting the two requires showing which changes in circuit activity account for which features of navigation. APS provides a shared vocabulary for asking how that connection bears on a further question: how does what matters to the worm, given its current state and past experience, shape what it does? The specialist accounts supply the detail needed to determine whether that proposed relationship holds.

Explanatory pluralism helps distinguish two ways in which accounts can relate. Studies of circuit activity, navigation, and evolutionary history address different questions about a capacity and can inform one another. Other accounts address the same question and may compete. For instance, experience might change the worm’s tendency to approach an odour, or the worm might use information about the particular food the odour predicts. In that case, the task is to identify evidence that could distinguish those accounts. ([Mitchell 2023](https://doi.org/10.1387/theoria.25273))

Specialist terms give each field the precision it needs to make such distinctions. Shared terms help researchers recognise where findings from different fields bear on a common question. *Memory*, for example, can refer to a persistent cellular change following an earlier event, the retention of a learned association, or the memory of a particular event whose availability an animal can assess when deciding whether to act. Each use directs attention to how the past affects later activity. A comparison becomes informative when it specifies what persists, how it affects subsequent activity, and, where relevant, whether the organism can use information about its availability.

A shared vocabulary helps researchers formulate these connections. Explanatory integration occurs when findings from one inquiry support, qualify, or constrain conclusions in another. A neural account may specify which components of a capacity an evolutionary account needs to explain. Evidence about how those components develop and vary among species may in turn narrow the plausible accounts of their origins. A more unified explanation would show how common principles or dependencies account for phenomena previously explained separately. Its scope would depend on which connections the evidence establishes. ([Brigandt 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsc.2013.06.002) and [Love and Lugar 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsc.2013.09.008))

### What APS Proposes to Connect

APS provides a shared vocabulary for connecting explanations across biological disciplines. That vocabulary is itself technical. Its terms are defined for the study of living organisation and are intended to connect questions that arise in different fields. To assess what they contribute, we can compare how APS and an established account formulate the same question, distinguish the claims at issue, and show what the evidence warrants. A vocabulary may help researchers communicate an explanation more precisely even when both accounts support the same conclusion. Its approach is one of *constrained explanatory pluralism*. To connect two explanations, we need to specify what each explains, identify the biological relationship between them, and show what evidence supports it. Organised persistence gives researchers a common point of reference where the continuity of living organisation matters to the question. See [What Is Biology Trying to Explain?](/articles/what-is-biology-trying-to-explain/) and [The Philosophical Foundations of APS](/articles/the-philosophical-foundations-of-aps/).

The shared vocabulary complements specialist terminology by helping researchers ask whose activity is at issue, how it is organised, what it helps sustain, and over what timescale. In the worm case, it helps distinguish three claims: that food contributes to the animal’s continued functioning, that experience changes how an odour guides navigation, and that the animal can adjust its response when the value of the expected food changes. Each claim calls for different evidence. The concepts and their relationships are set out in the [APS Glossary](/glossary/) and [The Core Structure of APS](/orientation/the-core-structure-of-aps-how-the-framework-fits-together/).

Terms with familiar mentalistic meanings can serve this purpose when their use is sufficiently precise. *Agency* connects questions about microbial regulation, animal navigation, and deliberate action by directing attention to how an organism’s activity bears on its conditions of viability. Each application requires evidence about which conditions matter and how the activity affects them. *Purpose* can likewise direct attention to the outcome towards which activity is organised. *Memory* raises a similar issue. The term can direct attention to how past events shape later activity, while retaining the effects of an event and monitoring whether a memory is available are distinct capacities that call for different evidence. Evidence about the organisation of activity towards an outcome and evidence about an organism’s intention support distinct claims.

The questions also extend to how people coordinate their activity to sustain an institution. An account of why a person acts and an account of how an institution persists have different explanatory targets. Connecting them requires evidence about how individual actions are coordinated and how that coordination contributes to institutional continuity over time. Specifying the scale makes clear whose activity is being explained, what persists, and which relationship requires evidence.

A demonstrated improvement in communication would be a worthwhile result in its own right. To test for it, readers from different specialisms could be given the same evidence in comparably detailed accounts, one using the APS vocabulary and the other using the relevant specialist concepts. Improvement would be shown if readers using APS judged more accurately what the evidence supports or reached equally accurate judgements in less time.

The pathway also distinguishes the relationships an explanation might establish. A process may enable a capacity or form part of the organisation that constitutes it. An observation may provide evidence that the capacity is present. Retaining a memory, for example, makes it possible for an animal to assess whether the memory is available, while evidence of retention provides a starting point for investigating whether the animal can make that assessment. Evidence of monitoring then raises a further question about what the animal knows or experiences about itself. The order of these questions guides the inquiry. Establishing a developmental or evolutionary sequence requires evidence about the history of the capacities. See [Architectural Dependency and Biological Explanation](/articles/architectural-dependency-and-biological-explanation/) and [How Do Scientific Explanations Go Wrong?](/articles/how-do-scientific-explanations-go-wrong/).

Food–odour learning in the worm provides a starting point for investigating the relation between biological integration and cognition. Existing studies show that experience changes navigation. The proposed test asks whether a worm would selectively alter its response to an odour after the value of the associated food had changed. A positive result would help specify what the animal can do with the learned association. To judge what that result would tell us about cognition and what APS contributes, we need to establish what an outcome-sensitive account of associative learning already explains.

In the macaque experiment, the animals decide whether to attempt a memory test before the test images appear, and their decisions track likely performance. The comparison asks whether a learning-based account can explain that pattern and what evidence would favour the interpretation that the animals monitor the availability of a memory. Evidence of monitoring would then raise a further question about what warrants attributing awareness of oneself as remembering.

These cases were chosen because they examine two different uses of past experience and illustrate two ways of investigating a proposed dependency. The worm case begins with how an animal uses what it has learned and develops a test of whether the current value of an expected outcome guides its behaviour. The macaque case turns to how an animal uses information about whether it remembers and assesses what an existing experiment warrants. Each conclusion applies to the particular claim, evidence, and comparator examined.

The two cases begin a broader assessment of the APS account of the matter-to-mind problem. Further comparisons can examine other proposed dependencies and test how far the findings extend across biological systems and scales. The broader assessment will also ask whether the architecture connects findings from different cases in ways that change what we can infer.

### How the Comparisons Will Be Judged

The two comparisons illustrate the strongest-comparator approach developed in the unpublished manuscript *Testing Claims of Explanatory Advance in Theoretical Biology: A Strongest-Comparator Approach*. They ask whether APS offers additional explanatory content when compared with the strongest established account addressing the same question.

1. **Specify the explanatory target.** State what is to be explained, the relevant contrast, and the scope of the claim.
2. **Reconstruct the strongest comparator.** Identify the best-supported established account, or set of accounts, available at the time relevant to the comparison. Establish what it already explains about the target.
3. **Translate the APS claim and check for redescription.** Restate the biological relationship that APS asserts in terms that preserve the claim and allow comparison with the established account. Ask whether an explanatory difference remains once differences in terminology have been set aside.
4. **Test the remaining difference.** If an explanatory difference survives, ask whether it changes what can be explained, inferred, ruled out, or expected relative to the comparator.
5. **State the outcome and its limits.** The accounts may address different targets, complement one another, or make incompatible claims. Where a direct comparison is possible, distinguish a finding of additional gain, a finding of no additional gain, and a comparison that remains unresolved.

The choice of comparator depends on the target. An explanation of how learning changes the worm’s navigation may draw on complementary findings about sensory processing, neural circuits, and whole-animal behaviour. A claim about the worm’s use of the current value of an expected food calls for comparison with outcome-sensitive accounts of associative learning. In the macaque case, a learning-based account must address the specific pattern of choices and test performance before we can judge what the results support about memory monitoring. See [How Should Scientific Explanations Be Compared?](/articles/how-should-scientific-explanations-be-compared/) and [How Should the Strongest Comparator Be Chosen?](/articles/how-should-the-strongest-comparator-be-chosen/).

Further work can extend the coverage of the inquiry or increase the resolution of a comparison. Coverage increases when we examine a different dependency, biological system, or kind of explanation. Resolution increases when we distinguish more precisely what an existing claim entails, what the comparator already explains, and what evidence would decide between them. A closer analysis of the worm case, for example, can distinguish a general change in approach behaviour from a selective response to a change in the value of one expected food. That distinction sharpens the test. Whether it yields explanatory gain depends on what the strongest outcome-sensitive account of associative learning already supports.

A comparison can clarify a claim by making the inference easier to follow, and it can sharpen the claim by specifying where it applies, what supports it, and what would count against it. Explanatory gain requires a further result. The comparison must identify a difference that warrants a conclusion the strongest existing account leaves open. For example, evidence from one field might rule out a possibility that remains open in another. To establish the gain, the comparison must show which possibility the evidence rules out and why. See [What Counts as Explanatory Gain?](/articles/what-counts-as-explanatory-gain/) and [How Do Biological Explanations Improve?](/articles/how-do-biological-explanations-improve/).

## Where the Inquiry Currently Stands

**12 November 2026.** Food–odour learning in *Caenorhabditis elegans* shows how experience changes navigation. A proposed revaluation test would ask whether the worm can use a particular food’s current value when responding to an odour learned earlier. An outcome-sensitive associative account already explains how such a result could arise. In rhesus macaques, decisions to decline a memory test track likely performance. How a learning-based account would explain the specific pattern of results remains to be assessed, and self-awareness is a further question. The two comparisons sharpen the proposed APS connections. Explanatory gain has yet to be established, and comparative clarity and efficiency remain untested.

## First Worked Comparison — From Biological Integration to Cognition

### What Needs Explaining?

A condition matters biologically when it makes a difference to an organism’s continued functioning. Organisms can coordinate several such conditions in a single response. APS calls this *integration*. In canonical bacterial chemotaxis, for example, recent chemical conditions affect how a bacterium responds to its current surroundings. Receptor adaptation and feedback account for how this temporal comparison biases movement. This case gives us a reference point. Evidence of integration across time leaves open whether the activity warrants a cognitive interpretation. ([Sourjik and Wingreen 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceb.2011.11.008))

In the worm, we can ask two more specific questions. Does experience change the strength or direction of a learned response to an odour? Can the worm also adjust that response when the food the odour predicts changes in value after training? The first question concerns learning. The second concerns what the animal can do with what it learned. Both bear on the proposed boundary of cognition, and each needs evidence of its own.

### What the Experiments Support

Cho and colleagues found that odour exposure shifted the sensitivity of the worm’s olfactory neurons whether the odour had been paired with food or with food deprivation. The two kinds of conditioning had opposing effects on how those neurons influenced motor circuits. This separates an effect of exposure on sensory processing from an effect of learning on behavioural preference. Chen and colleagues showed how learning alters specific components of navigation, including how worms steer and when they change direction. Together these studies explain why a familiar odour can guide movement differently after different experiences. They leave open what happens when the value of a particular food changes after the odour–food association has been learned. ([Cho et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.14000) and [Chen et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3003005))

### What Would Distinguish the Accounts?

Suppose worms learned to associate each of two odours with a different food. After training, the value of one food would be reduced while both odours were absent. A separate assay would need to establish that the worms distinguish the foods and that the change is specific to the food in question. The critical observation would be a selective change in response to the associated odour on the first test, with neither food present. Responses to the other odour and measures of ordinary odour sensitivity and movement would help assess whether a general change in state could explain the result. This is a proposed test for the worm. Work on outcome-specific revaluation provides the comparative logic. In honeybees, reducing the value of fructose after odour conditioning reduced subsequent responding to the learned odour. That gives the proposed worm test an empirical precedent in another species. ([Fanselow and Wassum 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4691796/) and [Lai et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nlm.2020.107278))

A learned tendency to approach an odour, combined with a general change in hunger, gives little reason to expect this selective result. An outcome-sensitive associative account predicts a selective change. On that account, the odour brings information about a particular food into play, and the food’s current value helps determine the response. The animal could therefore change what it does with the cue without further cue training. This would give us evidence that it can combine what it learned about **which outcome to expect** with a separately acquired change in **what that outcome is worth**. It would leave open whether the animal can consider alternative possibilities in the stronger sense proposed by Corcoran, Pezzulo, and Hohwy. ([Fanselow and Wassum 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4691796/) and [Corcoran et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10539-020-09746-2))

### How the Comparison Changes the APS Claim

The APS anchor proposes that cognition involves integrated biological significance guiding activity across possibilities beyond immediate or fixed regulation. A learned odour preference already involves history and biological significance. Selective revaluation would identify a more specific capacity, but calling that capacity cognitive still requires an argument about where to draw the boundary within adaptive regulation.

> **Revised claim for this case.** Food–odour learning shows that an organism’s past encounters can change how a cue guides present activity. Selective revaluation would support the further inference that the organism uses the current value of a specific learned outcome to adjust its response to the cue. Whether that capacity warrants cognitive attribution depends on the argument for drawing the boundary of cognition there.

The revised claim makes the empirical question more precise. The associative comparator supports the same inference. A further contribution from APS would require showing how independently measured consequences for the worm’s continued functioning constrain what we can infer about its choice beyond what the strongest associative account already provides. That is the next question about integration. A separate comparison with readers would be needed to establish whether the revised APS account helps them reach a defensible answer with less effort or fewer errors.

## Second Worked Comparison — From Memory Monitoring to Self-Awareness

### What Needs Explaining?

Remembering an image and deciding whether one remembers it are different capacities. An animal can use a memory to recognise an image. It may also use the strength or availability of that memory to decide whether to attempt a test. The second capacity is sometimes called *metamemory*. It raises a further question for the APS pathway. When does evidence that an animal adjusts its behaviour according to the state of its memory give us reason to say that it is aware of itself as remembering?

### What the Experiment Supports

Hampton gave two rhesus macaques a choice between taking a memory test for a larger but uncertain reward and declining it for a smaller guaranteed reward. They made that choice before seeing the test images. Their accuracy was generally higher when they chose to take the test than when they were required to take it, and they tended to decline when memory was likely to be weak or absent. The decision therefore carried information about how well they were likely to perform. Because the test images were still hidden, the animals could make that decision before encountering the difficulty of choosing among them. ([Hampton 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC33214/))

### What Would Distinguish the Accounts?

A choice can track the likelihood of success through several routes. An animal might learn to decline under familiar conditions associated with poor performance, such as a long delay. Hampton used probe trials to test a range of such explanations. More generally, learning-based models show how familiar reward contingencies can produce some of the behavioural patterns used to infer metacognition. A case-specific learning model would need to account for the advantage on tests the monkeys chose to take, the decisions they made before seeing the test images, and their responses on probe trials in which the sample was omitted. The comparison with such a model remains open. ([Hampton 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC33214/) and [Jozefowiez et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.3819/ccbr.2009.40003))

One candidate explanation is that the animal uses an internal signal related to the availability of a memory. That signal could guide the decision to take the test without requiring the animal to think of itself as someone who remembers. One way to test the scope of this explanation would be to ask whether an animal uses information about its memory to guide a new decision, such as whether to seek another look at the sample, under conditions where familiar cues and reward history cannot account for the choice. Successful transfer would strengthen the inference that the animal can use information about its own performance beyond the original task. It would still leave open what the animal experiences and whether it recognises the memory as its own.

### How the Comparison Changes the APS Claim

The APS pathway connects cognition, selfhood, and reflective agency. The macaque case helps specify one possible connection. Information about the availability of a memory can guide a decision about what to do next. That is evidence about how an animal regulates its own activity. A claim about self-awareness adds a further question about whether the animal is aware of itself as the one remembering. A claim about subjective experience asks what remembering feels like to the animal, if anything.

> **Revised claim for this case.** When an animal uses the availability of a memory to decide whether to act, that supports a functional claim about memory monitoring. That claim concerns performance within the task. Whether the capacity transfers to a new decision requires separate evidence. Attributing self-awareness requires a further argument about what the animal knows or experiences about itself.

This revision gives the APS pathway a more precise intermediate claim. The distinction between using a memory and using information about its availability is already explicit in the experimental literature. The comparison shows where that distinction belongs in the APS account and where the next inference requires further evidence. It leaves comparative explanatory gain open.

## What Would Count as Progress?

A clearer account should help readers identify what is being claimed, which evidence supports it, and what remains open. To test clarity and efficiency, give independent readers the same evidence and either the revised APS account or a comparably concise presentation of the strongest alternatives. Ask what the evidence establishes, which accounts remain viable, and what further observation would matter. Compare the accuracy and consistency of their answers and the time taken to reach a defensible judgement.

Explanatory gain requires a different result. Connecting the accounts would have to warrant a conclusion beyond what the strongest relevant comparator already supports. This could take the form of a specific constraint on how evidence from one domain bears on a claim in another. The comparison would need to identify the constraint, explain why it holds, and show which inference it changes.

## What the Two Comparisons Reveal Together

The worm test would vary the current value of an expected outcome. The macaque test varies conditions that affect the availability of information needed to act. The first asks whether an animal adjusts a learned response when the outcome changes in value. The second asks whether an animal’s decision to act tracks whether it has the information it needs.

The contrast gives the APS pathway more precision. It identifies two factors that could be examined together in a single task: how much an outcome matters and how reliable the information for obtaining it is. The studies concern different species and tasks, so the relationship between those capacities remains to be established.

## Where the Inquiry Goes Next

Hampton, Zivin, and Murray gave rhesus macaques the opportunity to look for a hidden food reward before choosing. The macaques looked more often when they had been prevented from seeing where the food was hidden. Tu, Pani, and Hampton subsequently examined how macaques adjust their search as information accumulates. These studies give the next comparison a starting point in existing experiments. ([Hampton et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-004-0215-1) and [Tu et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0039595))

The question is which features of the results require sensitivity to the information the animal has available. A learning-based account might explain search through familiar cues, such as whether the animal saw the food being hidden. An account based on monitoring information would expect search to vary with the reliability of what the animal remembers, even when a familiar cue stays the same. The next comparison should examine the strongest version of each account against the same task and ask what happens when information reliability and familiar cues to difficulty vary separately.

## Conclusion

The worm studies show that learning changes navigation. Selective revaluation would test a further claim about the use of a learned outcome’s current value, an inference already available from outcome-sensitive associative learning. The macaque study shows that decisions to take a test track likely memory performance. The comparison with a case-specific learning model remains open, and attributing self-awareness requires a further argument.

The two cases sharpen the APS account by specifying what the proposed connections claim and where further evidence is needed. They have yet to establish explanatory gain, and comparative clarity and efficiency remain untested. An account of how these capacities evolved would also require historical and comparative evidence about their distribution and antecedents.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

When does explaining one biological capacity help explain another, and what would show that the connection advances our understanding?

### Architectural Role

This article is an evolving worked example of how to specify an explanatory target, compare accounts, assess evidence, and revise a proposed dependency.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

[How Did Matter Become Aware of Itself?](/articles/how-did-matter-become-aware-of-itself/) sets out the proposed links among living organisation, cognition, and self-awareness examined here. [A Practical Guide to Building Better Scientific Explanations](/articles/a-practical-guide-to-building-better-scientific-explanations/) provides the method for assessing those links. Each comparison specifies what needs explaining, establishes what the evidence warrants, and asks what, if anything, the APS account adds to the strongest existing explanation of the same target.

### Related Explanatory Developments

The first comparisons examine the links from biological integration to cognition and from memory monitoring to self-awareness. The next comparison starts from existing macaque information-seeking experiments and asks how sensitivity to available information can be distinguished from reliance on familiar cues.

### Related Explanatory Questions

What distinguishes an enabling condition from evidence that a capacity is present? When does integrating existing explanations warrant a further conclusion? What evidence would show that one account is clearer or more efficient to use?

### Position Within APS

The article applies the APS approach to assessing explanatory advance to APS’s own account of cognition and mind. It records which proposed connections the comparisons clarify and which remain open.
