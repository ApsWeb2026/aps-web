**Where This Article Fits**

*This is an evolving companion article to [How Did Matter Become Aware of Itself?](/articles/how-did-matter-become-aware-of-itself/), it uses two worked comparisons to examine how proposed connections in the APS account can be tested. Each comparison specifies an explanatory target, assesses what the evidence and existing accounts support, and revises the APS claim in light of the result.*

## Introduction

How did matter become aware of itself?

Read as an evolutionary question, “How did matter become aware of itself?” calls for a historical account like an explanation of how an eye evolved. Such an account would identify the capacities that changed, reconstruct their history in particular lineages, and explain how those changes occurred. This article takes up a question that such a history depends on: what capacities would we need to trace, and what evidence would justify attributing them to an organism? The worm and macaque comparisons examine two parts of that problem. They ask what it takes to use a learned outcome to guide action and what evidence would distinguish monitoring a memory from being aware of oneself as remembering.

A bacterium responding to a chemical gradient, a worm learning to approach an odour, and a person wondering whether they recognise a face bring different aspects of the problem into view. What makes a condition matter to a living system? How does experience change what an organism does? What would show that an organism can monitor an aspect of its own activity or recognise it as its own? A scientific account must specify the capacity at issue and identify the processes that support it. Claims about how that capacity develops or arose in a lineage require evidence of their own. Whether and how such capacities are accompanied by subjective experience is a further question.

APS provides a philosophical framework for this inquiry by taking *organised persistence* as its biological starting point. It begins with the activity through which living systems sustain their organisation through change and asks what further forms of organisation support cognition and self-awareness. The concepts are chosen for the distinctions they allow us to make. *Significance* concerns why a condition matters to an organism in relation to its continued functioning, while *integration* concerns how several such conditions jointly shape its activity. *Cognition* concerns how integrated significance enables possibilities beyond the immediate situation to guide activity. *Self-awareness* raises a further question about how an aspect of the organism’s own activity can become available to it as its own.

Together, these precisely defined and interrelated concepts form an *explanatory grammar*. Their definitions give each concept a distinct role, and their relationships let us formulate claims about what one capacity may depend on. Making the explanatory grammar explicit has value in its own right. It allows readers to assess the starting point, trace what follows from the definitions, and identify where a proposed biological dependency requires independent evidence. For example, the APS definition of cognition includes integrated biological significance. Whether a particular cognitive capacity depends on a specific form of integration is an empirical question about the organism. The wider explanatory architecture is developed in [How Did Matter Become Aware of Itself?](/articles/how-did-matter-become-aware-of-itself/).

This article applies the strongest-comparator method to APS’s own account of the matter-to-mind problem. The two comparisons sharpen the account by specifying what evidence would support two proposed connections. In the worm case, the proposed test asks whether the current value of a learned outcome guides behaviour, a result already predicted by an outcome-sensitive account of associative learning. In the macaque case, existing results show that choices track likely memory performance, and whether a case-specific learning account can explain the full pattern remains open. The comparisons make the APS claims more precise. Neither has yet established additional explanatory gain.  

### How Explanations Connect Across Biology

When a worm changes how it navigates after encountering an odour with food, behavioural studies can measure changes in steering and turning, while neurobiological studies can investigate how sensory neurons influence motor circuits. Connecting the two accounts requires evidence about which changes in circuit activity help explain which features of navigation. An evolutionary account asks a further question about how the capacity for such learning arose in a lineage. A clear account of what the animal can do helps specify the capacity whose history needs explaining. See [Scale — How Life Is Coordinated Across Space and Time](/articles/scale-how-life-is-coordinated-across-space-and-time/) and the glossary entry on [Scale](/glossary/scale/).

Explanatory pluralism distinguishes accounts that answer complementary questions from accounts that compete over the same question. A circuit account and a behavioural account can jointly explain a change in navigation. At the behavioural level, two accounts may compete over whether experience simply changes the tendency to approach an odour or whether the worm uses information about the particular food the odour predicts. The latter comparison calls for evidence that could distinguish those possibilities. ([Mitchell 2023](https://doi.org/10.1387/theoria.25273))

An explanatory grammar can have a wider reach than any particular explanation that uses it. Mechanistic reasoning can guide inquiry across biology, while an explanation of how a worm steers depends on evidence about its particular sensory and motor circuits. See [Explanatory Grammar](/articles/explanatory-grammar/). Specialist terms give each field the precision it needs, and shared terms help researchers identify where their findings bear on a common question. *Memory*, for example, can direct attention to a persistent cellular change or to the retention of a learned association. In the macaque case, the question goes further and asks whether an animal can use the availability of a memory to guide a decision. Comparing these uses requires specifying what persists, how it affects later activity, and what evidence would show that the animal monitors its availability. Explanatory integration occurs when a connection is clear enough for findings in one inquiry to support, qualify, or constrain an explanation in another. ([Brigandt 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsc.2013.06.002) and [Love and Lugar 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsc.2013.09.008))

### What APS Proposes to Connect

APS provides a shared technical vocabulary for connecting explanations across biological disciplines. Its terms are defined for the study of living organisation and complement the specialist concepts needed to explain particular phenomena. This is what APS calls *constrained explanatory pluralism*. The question determines which accounts are relevant, and connecting them requires specifying what each explains, identifying the biological relationship between them, and showing what evidence supports it. Organised persistence gives researchers a common point of reference where the continuity of living organisation matters to the question. See [What Is Biology Trying to Explain?](/articles/what-is-biology-trying-to-explain/) and [The Philosophical Foundations of APS](/articles/the-philosophical-foundations-of-aps/).

In the worm case, APS asks how the food’s significance for the animal, its present condition, and its prior experience jointly shape navigation. That question brings three claims into view. Food can contribute to the animal’s continued functioning, experience can change how an odour guides navigation, and a change in the value of the expected food may alter the worm’s response to that odour. Each claim requires different evidence. Behavioural and neurobiological studies supply some of that evidence, while the proposed revaluation test asks whether the current value of the food guides the learned response. The APS concepts and their relationships are set out in the [APS Glossary](/glossary/) and [The Core Structure of APS](/orientation/the-core-structure-of-aps-how-the-framework-fits-together/).

Terms with familiar mentalistic meanings can help connect fields when their use is sufficiently precise. *Agency* connects questions about microbial regulation, animal navigation, and deliberate action by directing attention to how an organism’s activity bears on its conditions of viability. Each application requires evidence about which conditions matter and how the activity affects them. *Purpose* can direct attention to the outcome towards which activity is organised. Evidence that activity is organised towards an outcome and evidence that an organism intends that outcome support distinct claims.

A demonstrated improvement in communication would be worthwhile in its own right. What would count as such an improvement is considered below.

The pathway also distinguishes what enables a capacity, what constitutes it, and what provides evidence for it. Retaining a memory makes that memory available for use. Whether an animal can monitor its availability is a further question. Evidence that the availability of a memory guides a decision would support an attribution of monitoring. A claim that the animal recognises the remembered state as its own requires evidence for that further capacity. The order of these questions guides the inquiry. Establishing a developmental or evolutionary sequence requires evidence about how the capacities arise in individuals or change across lineages. See [Architectural Dependency and Biological Explanation](/articles/architectural-dependency-and-biological-explanation/) and [How Do Scientific Explanations Go Wrong?](/articles/how-do-scientific-explanations-go-wrong/).

The two cases were chosen because they examine different ways in which past experience can guide present activity. Food–odour learning in the worm provides a starting point for asking whether an animal can adjust a learned response when the expected outcome changes in value. The macaque experiment asks whether an animal’s decision to attempt a test is guided by the availability of a memory. The first case develops a proposed test of a capacity. The second evaluates what an existing experiment warrants. Their order follows the questions, from using what was learned to monitoring whether it is available for use.

These cases begin a broader assessment of the APS account of the matter-to-mind problem. Further comparisons can examine other proposed dependencies and test how far the findings extend across biological systems and scales. They can also ask whether connecting findings across cases changes what we can infer.

### How the Comparisons Will Be Judged

Both cases apply the approach developed in the unpublished manuscript *Testing Claims of Explanatory Advance in Theoretical Biology: A Strongest-Comparator Approach*. The comparison asks whether APS changes what we can explain or infer about the same biological question.

1. **Specify the explanatory target.** State what needs explaining, which possibilities are being compared, and where the claim applies.
2. **Reconstruct the strongest comparator.** Identify the best-supported established account, or combination of accounts, available at the time relevant to the comparison. State what it already explains about the target.
3. **Translate the APS claim and check for redescription.** Restate the biological relationship APS proposes in terms that preserve the claim and allow comparison with the established account. Ask whether a substantive difference remains once differences in terminology have been set aside.
4. **Test the remaining difference.** Ask whether it changes what can be explained, inferred, ruled out, or expected. Specify what evidence would establish that consequence and whether the comparator already supports it.
5. **State the outcome and its limits.** The accounts may complement one another, address different questions, or make incompatible claims. Where the evidence permits a verdict on explanatory gain, state what the comparison establishes. Where the comparison remains unresolved, identify what evidence or analysis is needed.

The comparator depends on the question. Explaining how learning changes navigation can draw on behavioural and neurobiological findings together. Explaining whether the worm uses the current value of an expected food calls for an outcome-sensitive account of associative learning. See [How Should Scientific Explanations Be Compared?](/articles/how-should-scientific-explanations-be-compared/) and [How Should the Strongest Comparator Be Chosen?](/articles/how-should-the-strongest-comparator-be-chosen/).

A comparison can improve an account by clarifying its claim, specifying where it applies, or identifying evidence that would test it. Additional explanatory gain requires a substantive difference that warrants an inference the strongest existing account leaves open. See [What Counts as Explanatory Gain?](/articles/what-counts-as-explanatory-gain/) and [How Do Biological Explanations Improve?](/articles/how-do-biological-explanations-improve/).

## First Worked Comparison — From Biological Integration to Cognition

### What APS Proposes

APS proposes that biological agency becomes cognitive when integrated biological significance guides activity across viability-relevant possibilities beyond immediate or fixed regulation. The question for this comparison is whether the way an animal uses a learned association helps us draw that distinction. Bacterial chemotaxis gives us a reference point. Recent chemical conditions affect how a bacterium responds to its present surroundings, and receptor adaptation and feedback explain how this temporal comparison biases movement. The case shows why sensitivity to past conditions alone leaves the question of cognition open. ([Sourjik and Wingreen 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceb.2011.11.008))

Food–odour learning shows that experience can change how an odour guides navigation. The proposed test asks whether a worm can selectively adjust its response to an odour after the food it predicts changes in value. Such an adjustment would support an attribution of flexible, outcome-sensitive regulation. Whether that flexibility involves guidance across alternatives in the APS sense would require a further argument and evidence.

### What the Strongest Comparator Already Explains

The existing studies establish how experience can change odour-guided navigation. Cho and colleagues found that odour exposure altered the sensitivity of the worm’s olfactory neurons whether the odour had been paired with food or with food deprivation. The two kinds of conditioning had opposing effects on how those neurons influenced motor circuits. Chen and colleagues showed how learning changes specific components of navigation, including how worms steer and when they change direction. Together, these studies explain how a familiar odour can guide movement differently after different experiences. They leave open whether the worm can adjust its response when the value of the expected food changes after training. ([Cho et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.14000) and [Chen et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3003005))

For that further question, the relevant comparator is an outcome-sensitive account of associative learning. On this account, an odour can cue an expectation of a particular food, and the food’s current value can influence the response. The account therefore predicts that reducing the value of one food after training could selectively weaken the response to the odour associated with it, even without further exposure to that odour during revaluation. This prediction is already available from associative learning theory. Whether it holds in the worm is the question for the proposed test. ([Fanselow and Wassum 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4691796/))

### What Evidence Would Distinguish the Accounts?

Suppose worms learned to associate each of two odours with a different food. After training, the value of one food would be reduced while both odours were absent. A separate assessment would need to establish that the worms distinguish the two foods and that the reduction in value is specific to one of them. The critical result would be a selective change in response to the associated odour on the first test, with neither food present. The response to the other odour, together with measures of odour sensitivity and movement, would help assess whether a general change in the worms’ state could explain the result. Work on honeybees provides a precedent. Reducing the value of fructose after odour conditioning reduced subsequent responding to the learned odour. ([Lai et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nlm.2020.107278))

This test would help distinguish a general change in approach behaviour from a response sensitive to the current value of a specific learned outcome. The outcome-sensitive associative account already predicts the selective response. To test for an additional APS contribution, we would need to specify what independent evidence about the food’s contribution to the worm’s continued functioning would let us infer about its behaviour beyond what the associative account already supports.

### What the Evidence Warrants

The existing worm studies show that experience changes how an odour guides navigation. If the proposed test produced a selective response, it would support a further inference. The worm can use what it learned about which food to expect and adjust its response in light of that food’s current value. Whether this capacity warrants calling the behaviour cognitive depends on how the cognitive boundary is drawn. The result would also leave open whether the worm can consider alternatives in the stronger sense discussed in work on counterfactual cognition. ([Corcoran et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10539-020-09746-2))

### What the Comparison Changes in APS

The comparison sharpens the APS question about the boundary between integrated regulation and cognition. Food–odour learning shows that prior experience changes how a cue guides navigation. Selective revaluation would help establish whether the worm can use the current value of a particular learned outcome. Whether that capacity meets the APS criterion for cognition depends on which viability-relevant possibilities guide the worm’s activity and what evidence would establish their role.

**Comparative verdict.** A positive result would support outcome-sensitive behaviour in the worm and fulfil a prediction already made by the strongest associative account. That result alone would leave additional explanatory gain for APS unestablished.

## Second Worked Comparison — From Memory Monitoring to Self-Awareness

### What APS Proposes

In the APS account, self-awareness involves a capacity to recognise some aspect of one’s own activity as one’s own. The macaque case lets us ask how far evidence about memory bears on that claim. An animal can retain and use a memory to recognise an image. It may also use the availability of that memory to decide whether to attempt a test. This second capacity is a form of memory monitoring, sometimes called *metamemory*. Recognising the remembered state as one’s own is a further capacity.

The comparison therefore asks two questions. What would show that the macaques’ decisions are guided by the availability of a memory? If the evidence supports memory monitoring, what further evidence would warrant saying that the animals are aware of themselves as remembering?

### What the Strongest Comparator Already Explains

Hampton gave two rhesus macaques a choice between attempting a memory test for a larger but uncertain reward and declining it for a smaller guaranteed reward. They decided before seeing the test images. They generally performed better on tests they chose to take than on tests they were required to take, and they tended to decline when memory was likely to be weak or absent. Their choices therefore tracked likely performance. ([Hampton 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC33214/))

The relevant comparator is a learning-based account of those choices. An animal could learn to decline under conditions associated with poor performance, such as a long delay. Learning-based models show how reward contingencies can produce some patterns of performance associated with metacognition. To assess such an account here, we would need to show whether it can explain the advantage on tests the macaques chose to take, how they made that choice before seeing the test images, and how they responded on probe trials in which the sample was omitted. Hampton’s probes constrain the learning-based explanation, and the adequacy of a case-specific account remains unresolved. ([Hampton 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC33214/) and [Jozefowiez et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.3819/ccbr.2009.40003))

### What Evidence Would Distinguish the Accounts?

One possibility is that the macaques use a signal related to memory availability when deciding whether to attempt the test. A follow-up could ask whether they use that signal to guide a different decision, such as whether to seek another look at the sample. The test would be most informative if memory availability varied independently of familiar cues to difficulty and the competing accounts made different predictions. If information-seeking tracked memory availability under those conditions, the result would strengthen the case for monitoring beyond the original task.

### What the Evidence Warrants

Hampton’s results show that the macaques’ choices tracked likely memory performance. Their decisions before seeing the test images and their responses on probe trials constrain how a learning-based account can explain that pattern. Whether a case-specific learning account can explain the full set of results remains open. Evidence of transfer would strengthen the inference to flexible memory monitoring. Attributing self-awareness would require further evidence that the animal recognises the remembered state as its own.

### What the Comparison Changes in APS

A retained memory may be available for use. Evidence that its availability guides a decision would support an attribution of memory monitoring. Recognising the remembered state as a state of oneself is a further capacity that requires evidence of its own.

**Comparative verdict.** The results constrain learning-based accounts of the macaques’ choices. Whether a case-specific learning account can explain the full pattern remains open, so the comparison is unresolved. The next step is to identify evidence that would distinguish such an account from an account based on memory monitoring. Attributing self-awareness would require separate evidence of self-attribution.

## What Would Count as Progress?

A clearer account should help readers identify what is being claimed, which evidence supports it, and what remains open. To test clarity and efficiency, give independent readers the same evidence and either the revised APS account or a comparably concise presentation of the strongest alternatives. Ask what the evidence establishes, which accounts remain viable, and what further observation would matter. Compare the accuracy and consistency of their answers and the time taken to reach a defensible judgement.

Explanatory gain requires a different result. Connecting the accounts would have to warrant a conclusion beyond what the strongest relevant comparator already supports. This could take the form of a specific constraint on how evidence from one domain bears on a claim in another. The comparison would need to identify the constraint, explain why it holds, and show which inference it changes.

When a comparison finds no additional explanatory gain, one response is to revise the claim to reflect what the evidence and the established account already support. The revised account may clarify a distinction, specify where a claim applies, or identify a question that can now be tested. In the worm case, distinguishing a general change in approach behaviour from a selective response to the changed value of an expected food makes the claim more precise and gives us a test. An outcome-sensitive associative account already predicts that selective response, so a positive result would tell us something about the worm without establishing additional explanatory gain for APS. Further work can increase resolution by examining a consequential difference more closely, or broaden coverage by examining a different dependency, system, or scale. Where the comparison remains unresolved, the priority is to establish what the strongest comparator can explain and what evidence would decide between the accounts.

The same standard applies to explanations within a subdiscipline and to frameworks that connect disciplines. A specialist inquiry might identify a mechanism or establish the conditions under which it operates. A cross-disciplinary inquiry might show that evidence at one scale constrains an explanation at another. In either case, explanatory gain requires identifying which inference changes and showing why the strongest existing account does not already warrant it. A framework can also improve the precision with which researchers formulate shared questions or communicate their answers. Those improvements are worth assessing in their own right.

## What the Two Comparisons Reveal Together

Taken together, the two comparisons suggest a question about how animals regulate action. How do the current value of an expected outcome and the reliability of the information needed to obtain it jointly affect whether an animal acts or seeks more information? The worm comparison isolates the question about outcome value, while the macaque comparison examines whether memory availability guides a decision.

The two cases concern different species and tasks. To investigate the relationship directly, researchers could vary outcome value and information reliability independently within a single task. They could then ask whether, for example, the value of a reward changes how much uncertainty an animal will tolerate before it seeks more information.

Bringing the cases together helps formulate a testable question. An explanatory gain for APS would require showing that relating outcome value to information reliability changes what we can explain, infer, or predict beyond what the strongest existing account of the same behaviour already supports.

## Where the Inquiry Goes Next

Hampton, Zivin, and Murray gave rhesus macaques the opportunity to look for a hidden food reward before choosing. The macaques looked more often when they had been prevented from seeing where the food was hidden. Tu, Pani, and Hampton subsequently examined how macaques adjust their search as information accumulates. These studies give the next comparison a starting point in existing experiments. ([Hampton et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-004-0215-1) and [Tu et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0039595))

The immediate follow-up asks whether macaques seek information because what they remember is unreliable or because a familiar cue, such as having missed the hiding event, has been associated with poor performance. A comparison would vary memory reliability independently of such cues and ask which account better explains when the animals choose to look. This would increase the resolution of the macaque comparison by sharpening the distinction between the two accounts. It would also test the reach of the monitoring interpretation by asking whether it applies to actively seeking information as well as to declining a memory test.

A longer-term comparison would vary outcome value and information reliability within the same task. This would broaden the inquiry by asking how the two factors jointly influence the decision to act or seek more information. To assess what APS contributes, the comparison would identify an inference about their joint influence and ask whether the strongest existing account already supports it.

## Conclusion

The worm studies show that learning changes navigation. Selective revaluation would test a further claim about the use of a learned outcome’s current value, an inference already available from outcome-sensitive associative learning. The macaque study shows that decisions to take a test track likely memory performance. The comparison with a case-specific learning model remains open, and attributing self-awareness requires a further argument.

The two cases sharpen the APS account by specifying what the proposed connections claim and where further evidence is needed. They have yet to establish explanatory gain, and comparative clarity and efficiency remain untested. An account of how these capacities evolved would also require historical and comparative evidence about their distribution and antecedents.
