## Understanding Biological Explanation Across Frameworks

Contemporary biology possesses extraordinary explanatory power. Across genetics, evolutionary theory, ecology, systems biology, cognitive science, autonomy theory, organisational biology, enactivism, information theory, and process philosophy, researchers have developed increasingly sophisticated accounts of how living systems function, develop, regulate themselves, adapt to changing conditions, and evolve through time. Yet despite this remarkable success, biology remains theoretically fragmented. Different traditions often explain different aspects of living systems while relying upon distinct assumptions concerning what life fundamentally is and what biological explanation is ultimately trying to explain.

At first glance these disagreements may appear to concern theories alone. In practice, however, they often reflect deeper disagreements concerning biological concepts themselves. Agency, function, information, cognition, organisation, normativity, adaptation, and explanation are frequently defined in different ways by different theoretical traditions. As a result, frameworks that appear to disagree about biological reality are often organising biological concepts according to different explanatory priorities. What counts as fundamental within one framework may appear derivative within another.

Some approaches begin from information processing, others from evolutionary history, organisational closure, predictive regulation, self-production, adaptive behaviour, distributed systems dynamics, cognition, or processual continuity. Each captures something important about living systems. Each illuminates a genuine dimension of biological organisation. Yet no single perspective fully explains why these phenomena belong together within a unified account of life itself.

[[box:how-aps-relates-to-other-theoretical-frameworks]]

APS approaches this problem from a different starting point. Rather than beginning with information, cognition, adaptation, regulation, development, or evolution, it begins with viability-oriented organised persistence sustained across time. The central question therefore becomes not simply how particular biological phenomena operate, but what kind of organisation must exist for any biological phenomenon to count as biological at all.

This shift in emphasis has important consequences. Information, regulation, adaptation, cognition, and evolution are not rejected. On the contrary, APS treats them as indispensable dimensions of biological reality. However, they are interpreted as organisational achievements rather than explanatory foundations. Their significance derives from the fact that they contribute, directly or indirectly, to the maintenance of organised persistence. Biological explanation therefore begins not with the consequences of living organisation but with the conditions that make those consequences possible.

From this perspective, contemporary theories are best understood as partial explanatory orientations toward a common underlying reality. Evolutionary theory illuminates the historical transformation of organised persistence. Systems approaches illuminate patterns of interaction and coordination. Organisational and autonomy theories clarify the self-maintaining character of living systems. Cognitive approaches reveal increasingly sophisticated forms of evaluation and environmental engagement. Information-theoretic approaches illuminate the role of difference-sensitive regulation. Each contributes genuine explanatory insight while often elevating one organisational dimension into a complete account of life itself.

APS instead attempts to situate these perspectives within a broader explanatory grammar grounded in viability, continuity, persistence, agency, perturbation-sensitive regulation, constraint closure, and scale-integrated transformation. The aim is not to replace existing biological theories but to clarify the organisational conditions that make their explanatory successes possible and their limitations intelligible.

Recent developments within APS have further strengthened this orientation by identifying agency as the defining activity through which viability-oriented organised persistence is maintained. Agency is therefore not treated as one biological phenomenon among many. It becomes the activity through which living systems continually regulate conditions, respond to perturbations, preserve continuity, and sustain the organisational structures required for continued existence. Process and scale remain indispensable explanatory dimensions, but agency increasingly occupies a central position because it identifies what living systems actively do in order to remain living systems.

> **Where this article fits:** This article situates APS comparatively in relation to contemporary frameworks in biology, cognition, systems theory, organisational biology, process philosophy, and philosophy of biology more generally. Its purpose is not to provide a complete presentation of APS, nor to defend organisational realism or reconstruct biological intelligibility in its entirety. Rather, it examines how APS integrates, extends, constrains, and reorganises insights drawn from existing theoretical traditions within a continuity-oriented explanatory architecture.

For the broader philosophical reconstruction of APS, see *APS as Philosophy — A Viability-Oriented Account of Biological Reality*. For the formal structure of the framework, see *The Explanatory Geometry of Biology — How APS Organises Biological Explanation*.

## The Problem of Partial Explanation

One of the recurring features of contemporary biology is the tendency for successful explanatory approaches to become identified with biology itself. A framework that successfully explains one important dimension of living organisation is often extended until it is treated as a complete account of life. The history of biological theory is filled with such expansions. Genes become the key to understanding life. Information becomes the fundamental biological currency. Control, prediction, cognition, autonomy, adaptation, or process become candidates for explanatory primacy.

The problem is not that these concepts are unimportant. Quite the opposite. Their explanatory success is precisely what makes such expansions attractive. Information genuinely matters to living systems. Regulation genuinely contributes to continuity. Prediction can contribute to adaptive success. Cognition increasingly shapes organism-environment relations. Organisational closure captures important features of biological autonomy. Process philosophy correctly emphasises the temporally extended character of living systems.

The difficulty arises when one explanatory achievement is mistaken for the explanatory foundation of biology as a whole. A framework may successfully explain how information functions within living systems without explaining why information matters biologically in the first place. A theory may explain adaptive behaviour without fully explaining the organisational conditions that make adaptation necessary. A model of cognition may illuminate evaluation and decision-making while leaving unexplained the viability-oriented organisation that makes such activities meaningful.

APS argues that many contemporary theories remain explanatorily incomplete for precisely this reason. They begin from organisational consequences rather than from the conditions that make those consequences possible. Information matters because differences can affect viability. Regulation matters because living systems must preserve continuity. Prediction matters because future-oriented activity can contribute to persistence. Cognition matters because evaluation increasingly shapes adaptive continuity. Autonomy matters because living systems regenerate the constraints that support their own existence. Process matters because persistence requires ongoing transformation rather than static permanence.

In every case, the explanatory phenomenon already presupposes organised persistence. The phenomenon may be real, important, and worthy of investigation, yet it is not necessarily explanatorily foundational. APS therefore shifts attention toward the organisational conditions that underlie these diverse biological realities. Rather than asking which explanatory perspective should dominate biology, the framework asks what organisational conditions must already exist for information, regulation, adaptation, prediction, cognition, autonomy, development, or evolution to occur within a living system at all.

## The APS Perspective

APS approaches biological explanation through the integrated relations of agency, process, scale, viability, continuity, perturbation, and organised persistence. Living systems are understood as viability-oriented, constraint-closed, perturbation-sensitive continuities that continually regenerate and modulate the conditions required for their own persistence. Life is therefore not interpreted as a static state, a collection of mechanisms, or a particular informational architecture. It is understood as an ongoing organisational achievement maintained through continuous activity across multiple interacting scales.

This perspective alters how explanatory priority is understood. No single mechanism, level of analysis, or theoretical vocabulary becomes universally primary. Depending upon the question being investigated, explanatory attention may focus on genes, developmental systems, organisms, ecological relations, regulatory dynamics, cognitive processes, or evolutionary histories. APS therefore rejects the assumption that explanatory importance implies ontological priority. A phenomenon may be central to a particular explanation without constituting the foundational basis of biological organisation itself.

[[box:scale-is-not-hierarchy]]

This commitment distinguishes APS from approaches that attempt to reduce life to any single organisational dimension. The framework does not identify life with information processing, computation, control, prediction, adaptation, cognition, autonomy, or process. Each captures a genuine aspect of living organisation, but none fully explains why living systems persist as organised entities through time. APS instead treats these phenomena as interconnected dimensions of a broader continuity-producing organisation grounded in viability-oriented persistence.

Perturbation occupies a particularly important place within this explanatory framework because living organisation often becomes most visible when it is challenged. Stable functioning can conceal organisational dependencies that only become apparent when continuity is threatened. Repair, compensation, resilience, adaptation, recovery, and failure reveal the organisational structures that sustain persistence. For this reason, APS frequently treats perturbation as an especially powerful window into biological explanation.

[[box:perturbation-reveals-organisation]]

APS consequently functions not as a specialised competitor to existing biological theories but as a continuity-oriented explanatory grammar capable of organising their relations. Its aim is to clarify why particular theoretical approaches succeed, what dimensions of biological organisation they illuminate, where their explanatory limits arise, and how their insights may be integrated within a broader account of viability-oriented organised persistence. In this respect APS is less concerned with replacing contemporary theories than with understanding how their explanatory achievements relate to one another within a more comprehensive account of life.

## APS and Competing Biological Definitions

Contemporary theoretical biology is often presented as a landscape of competing theories. In many respects, however, it is more accurately understood as a landscape of competing definitions. Disagreements concerning agency, function, normativity, organisation, information, cognition, and explanation frequently arise because different frameworks organise these concepts in fundamentally different ways. What appears to be a dispute about biological reality is often a dispute about which concepts should be regarded as explanatory foundations and which should be treated as consequences of deeper organisational processes.

APS approaches this problem by shifting attention from the concepts themselves to the organisational conditions that make them possible. Rather than asking how agency should be defined, what function really is, or where normativity originates, the framework first asks what kind of organisation must exist for any of these phenomena to become biologically meaningful. This question leads APS toward viability-oriented organised persistence as the common organisational reality underlying many apparently separate biological concepts.

The result is not merely a new theory of life but a reorganisation of several central biological definitions. Concepts that are often treated as explanatory starting points become intelligible as interconnected dimensions of a broader continuity-producing organisation. The significance of this shift becomes particularly clear when examining some of the most influential concepts in contemporary biology.

### Biological Agency

Few concepts reveal the differences between contemporary biological theories more clearly than agency. Over the past several decades, agency has become increasingly important across fields as diverse as theoretical biology, evolutionary theory, systems cognition, autonomy theory, niche construction, and philosophy of biology. Yet despite this growing interest, there remains little agreement concerning what agency actually is.

Some accounts identify agency with goal-directed behaviour. Others emphasise adaptive regulation, environmental modification, self-determination, autonomy, or organism-environment interaction. Each of these perspectives captures something important about living systems. Organisms do regulate themselves, modify their environments, pursue goals, and adapt to changing circumstances. The difficulty is that such descriptions often identify manifestations of agency without fully explaining why these activities matter biologically.

APS approaches the issue from a different direction. Rather than treating agency as a specialised capacity or an emergent property of sufficiently complex organisms, it treats agency as the defining activity through which living systems maintain viability-oriented organised persistence. Agency refers to the ongoing activity by which living systems preserve the organisational conditions required for continued existence. Regulation, adaptation, goal-directedness, environmental modification, and behavioural flexibility are therefore not separate foundations of agency but different expressions of a more fundamental continuity-producing activity.

This perspective has important implications. Agency is no longer restricted to cognition, intelligence, or behavioural sophistication. Nor is it reduced to a particular mechanism of control. Instead, it becomes a general biological principle describing the active maintenance of organised persistence across scales and contexts. The significance of agency derives not from what organisms happen to do, but from the role those activities play in sustaining viability through time.

### Biological Function

The concept of function occupies a similarly contested position within contemporary biology. For much of the twentieth century, debates concerning function revolved around two dominant traditions. Selected-effect theories explained functions through evolutionary history, arguing that traits possess functions because particular effects contributed to reproductive success in the past. Causal-role theories explained functions through present contribution, identifying functions with the causal roles components play within larger systems.

More recently, organisational approaches have attempted to ground function in contributions to self-maintaining biological organisation. These developments have generated important insights, yet no consensus has emerged. Each account successfully explains some aspects of biological practice while encountering difficulties elsewhere.

APS approaches function by asking why biological systems distinguish functioning from malfunctioning in the first place. The answer lies not primarily in evolutionary history, causal contribution, or organisational participation considered independently, but in viability-oriented organised persistence. Functions matter because they contribute to the maintenance, regulation, and continuation of living organisation. Evolutionary history may explain why a function emerged. Mechanistic analysis may explain how it operates. Organisational analysis may explain its systemic relations. Yet viability explains why the function is biologically significant.

This perspective allows a range of apparently separate concepts to be understood within a common framework. Function, malfunction, repair, resilience, adaptation, and regulation all concern the maintenance of organisational continuity under changing conditions. The distinction between proper functioning and dysfunction is therefore not imposed externally upon biological systems. It emerges from the organisational realities of living persistence itself.

### Biological Explanation

Disagreements concerning biological explanation often mirror the broader fragmentation of contemporary biology. Different traditions prioritise different explanatory forms. Mechanistic approaches explain how organised processes occur through interactions among components. Evolutionary explanations account for historical transformation. Developmental explanations investigate the emergence of organised structures. Ecological explanations focus on organism-environment relations, while informational approaches emphasise communication, signalling, and regulation.

Each of these explanatory forms captures genuine dimensions of biological reality. The challenge arises when one explanatory strategy is treated as universally primary. Mechanisms explain operation, but not always significance. Evolution explains historical emergence, but not necessarily present organisation. Development explains formation, but not always ongoing regulation. Ecology explains environmental relations, but not necessarily the internal conditions that make those relations biologically meaningful.

APS therefore rejects the search for a single privileged explanatory vocabulary. Biological explanation is understood as the analysis of how organised persistence is generated, maintained, transformed, and regulated across interacting processes and scales. Mechanistic, developmental, ecological, cognitive, organisational, and evolutionary explanations become complementary perspectives on the same continuity-producing organisation.

The significance of this move should not be underestimated. APS does not simply combine existing explanatory approaches into a larger framework. It attempts to explain why those approaches work in the first place. Their explanatory power derives from the fact that each illuminates particular dimensions of viability-oriented organised persistence. Explanation therefore becomes integrative without collapsing into theoretical pluralism, because the diverse explanatory forms remain anchored within a common organisational reality.

### Biological Normativity

Normativity remains one of the most distinctive and controversial features of biological explanation. Biological systems appear capable of succeeding and failing, functioning properly or improperly, maintaining viability or moving toward breakdown. These normative distinctions are embedded throughout biological practice, yet their theoretical foundations remain deeply contested.

Many contemporary approaches ground normativity in evolutionary history, adaptive success, selected effects, or organisational closure. APS approaches the issue more directly. Normativity emerges from the conditions required for viability-oriented organised persistence. A biological process becomes normatively significant because it affects the capacity of a living system to maintain itself through time.

This view allows biological evaluation to be understood as an intrinsic feature of living organisation rather than an external interpretive framework. A process is beneficial because it contributes to viability. A malfunction is significant because it compromises viability. Repair matters because it restores conditions necessary for continued persistence. Success and failure therefore emerge naturally from the organisational realities of life itself.

Normativity, on this account, is neither metaphorical nor mysterious. It is a consequence of the fact that living systems must continually preserve the conditions required for their own continuation. Wherever viability can be maintained, enhanced, compromised, or lost, normative distinctions necessarily arise. APS therefore treats normativity not as a specialised philosophical problem but as a direct expression of the organisational logic of living systems.

### Biological Organisation

Perhaps no concept has become more important within contemporary theoretical biology than organisation. Across organisational biology, autonomy theory, systems theory, and process philosophy, there is growing recognition that living systems cannot be adequately understood as collections of independently functioning parts. Biological explanation increasingly requires attention to patterns of organisation, relations among processes, and the coordinated activities that sustain living systems through time.

APS strongly converges with this movement. Living systems are understood as organised continuities rather than static assemblies of components. Organisation is not an accidental arrangement of parts but a dynamically maintained pattern of relations through which viability is sustained. Biological identity therefore depends less upon material continuity than upon the continued preservation of organisational continuity across ongoing transformation.

Yet APS also introduces a further question that many organisational approaches leave partially unresolved. To say that living systems are organised does not by itself explain how that organisation persists. Organisation is not a final explanatory destination. It is itself something that must be maintained, regulated, repaired, and reproduced under changing conditions.

This shift directs attention toward agency, continuity regulation, adaptive response, and viability-oriented activity. Organisation becomes intelligible as an ongoing achievement rather than a static condition. APS therefore seeks not merely to explain why living systems are organised, but how organised persistence is continually sustained despite constant material turnover, developmental transformation, ecological interaction, and evolutionary change.

## APS and Process Philosophy

Among contemporary developments in philosophy of biology, process philosophy represents one of the closest intellectual neighbours of APS. Process-oriented approaches challenge the traditional view that organisms are fundamentally stable things possessing fixed properties. Instead, they argue that living systems are better understood as ongoing processes sustained through continual material and organisational transformation.

This perspective has gained increasing influence through the work of thinkers such as Dupré and Nicholson, who emphasise that biological identity is inherently temporal. Organisms persist not by remaining unchanged but by continuously reorganising themselves through time. Metabolism, development, ecological interaction, and evolution are therefore not secondary features of life. They are central to what living systems are.

APS strongly converges with this insight. The framework likewise rejects substance-based conceptions of life and treats continuity through transformation as a defining characteristic of biological organisation. Living systems do not persist despite change. They persist through change. The continual replacement of components, the reorganisation of structures, and the adaptive modification of activity are not threats to biological identity but expressions of it.

At the same time, APS extends process-oriented thinking by asking a question that process philosophy does not always foreground: why do some processes persist while others disappear? Process alone does not explain biological normativity, function, diagnosis, or adaptive regulation. Nor does it fully explain why particular forms of continuity matter biologically while others do not.

APS therefore argues that biological processes become intelligible through their relation to viability-oriented organised persistence. The central explanatory question is not merely what processes constitute living systems, but how those processes contribute to the continued viability of the organisations they sustain. Agency becomes particularly important in this context because living systems do not simply undergo processes. They actively regulate, modify, repair, and reorganise themselves in response to changing conditions.

In this respect APS converges strongly with process philosophy while extending it toward a more explicitly biological account of persistence, regulation, function, normativity, and explanation. Process remains indispensable, but it becomes intelligible within a broader framework centred on the active maintenance of viability-oriented organised persistence.

## APS and Organisational Biology

Among contemporary approaches to theoretical biology, organisational and autonomy-based frameworks stand closest to APS. Over recent decades, organisational biology has transformed discussions of life by emphasising that organisms cannot be adequately understood as collections of independently functioning parts. Living systems are increasingly recognised as dynamically organised networks of mutually dependent processes and constraints whose continued activity contributes to the maintenance of the organisation itself.

APS strongly converges with this development. Like organisational biology, it treats living systems as organisationally constituted, dynamically maintained, perturbation-sensitive, and intrinsically normative. Biological functions are understood not as isolated mechanical effects but as contributions to the persistence of organised systems. Failure, malfunction, repair, adaptation, resilience, and recovery become intelligible only relative to the continued viability of the organisation within which they occur. The significance of any biological process therefore depends upon its contribution to the maintenance of organised continuity through time.

At the same time, APS extends organisational approaches in several important respects. Organisational biology has often focused on self-maintaining structures, constraint closure, and the mutual dependence of biological processes. APS incorporates these insights while placing greater emphasis on temporally extended continuity. Living systems are not merely organised states or self-maintaining structures. They are organised continuities whose persistence depends upon ongoing transformation across time. Development, adaptation, ecological interaction, cognition, and evolution are therefore not peripheral additions to biological organisation but essential dimensions of the processes through which continuity is maintained.

[[box:organisational-biology-and-persistence]]

This broader temporal perspective allows APS to extend organisational analysis beyond the boundaries traditionally assigned to it. Organisation is not treated as one topic among many within theoretical biology. Rather, organisational continuity becomes a central feature of biological intelligibility itself. Questions concerning agency, diagnosis, resilience, semiosis, cognition, development, and explanation all become questions concerning how living systems sustain organised persistence under changing conditions. Organisation therefore functions not as a specialised explanatory domain but as a unifying principle linking otherwise fragmented areas of biological inquiry.

APS consequently converges strongly with organisational biology while seeking to provide a broader explanatory grammar capable of integrating organisational insights with the wider landscape of contemporary biological theory.

## Integration Rather Than Rejection

A recurring misunderstanding of integrative frameworks is that they seek to replace existing theories with a single overarching perspective. APS does not pursue such a goal. The framework neither rejects nor supersedes the major traditions of contemporary biology. On the contrary, it draws extensively upon insights developed within systems theory, autonomy theory, process philosophy, enactivism, evolutionary biology, ecology, biosemiotics, organisational biology, and mechanistic explanation. These traditions have generated powerful explanatory tools and continue to illuminate genuine dimensions of living organisation.

What distinguishes APS is not opposition to these approaches but a different understanding of their relationship. Rather than treating them as competing candidates for explanatory primacy, APS interprets them as partial perspectives on a common organisational reality. Their differences are often less important than the fact that they each illuminate particular aspects of the processes through which living systems maintain themselves through time.

This shift in perspective changes how familiar biological concepts are interpreted. Genes matter because organised systems persist and inheritance contributes to that persistence. Information matters because differences can affect viability. Regulation matters because continuity requires active maintenance. Prediction matters because future-oriented activity may contribute to persistence. Cognition matters because evaluation increasingly shapes adaptive continuity. Organisation matters because persistence depends upon coordinated relations among processes. Agency matters because organised persistence must continually be maintained through activity.

Viewed in this way, the explanatory significance of these concepts derives not from their isolated importance but from their contribution to a larger continuity-producing organisation. APS therefore seeks not merely explanatory synthesis but explanatory reorganisation. The framework attempts to identify the organisational conditions that underlie diverse explanatory successes across contemporary biology and thereby clarify how apparently distinct theoretical traditions relate to one another.

The central question becomes:

> What must be true of a system for any biological phenomenon to matter biologically at all?

APS argues that viability-oriented organised persistence provides the most general answer currently available to that question.

## Why APS Reorganises Contemporary Biology

The claim that APS reorganises biological explanation rather than merely adding another theoretical perspective is central to understanding the framework. Contemporary biology already possesses successful explanations of adaptation, cognition, development, regulation, information processing, ecological interaction, and evolutionary transformation. APS does not challenge the legitimacy of these explanations. Instead, it asks what they presuppose.

A theory may successfully explain information without explaining why information matters biologically. A theory may successfully explain cognition without explaining why evaluative capacities contribute to the persistence of living systems. A theory may account for adaptive regulation without explaining why regulation is necessary in the first place. In each case, explanatory success occurs within a broader organisational context that is often taken for granted.

APS directs attention toward that context. It argues that many concepts commonly treated as explanatory foundations become more intelligible when viewed as dimensions of viability-oriented organised persistence. Information becomes viability-relevant difference. Function becomes contribution to organised persistence. Normativity becomes viability-relative evaluation. Agency becomes the activity through which persistence is maintained. Explanation becomes the analysis of organised continuity across process and scale. Adaptation becomes the modification of organisation in ways that contribute to continued viability under changing conditions.

This does not diminish the importance of these concepts. On the contrary, it helps explain why they matter. Their significance derives from their participation in the broader organisational realities that make living systems possible. APS therefore seeks to clarify not only what contemporary theories explain but why those explanations are biologically meaningful.

The framework consequently occupies a distinctive position within theoretical biology. It is neither a replacement for existing theories nor a simple synthesis of them. It is an attempt to identify the organisational conditions that underlie their explanatory successes and to provide a common framework within which their relationships become intelligible.

## Contemporary Convergences

One of the most striking features of recent theoretical biology is the extent to which independently developed research programmes are beginning to converge upon themes long emphasised within APS. Work in systems cognition, multiscale agency, organisational intelligence, autonomy theory, biosemiotics, ecological regulation, and life-inspired machine intelligence increasingly highlights continuity, persistence, distributed regulation, adaptive organisation, and multilevel coordination as fundamental characteristics of living systems.

Research by Pezzulo and Levin provides a particularly illuminating example. Although developed within a different conceptual framework, their work on cognitive light cones draws attention to temporally extended agency, distributed regulation, persistence-sensitive organisation, and multiscale coordination. Similar themes appear within organisational biology, process philosophy, autonomy theory, and systems approaches that increasingly recognise that biological organisation cannot be adequately explained through isolated mechanisms or local optimisation alone.

These convergences are philosophically significant because they suggest that contemporary biology may be progressively rediscovering organisational principles that transcend the boundaries of individual theoretical traditions. Continuity, persistence, perturbation-sensitive regulation, scale integration, distributed agency, and adaptive organisation appear repeatedly across fields that were once largely disconnected. The recurrence of these themes suggests that they may reflect deep organisational realities rather than merely disciplinary preferences.

APS attempts to provide an explanatory architecture capable of situating these developments within a unified account of viability-oriented organised persistence. In this sense, the framework functions not merely as a participant in contemporary theoretical discussions but as an effort to clarify the broader organisational patterns toward which many of those discussions appear to be converging.

## APS, Holism, and Organicism

The emphasis APS places upon organisation, continuity, and integration can sometimes create the impression that the framework is a form of holism or a revival of classical organicism. Such interpretations misunderstand both the ambitions and the constraints of the framework.

APS rejects reductionist approaches that attempt to explain biological organisation entirely through the behaviour of isolated components. Yet it does not conclude that explanation can simply be transferred to "the whole." Organised systems are not treated as mysterious entities whose properties somehow transcend analysis. Nor does APS return to older organismic metaphysics in which organisms are regarded as possessing special ontological status independent of their processes and relations.

Instead, APS remains firmly committed to biological explanation. Living systems must be understood as integrated, self-maintaining, temporally extended, continuity-producing organisations, but those organisations remain fully open to mechanistic, developmental, ecological, cognitive, and evolutionary investigation. The framework therefore rejects both reductionism and undisciplined holism, seeking instead an account of biological intelligibility grounded in the dynamics of organised persistence itself.

Readers interested in these distinctions should consult *Why APS Is Not Holism* and *Why APS Is Not Organicism*.

## Related Clarification Articles

The comparative analysis developed throughout this article is extended through a series of clarification articles examining influential contemporary frameworks individually. These essays explore both the explanatory strengths of particular traditions and the organisational limitations that prevent them from functioning as complete accounts of life.

Topics include information processing, control theory, active inference, enactivism, autopoiesis, computation, intelligence, gene-centred explanations, and broader attempts to reframe biological explanation. Each article follows the same general strategy employed throughout APS: identifying what a framework explains well, clarifying where its explanatory boundaries arise, and situating its insights within a broader account of viability-oriented organised persistence.

These analyses should not be interpreted as dismissals. APS does not claim that contemporary theories are mistaken. Rather, it argues that no single framework, by itself, fully explains the organisational conditions that distinguish living systems as living systems.

Readers may enter the comparative literature of APS from many different directions. Some may begin with questions concerning agency, function, normativity, or biological explanation. Others may approach the framework through debates surrounding information, cognition, evolution, systems theory, or autonomy. Regardless of the point of entry, the purpose of the comparative series remains the same: to clarify how diverse theoretical traditions relate to one another when viewed through the lens of organised persistence.

For readers seeking a more structured pathway, the recommended sequence begins with the core APS account of life, proceeds through the philosophical reconstruction developed in *APS as Philosophy*, and then moves outward into the comparative and clarification literature. Taken together, these articles demonstrate how APS attempts to transform a fragmented theoretical landscape into a more integrated account of biological organisation.

The goal is neither theoretical unification through reduction nor unrestricted pluralism without explanatory constraint. It is explanatory integration through viability-oriented organised persistence sustained across time.

# Conclusion

Contemporary biology contains a remarkable diversity of explanatory traditions. Mechanistic, evolutionary, ecological, organisational, cognitive, informational, systems-oriented, and process-based approaches have each revealed important dimensions of living systems. Their collective success demonstrates the richness and complexity of biological reality. Yet it has also contributed to a theoretical landscape in which different concepts are frequently treated as explanatory foundations within different domains.

APS approaches this situation from a different direction. Rather than beginning with a particular explanatory vocabulary, it begins with the organisational conditions that make biological explanation possible. The framework argues that living systems become intelligible through the viability-oriented organised persistence they continuously maintain across perturbation, adaptation, development, cognition, ecology, and evolution. Concepts such as agency, function, normativity, information, organisation, and explanation are therefore not isolated foundations but interconnected dimensions of a broader continuity-producing reality.

In this respect, APS does not simply propose another biological theory. It proposes a different way of organising biological explanation itself. Its central claim is that many of biology's most important concepts become more intelligible when understood through the common framework of viability-oriented organised persistence. The framework therefore functions not merely as a specialised theory of life but as a comparative explanatory grammar capable of clarifying the relationships among contemporary biological theories and the organisational realities they seek to explain.
