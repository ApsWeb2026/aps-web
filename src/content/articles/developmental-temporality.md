Living systems are fundamentally temporal.

Development does not occur within organisms that remain otherwise static or complete. Organisms persist only because viability-oriented developmental organisation continuously reorganises itself across time while preserving sufficient continuity to maintain biological individuality.

APS therefore treats temporality not as an external background against which development unfolds, but as an intrinsic feature of living organisation itself.

Living systems exist through temporally organised continuity.

## Development Is Temporally Constituted

Traditional biological explanation often treats time as secondary.

Development may be described as unfolding through stages, sequences, or programs, while the organism itself is implicitly treated as an enduring structural object moving through externally measurable temporal intervals.

APS rejects this interpretation.

Organisms do not first exist and then change through time. Rather, biological organisation is itself temporally constituted through ongoing developmental transformation.

Developmental continuity therefore depends upon:

- continual physiological turnover,
- ecological interaction,
- repair processes,
- behavioural adaptation,
- regulatory coordination,
- and continuity-preserving reorganisation.

Living systems persist because developmental organisation remains dynamically coordinated across time despite continual material and organisational change.

## Continuity Through Transformation

Developmental temporality is not the persistence of static identity.

Organisms remain viable precisely through continual transformation.

Cells divide, tissues reorganise, behaviours change, ecological relations shift, and physiological organisation continuously adapts to changing conditions. Yet viable continuity is ordinarily preserved across these transformations.

APS therefore interprets development as continuity-through-change rather than persistence-through-stasis.

This perspective dissolves the apparent contradiction between:

- developmental transformation,
and:
- biological persistence.

Organisms remain themselves not because they avoid change, but because continuity-preserving developmental organisation remains dynamically coordinated across time.

## Temporal Organisation and Viability

Viability depends upon temporal coordination.

Developmental organisation must preserve relations across multiple temporal scales simultaneously, including:

- metabolic rhythms,
- repair cycles,
- developmental transitions,
- behavioural timing,
- reproductive organisation,
- ecological interaction,
- and life-history coordination.

Disruption of temporal coordination may destabilise developmental continuity even when structural components remain materially present.

APS therefore treats temporal organisation as biologically constitutive rather than merely descriptive.

Living systems remain viable because developmental organisation preserves coordinated continuity across temporally distributed processes.

## Developmental Timing and Organisational Coherence

Development depends upon organised timing relations.

Processes occurring too early, too late, too rapidly, or too slowly may destabilise viability-oriented organisation.

Developmental coherence therefore requires continual temporal coordination across interacting physiological, behavioural, ecological, and regulatory systems.

This applies across all scales of development, including:

- embryogenesis,
- growth,
- maturation,
- reproduction,
- repair,
- ageing,
- and degeneration.

Temporal disorganisation may produce developmental instability, malfunction, or continuity breakdown.

APS consequently interprets developmental timing as an organisational feature of viable persistence rather than as a merely external chronological measure.

## Temporality and Ecological Interaction

Developmental temporality is ecologically distributed.

Organisms develop within temporally structured ecological systems involving:

- seasonal cycles,
- resource availability,
- parental care,
- social interaction,
- symbiotic relations,
- environmental rhythms,
- and ecological succession.

Developmental continuity therefore depends partly upon alignment between organismal and ecological temporal organisation.

Disruption of ecological timing relations may destabilise development even when internal physiological systems remain partially intact.

APS consequently treats developmental temporality as relationally organised across organism–environment systems rather than confined entirely within isolated organisms.

## Ageing and Developmental Temporality

Ageing reveals the temporality of living organisation especially clearly.

Living systems do not merely accumulate chronological duration. Rather, ageing reflects gradual transformations in continuity-maintaining developmental organisation itself.

Repair capacities may weaken, resilience may decline, regulatory coordination may destabilise, and ecological dependencies may become increasingly fragile across time.

APS therefore interprets ageing not as external wear imposed upon static organisms, but as ongoing transformation within temporally organised developmental continuity.

Ageing consequently remains inseparable from development itself.

## Life Cycles and Temporal Continuity

Developmental temporality extends beyond individual developmental moments.

Life cycles organise recurring developmental continuities across broader temporal structures involving:

- maturation,
- reproduction,
- inheritance,
- ecological transition,
- and lineage persistence.

Living systems therefore preserve continuity simultaneously across:

- moment-to-moment regulation,
- organismal development,
- life-history organisation,
- and intergenerational persistence.

APS consequently treats biological temporality as multiscalar and organisationally distributed rather than linear or uniform.

## Developmental Temporality and Organised Persistence

APS interprets developmental temporality as the organised continuity of viability-oriented developmental transformation across time.

Living systems persist not because they resist change, but because developmental organisation continually reorganises continuity across changing material, ecological, and organisational conditions.

Development is therefore intrinsically temporal.

Living organisation does not merely unfold in time. Rather, temporally organised continuity constitutes the very possibility of biological persistence itself.
