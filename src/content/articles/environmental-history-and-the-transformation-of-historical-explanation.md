## Introduction — Nature Enters Historical Explanation

Human history has commonly been narrated through the activities of people: the formation and dissolution of states, the development of institutions, the movement of populations, the invention of technologies, and the changing organisation of economic and cultural life. The natural world appears throughout these accounts, but often as the setting within which historical events unfold. Rivers provide routes of communication, soils support agriculture, forests supply timber, and climates influence the possibilities of settlement. Nature supplies conditions and resources, while human intentions, institutions, and actions occupy the centre of historical explanation.

Environmental history challenges the adequacy of this division by examining the changing relationships through which human societies and their environments have influenced one another. Agriculture transforms soils and vegetation; domestication alters organisms and their distributions; settlements modify water systems; commerce redistributes plants, animals, and pathogens; and the extraction of energy and materials reshapes landscapes. These transformations may subsequently influence the societies that produced them, affecting their economic possibilities, institutions, patterns of settlement, and relationships with other populations. Human and environmental histories are consequently connected through processes whose effects may extend well beyond the intentions or immediate circumstances of those involved.

The recognition that nature influences human affairs is, however, much older than environmental history as a recognised academic discipline. Classical authors discussed relationships between climate, geography, and human life, while naturalists and geographers examined cultivation, deforestation, and environmental change long before the emergence of modern environmental historiography. Historical geographers, agricultural historians, and economic historians likewise investigated material relationships that environmental historians would subsequently regard as central to their enquiries. Environmental history therefore cannot be credited with discovering environmental causation or inventing a new general logic of historical reasoning. Its distinctive achievement was instead to consolidate and extend forms of enquiry that made changing relationships between human societies and environmental processes integral to the investigation and explanation of historical developments.

This achievement involved two connected but distinguishable changes. The first concerned the scope of historical evidence and causal investigation. Biological, ecological, climatic, and material processes became increasingly important in explaining historical outcomes, while evidence from the natural sciences could be considered alongside documentary, archaeological, and other historical records. The second involved a critical reassessment of the categories through which environments were described, the authority of inherited environmental interpretations, and the political relationships that shaped both environmental change and knowledge of it. Environmental history thus developed not only by enlarging the range of processes considered historically relevant, but also by questioning how environmental knowledge was produced and how particular interpretations acquired authority.

Neither development occurred uniformly or within a single intellectual tradition. Environmental history emerged through different scholarly institutions, historical circumstances, and geographical settings, and its formation cannot adequately be reconstructed as a straightforward Anglo-American intellectual progression. Its explanatory significance is most convincingly demonstrated through particular investigations in which environmental research changed an established historical interpretation. Studies of biological exchange, colonial landscapes, and African forest histories provide different examples of such change, revealing how environmental evidence may alter causal reconstruction, challenge inherited assumptions, or transform the interpretation of familiar historical developments.

The present article examines environmental history as a development in the history of ideas, tracing its intellectual antecedents, disciplinary formation, explanatory contributions, international controversies, and relationship to contemporary questions concerning planetary change. Particular attention is given to plants, botanical institutions, and the global redistribution of cultivated organisms, whose histories connect biological processes with scientific knowledge, political power, and economic activity. The central argument is deliberately bounded: environmental history significantly reoriented particular historical explanations and enlarged the scope of historical enquiry, but it did not establish a universal replacement for existing historical methods.

## Before Environmental History: Nature, Geography, and Human Affairs

The intellectual antecedents of environmental history extend far beyond the modern environmental movement and the institutions through which environmental history became established as an academic specialism. Relationships between environmental conditions and human life had already been considered within classical geographical, medical, and natural-historical traditions. The Hippocratic text *Airs, Waters, Places* associated differences in health and ways of life with climatic and geographical circumstances, while Aristotle and Theophrastus investigated living organisms in relation to their conditions of existence. Geographical writers also described the characteristics of regions and populations, contributing to a longstanding intellectual concern with the ways in which environmental circumstances might influence human and biological life.

These traditions demonstrate that environmental relationships were already subjects of systematic reflection, but they also reveal an enduring explanatory danger. Recognising that climate or geography affects human possibilities does not establish that environmental conditions determine political institutions, cultural characteristics, or historical outcomes. Such outcomes may depend upon interactions among environmental circumstances, technologies, institutions, inherited practices, and human decisions. Explanations that overlook these relationships risk transforming legitimate observations concerning environmental influence into unsupported generalisations or forms of environmental determinism.

Early modern natural history, geography, agriculture, and the expanding European study of the natural world introduced additional dimensions to these enquiries. Exploration, commerce, and colonial expansion generated observations concerning unfamiliar organisms, environments, agricultural practices, and landscapes, while the increasing movement of plants and animals between continents made environmental transformation especially conspicuous. Cultivation altered landscapes, organisms were introduced into new regions, and the economic value of particular species encouraged the establishment of institutions devoted to their collection, classification, and management.

These developments were not simply the products of European observation or scientific initiative. European natural history was also shaped by encounters with established non-European systems of knowledge, frequently under conditions of considerable political inequality. Local expertise could be appropriated, translated into European classifications, or omitted from accounts that subsequently attributed discoveries to colonial institutions. The expansion of environmental knowledge therefore needs to be understood in relation to the political and institutional circumstances through which observations were collected, interpreted, circulated, and credited.

During the nineteenth century, concern about the consequences of human environmental modification became increasingly explicit. George Perkins Marsh's *Man and Nature* (1864) examined the effects of human activity on vegetation, soils, water, and landscapes, challenging assumptions that nature constituted an inexhaustible background to human development. Marsh emphasised the capacity of human societies to produce extensive environmental change, thereby contributing to an intellectual setting in which geography, natural history, conservation, and political concerns became increasingly connected (Marsh, 1864). His work is an important antecedent of environmental history, although it would be misleading to identify him simply as the founder of the modern discipline.

The relationship between such antecedents and the subsequent emergence of environmental history requires careful historical interpretation. An intellectual antecedent is an earlier work addressing a related problem, whereas demonstrable influence requires evidence that later scholars actually drew upon that work. Retrospective conceptual affinity is different again: it identifies similarities recognised through subsequent interpretation without necessarily establishing a historical connection. These relationships cannot be treated as interchangeable, and the presence of environmental ideas in classical or nineteenth-century scholarship does not establish an uninterrupted developmental sequence leading to modern environmental history.

Nor should the institutional emergence of a modern academic discipline be confused with the geographical origins of environmental knowledge. Environmental thought, landscape management, and knowledge of ecological relationships developed within many societies, often through practices and intellectual traditions that cannot be adequately represented within a history organised exclusively around European and North American scholarship. This distinction is particularly important when examining Indigenous and non-European traditions, whose historical significance should not be measured solely by whether they anticipated concepts subsequently developed within Western academic institutions.

Environmental history consequently emerged from an already extensive, diverse, and unevenly documented history of human environmental understanding. Its development as a recognised field of scholarship must be distinguished from the much longer history of the practices, observations, and interpretations through which societies investigated and managed their relationships with the natural world.


## Historical Geography, Ecology, and the Longue Durée

Environmental history developed through relationships with several established fields of enquiry, among which historical geography, ecological science, and approaches to long-term historical change were especially important. Each contributed concepts, evidence, and methods for investigating relationships between human activities and environmental conditions, although none can be regarded simply as an incomplete version of the discipline that subsequently emerged.

Historical geography had long investigated the transformation of landscapes through settlement, cultivation, technology, economic activity, and cultural practice. Carl Sauer's influential account of the cultural landscape treated landscapes as historical products of relationships between human activities and physical environments rather than as immutable natural settings (Sauer, 1925; Williams, 1994). Fields, irrigation systems, roads, settlements, forests, and cultivated vegetation could consequently be interpreted as material records of historical transformation. Such an approach challenged any simple division between human history and its geographical setting by recognising that landscapes themselves bear evidence of successive forms of occupation, management, and environmental change.

The intellectual territory shared by historical geography and environmental history is therefore substantial. As Michael Williams (1994) emphasised in examining their relationship, historical geographers had already developed methods for investigating landscape change and the interactions between environmental conditions and human activities. Environmental history did not introduce nature into a previously exclusively human historical enquiry. Rather, it became recognisable through the consolidation of particular research questions, scholarly institutions, methods, and controversies, many of which overlapped with existing geographical scholarship. The relationship between the fields is better understood in terms of their differing emphases and historical development than as a simple succession in which one displaced or completed the other.

Ecological science supplied a different but complementary set of intellectual resources. By investigating relationships among organisms and their environments, ecology developed concepts concerning population change, succession, competition, dispersal, disturbance, and ecological communities that could illuminate historical transformations. Forests, for example, could be investigated not merely as sources of timber or objects of ownership, but as changing ecological systems affected by extraction, fire, cultivation, regeneration, and species introductions. Agricultural landscapes could likewise be examined as biological and material systems whose productivity depended on relationships among plants, soils, water, other organisms, and human management. These perspectives enlarged the range of processes potentially relevant to the historical interpretation of landscapes and human activities.

The incorporation of ecological concepts into historical scholarship nevertheless requires more than the identification of plausible environmental mechanisms. A mechanism must be shown to have operated under the particular circumstances being investigated, and its historical significance may depend upon institutions, technologies, labour, property relationships, and other conditions that ecological description alone does not establish. The integration of ecology and history consequently raises questions of evidential relevance and causal interpretation: environmental processes must be reconstructed with sufficient precision to explain particular historical outcomes rather than merely invoked as general influences.

A further contribution came from historical approaches concerned with long-term structures and processes. The French Annales tradition challenged histories concentrated primarily on political events and prominent individuals, encouraging investigation of geographical, economic, and social relationships extending across different durations. Fernand Braudel's *The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II* placed political events within a broader account of such relationships, while his approach to the *longue durée* drew attention to relatively slow processes that shaped the circumstances within which more rapid events occurred (Braudel, 1949). This emphasis provided an important intellectual resource for historical investigations in which environmental conditions and changes operate over timescales different from those of particular political or institutional events.

Braudel's significance for environmental historiography lies especially in the recognition that historical explanation may require attention to processes operating at different temporal scales. His scholarship should not, however, be assimilated retrospectively to modern environmental history. Its purposes were broader, and any claim that particular environmental historians were directly influenced by his work requires historical evidence. The distinction between a useful conceptual antecedent and demonstrated intellectual influence remains important here, as it does elsewhere in reconstructing the field's development.

These scholarly traditions also need to be situated within a wider history of environmental understanding extending beyond Europe and North America. Knowledge of soils, vegetation, water, seasonal change, and other environmental relationships was embedded in agricultural, pastoral, forestry, and land-management practices in many societies. Colonial encounters brought different systems of environmental understanding into contact, frequently under unequal political conditions that shaped which forms of knowledge entered scientific and administrative records and whose authority was recognised. The development of modern environmental history cannot therefore be adequately reconstructed solely through a sequence of intellectual exchanges among European and American scholars.

Environmental history assembled and extended resources from these diverse traditions while increasingly questioning the adequacy of disciplinary divisions that separated environmental processes from human historical activity. Its emergence was historically contingent, shaped by particular institutions, research problems, and political circumstances, rather than the inevitable culmination of earlier environmental thought.

## The Emergence of Environmental History

Environmental history became recognisable as a distinct academic field during the later twentieth century, particularly from the 1960s and 1970s onwards (Mcneill 2003). Its institutional development coincided with growing public concern about pollution, resource depletion, species loss, environmental degradation, and the consequences of industrial development. Environmental movements encouraged historians to reconsider subjects that had often remained peripheral to established political and institutional narratives, but the resulting scholarship cannot be understood simply as environmental advocacy conducted through historical writing. The formation of the field involved the development of research questions, methods, scholarly institutions, and interpretative debates whose claims could be examined according to the standards of historical enquiry.

In the United States, Roderick Nash helped establish environmental subjects within historical scholarship, contributing to an intellectual setting in which relationships between American history, environmental ideas, and changing attitudes towards nature became increasingly prominent (Nash, 1967; McNeill, 2010). The subsequent development of environmental history was marked by studies that approached these relationships from different directions, investigating biological transformations, ecological ideas, scientific knowledge, cultural interpretation, and the material consequences of human activity.

Alfred W. Crosby's *The Columbian Exchange* (1972) provided an especially influential demonstration of the historical significance of biological transfers between the Americas and the rest of the world following transatlantic contact. The redistribution of plants, animals, and pathogens affected agricultural possibilities, disease environments, settlement patterns, demographic change, and economic relationships, making biological processes central to the interpretation of developments commonly investigated through political and economic history (Crosby, 1972). Crosby's achievement was not to discover that organisms had moved between continents, nor to invent every mechanism involved in their movement. It was to organise biological exchange systematically as an explanatory dimension of a major historical transformation, demonstrating how established accounts could be enlarged and reconsidered through attention to processes that crossed conventional disciplinary boundaries.

Other influential studies extended environmental history in different intellectual directions. Donald Worster's *Nature's Economy* (1977) examined the history of ecological ideas, showing how scientific understandings of nature possessed their own intellectual and cultural histories (Worster, 1977). Carolyn Merchant's *The Death of Nature* (1980) explored relationships between scientific thought, changing conceptions of nature, and broader social and cultural transformations (Merchant, 1980). William Cronon's *Changes in the Land* (1983), by contrast, investigated ecological transformation in colonial New England through relationships among Indigenous and colonial land use, economic practices, property arrangements, and environmental change (Cronon, 1983). These works did not establish a single method or unified interpretation. Together, they illustrated the diversity of questions that environmental history could bring into scholarly investigation, from the material transformation of landscapes to the historical formation of ideas through which nature was understood.

The institutional development of environmental history in North America must nevertheless be distinguished from the wider history of environmental thought and the emergence of environmental historiography in other regions. In South Asia, historical investigations of colonial forestry, peasant resistance, resource management, and environmental conflict developed around questions shaped by particular political and ecological circumstances. Ramachandra Guha's *The Unquiet Woods* (1989) examined ecological change and resistance in the Himalayan region, while Guha and Madhav Gadgil's *This Fissured Land* (1992) connected ecological history with social conflict and patterns of resource use (Guha, 1989; Gadgil & Guha, 1992). Such scholarship demonstrated that environmental change could not be adequately explained without examining the political organisation of access to land and resources, including the institutional arrangements through which particular forms of environmental management were imposed or contested.

African environmental historiography developed related but distinct concerns, particularly where colonial environmental administration and local land management had generated influential narratives of degradation. Research questioning these narratives showed that environmental history could involve criticism of the categories and assumptions through which landscapes had been described, rather than merely the reconstruction of material environmental change. In Latin America, historians investigated relationships among colonialism, capitalism, conservation, resource extraction, agriculture, and the historical construction of landscapes. These enquiries reflected the different environmental, political, and economic histories of the regions concerned and resisted reduction to a single explanatory programme.

Australasian environmental history likewise developed through relationships among agricultural science, settler colonialism, ecological change, and Indigenous histories. Robin and Griffiths (2004) emphasised the importance of science, particularly agricultural science, in shaping environmental policy and historiography in Australia and New Zealand. Their analysis also demonstrated why differences in environmental conditions and Indigenous histories make the two traditions less uniform than their shared experience of settler colonialism might suggest. The development of environmental history in Australasia therefore involved questions arising from its particular scientific institutions, landscapes, and historical relationships, rather than the straightforward adoption of an intellectual programme formed elsewhere.

Taken together, these developments challenge accounts in which environmental history originated in a single intellectual centre and subsequently spread unchanged across the world. The institutional formation of particular academic communities is one historical process; the development of environmental knowledge, practices, and interpretations within different societies is another. Environmental history emerged as a plural scholarly enterprise whose common concern was the historical significance of changing human–environment relationships, but whose explanatory priorities varied according to the problems, evidence, and political histories under investigation. Its intellectual coherence lay less in adherence to a uniform theory than in the recognition that environmental processes, their historical consequences, and the knowledge through which they were interpreted could become integral subjects of historical enquiry.


## The Transformation of Historical Explanation

The most important intellectual contribution of environmental history concerns the relationships considered relevant to explaining historical change. Historians had long employed causal reconstruction, interpretation, comparison, and evidential criticism, and the emergence of environmental history did not replace these established practices. What changed was the range of processes brought into historical investigation, the forms of evidence considered relevant, and, in some cases, the adequacy of inherited explanations. Environmental history encouraged historians to examine biological, ecological, climatic, and material processes alongside human intentions, institutions, and social relationships, while also questioning the assumptions through which environmental change had previously been understood.

These developments were not all equivalent in their explanatory significance. The identification of a neglected historical subject, the incorporation of additional evidence, and the revision of an established causal interpretation represent distinguishable achievements. Their importance must therefore be assessed in relation to the historical questions being investigated and the explanations already available. The strongest demonstrations of environmental history's contribution are found not in general declarations about the importance of nature, but in particular investigations where environmental evidence changed what historians could reasonably conclude.

### Expansion of historical subjects

At its most immediate level, environmental history enlarged the subjects considered worthy of sustained historical enquiry. Forests, soils, rivers, species distributions, disease environments, and ecological transformations acquired greater prominence within historical scholarship. Processes that had sometimes appeared principally as background conditions became objects of historical investigation in their own right, with their own trajectories, evidence, and relationships to human activities.

This expansion was significant because it challenged assumptions about the boundaries of historical enquiry. Nevertheless, the investigation of a previously neglected subject does not necessarily transform the explanation of an established historical problem. A history of forest management, for example, may contribute valuable knowledge without demonstrating that an existing interpretation of a political or economic development was mistaken. The enlargement of historical subjects is therefore an intellectual achievement that must be distinguished from explanatory advance in the more demanding sense of revising or improving an account of a particular historical outcome.

### Expansion of evidence

The enlargement of historical enquiry was accompanied by a corresponding expansion in the evidence available for reconstructing the past. Environmental historians increasingly drew upon ecology, climatology, geology, archaeology, and other scientific fields, incorporating records that could reveal environmental conditions and changes not adequately documented in conventional historical sources. Tree rings, pollen deposits, sediments, historical vegetation records, and biological remains could contribute to the reconstruction of past environments, sometimes extending investigation beyond the periods or circumstances represented in written records.

Such evidence can alter the possibilities of historical interpretation, but its significance depends upon the reasoning through which it is connected to a particular question. A pollen sequence, for example, may indicate changes in vegetation without establishing whether those changes resulted from climate, cultivation, fire, grazing, settlement, or some combination of these processes. Different forms of evidence also possess different temporal resolutions, uncertainties, and geographical coverage, making their integration an interpretative task rather than a straightforward accumulation of scientific facts.

The contribution of interdisciplinary evidence consequently lies not simply in increasing the quantity or variety of historical information, but in making particular reconstructions and explanations more adequately supported. Scientific records can constrain interpretations, reveal previously unrecognised changes, and help discriminate among competing accounts. They do not, however, eliminate the need for historical judgement concerning causal relevance, contextual relationships, and the adequacy of the resulting explanation.

### Causal reorientation: biological exchange

Alfred W. Crosby's account of the Columbian Exchange provides an influential example of environmental history's capacity to reorient causal explanation. Political and economic histories of European expansion necessarily investigate conquest, imperial institutions, commerce, technology, and the exercise of power. Crosby demonstrated that the consequences of transatlantic contact also required systematic attention to the movement of organisms between the Americas and the rest of the world (Crosby, 1972).

The redistribution of plants, animals, and pathogens affected agricultural possibilities, disease environments, settlement patterns, demographic change, and economic relationships. These biological processes were not merely incidental consequences of political and commercial activity; they could contribute substantially to the historical outcomes being explained. By making biological exchange central to the interpretation of transatlantic expansion, Crosby showed how an established historical subject could be reconsidered through relationships that crossed conventional divisions between human history and natural history.

This achievement did not require the replacement of political or economic explanation. The movement of organisms occurred through human activities and institutions, while its consequences depended upon the biological characteristics of the organisms involved and the circumstances into which they were introduced. Crosby's contribution was to demonstrate that explanations confined too narrowly to political intentions or commercial institutions could leave important causal relationships insufficiently examined.

The explanatory significance of this work should not be exaggerated by suggesting that earlier historians had entirely ignored disease, agriculture, or the movement of organisms. Its importance lies instead in the systematic organisation of biological exchange as a major dimension of historical interpretation. The case illustrates causal reorientation: environmental processes became necessary to an adequate explanation of particular historical developments without rendering other forms of historical enquiry obsolete.

### Interpretative revision: colonial New England

William Cronon's *Changes in the Land* (1983) provides a related but distinct example of explanatory development. His investigation of colonial New England connected ecological transformation with Indigenous and colonial land use, economic practices, property arrangements, and changing relationships between human societies and their environments. Rather than treating the landscape encountered by European settlers as an untouched natural setting, Cronon examined its existing environmental history and the different practices through which Indigenous peoples and colonists used and transformed it (Cronon, 1983).

This approach challenged interpretations in which significant environmental change began principally with European settlement. The historical question was no longer confined to how colonists altered an existing natural landscape, but extended to the environmental transformations preceding colonisation and the contrasting relationships between land use, economic organisation, and ecological change. Indigenous practices became relevant to reconstructing the landscape's history, while colonial property systems and economic activities acquired significance partly through their environmental consequences.

Cronon's interpretation required the integration of ecological evidence with social and economic history. Environmental change was not explained independently of human institutions, nor were institutions treated as sufficient explanations without attention to the material processes through which their consequences developed. The resulting account demonstrated how ecological and historical relationships could alter the interpretation of a familiar period of colonial history.

The precise degree of explanatory advance over earlier scholarship remains a comparative historical question. Cronon's contribution should not be assessed against an artificially narrow representation of previous historical research, particularly where earlier scholars had already recognised Indigenous land use or colonial environmental change. Its significance lies in the particular synthesis and interpretation achieved, and in the historical questions that this synthesis made possible.

### Interpretative reversal: the forest islands of Guinea

A particularly instructive example of interpretative reversal is provided by James Fairhead and Melissa Leach's research on the forest–savanna landscapes of Guinea. Colonial administrators and subsequent environmental interpretations had treated forest islands surrounded by savanna as remnants of formerly extensive forest cover. Within this account, local land use was understood primarily as a cause of deforestation and environmental degradation, and the apparent contraction of forest provided a rationale for administrative concern and intervention.

Fairhead and Leach investigated these assumptions through historical records, oral histories, local environmental knowledge, ecological evidence, and other forms of landscape reconstruction (Fairhead & Leach, 1995, 1996). Their published study also acknowledged research collaboration with Dominique Millimouno and Marie Kamano, an attribution that matters because the investigation drew upon situated environmental knowledge and collaborative enquiry rather than solely upon externally authored historical analysis (Fairhead & Leach, 1996).

The resulting interpretation challenged the established narrative. In the landscapes investigated, local land-management practices had contributed to the creation and maintenance of forest islands. Features previously interpreted as surviving remnants of extensive forest destruction could instead include evidence of forest establishment and enrichment through human activity. The historical significance of local practices was therefore substantially different from that assumed in the degradation narrative.

This was more than the introduction of another environmental factor into an existing explanation. Evidence concerning the landscape's history altered the direction of causal interpretation: activities previously represented primarily as destructive could also be understood as contributing to the formation and persistence of forest. At the same time, the investigation raised questions about the administrative assumptions and inherited environmental knowledge through which the earlier interpretation had acquired authority.

The case thus demonstrates two connected forms of explanatory development. Historical and ecological evidence revised the causal reconstruction of particular landscapes, while critical investigation exposed the political and institutional circumstances through which environmental interpretations had been produced and maintained. These achievements are analytically distinguishable, although closely connected in the historical investigation itself.

The wider significance of the Guinea research lies partly in its demonstration that environmental history need not be organised around an anticipated narrative of decline. Human activities can contribute to environmental degradation, but they may also create, maintain, or enrich particular ecological relationships. The historian's task is to establish what occurred under the circumstances being investigated, rather than to interpret evidence according to an assumed progression from an undisturbed natural condition towards environmental destruction.

### Environmental determinism and historical contingency

The incorporation of environmental processes into historical explanation creates a continuing danger of attributing excessive causal significance to environmental conditions. A drought may contribute to agricultural failure, migration, or conflict, but its consequences depend upon technologies, institutions, stored resources, social inequalities, and political decisions. Similarly, a pathogen may influence demographic change, although its effects depend upon biological characteristics, patterns of contact, social organisation, and environmental circumstances.

Environmental influence must therefore be distinguished from environmental determination. The identification of a relevant climatic, ecological, or biological process does not establish that it was sufficient to produce a particular historical outcome, nor does it remove the need to investigate competing causes and mediating relationships. Strong environmental explanations reconstruct mechanisms and contingencies, showing how environmental processes operated within historically specific conditions.

This requirement is consistent with the broader standards of historical explanation. Environmental history contributes most effectively when it makes causal accounts more adequate through the investigation of previously neglected relationships, rather than substituting general environmental influences for the detailed reconstruction of historical events and processes.

### What actually changed?

The historical evidence supports several distinguishable conclusions about the transformation associated with environmental history. The field expanded the range of subjects and sources considered relevant to historical enquiry, bringing environmental processes and evidence into more sustained historical investigation. This expansion was important in its own right, although it did not invariably alter established explanations.

In particular cases, however, environmental research produced more consequential changes. Crosby's account of biological exchange demonstrated how organisms and their movements could become central to explanations of transatlantic expansion. Cronon's investigation of colonial New England reconsidered relationships among land use, economic organisation, and ecological transformation. Fairhead and Leach's research in Guinea went further in challenging an established interpretation of environmental degradation, while also exposing the historical assumptions through which that interpretation had been maintained.

These examples demonstrate different forms of explanatory development rather than successive stages in a single intellectual progression. The inclusion of additional evidence may support an existing interpretation, reveal its incompleteness, or justify its revision. In some circumstances, it may contribute to the reversal of a previously influential account. Environmental history also made the production of environmental knowledge itself a subject of critical enquiry, bringing questions of historical baselines, political authority, and the reliability of inherited categories into the investigation.

Environmental history did not thereby invent new universal principles of historical reasoning. Its contribution was more specific and historically demonstrable: it enlarged the scope of historical investigation and, in important cases, changed the explanations that could be defended on the available evidence. The significance of this achievement lies not in replacing established historical methods, but in showing why adequate historical explanations sometimes require the integration of environmental processes, human activities, and critical examination of the knowledge through which their relationships are reconstructed.


## Plants, Botanical Institutions, and Environmental Transformation

The history of plants provides an especially revealing example of environmental history's interdisciplinary character because plants have been integral to human subsistence, economic development, scientific investigation, and the transformation of landscapes. They have supplied food, fibres, timber, medicines, fuels, beverages, and materials used in construction and manufacture, while their cultivation and movement between regions have influenced agriculture, trade, consumption, and ecological relationships. These developments have often been investigated within separate historical specialisms, each concerned with different aspects of the relationships between plants and human societies.

Agricultural history has examined cultivation and production, economic history has investigated commodities, markets, and labour, and political history has addressed the institutions and imperial relationships through which plant resources were controlled and exchanged. Botanical history, meanwhile, has traced the development of plant knowledge, classification, collection, and scientific practice. Environmental history provides an opportunity to investigate relationships among these activities, asking how biological characteristics, human institutions, economic incentives, and environmental conditions interacted in particular historical circumstances. Its contribution lies not in replacing the established specialisms, but in bringing their evidence and explanations into a more integrated account where the historical problem warrants such integration.

### Botanical institutions and their historical functions

Botanic gardens developed through changing relationships among cultivation, medicine, teaching, scientific investigation, horticulture, and plant exchange. Their historical origins and subsequent functions cannot adequately be represented by a single institutional model or a straightforward progression from medicinal gardens to modern centres of botanical research. Spencer and Cross (2017) examine the development of botanical gardens and their relationship to plant science, emphasising antecedents extending beyond the familiar account of Renaissance European physic gardens. Their study provides an important foundation for understanding botanical institutions as historically changing organisations whose activities reflected different combinations of practical, educational, scientific, and economic purposes.

The historical significance of these institutions extended beyond the preservation, cultivation, and classification of plants. Collections, nurseries, cultivation experiments, and exchange networks could contribute to the movement of organisms between environments, while the knowledge generated through these activities influenced subsequent cultivation and distribution. Botanical institutions thus participated in both the production of plant knowledge and the material transformation of plant distributions. Their histories illustrate why scientific institutions may be understood not only through their intellectual achievements, but also through the biological and environmental consequences of their practices.

### Plant transfers and imperial networks

The global redistribution of plants provides a particularly important setting for examining the connections among biological processes, scientific institutions, commerce, and political power. Crosby's studies of the Columbian Exchange and ecological imperialism established the historical significance of biological movements associated with European expansion, showing how the transfer of organisms could contribute to demographic, agricultural, economic, and ecological transformations (Crosby, 1972, 1986). Such movements occurred through networks of exploration, settlement, cultivation, and trade, but their consequences depended also upon the characteristics of the organisms involved and the environments into which they were introduced.

Research on botanical institutions has further demonstrated that plant redistribution was frequently connected to imperial economic objectives. Brockway (1979) examined the role of British botanical institutions in projects involving commercially valuable plants, while Grove (1995) investigated the development of environmental knowledge and conservation concerns within colonial settings. These studies reveal different dimensions of the relationships among scientific knowledge, imperial administration, economic interests, and environmental transformation. Botanical institutions were not simply repositories of scientific information; their activities could form part of wider networks through which plants, expertise, and resources were mobilised for particular political and economic purposes.

Beinart and Middleton (2004) subsequently examined plant transfers through a broader historical perspective that brought cultivated crops, garden plants, weeds, and invasive organisms into a common field of analysis. Their approach emphasised the importance of integrating plant characteristics with political economy, institutions, and diverse forms of human participation. Taken together, these studies already establish much of the historical synthesis required for an environmental history of plant redistribution. The significance of botanical institutions as participants in environmental change is therefore not a new discovery of the present article. The more useful question concerns what becomes visible when the different explanatory perspectives developed within this scholarship are considered together.

### Three complementary explanations

The history of botanical institutions and plant redistribution can be approached through several complementary forms of explanation. An institutional history of botanic gardens may account for the development of collections, scientific teaching, classification, and cultivation practices, while an environmental history of plant transfers may investigate the biological and ecological consequences of introducing organisms into new environments. Political-economic history, in turn, may explain why particular plants were transferred, who controlled the institutions involved, which forms of labour supported cultivation, and how the benefits and harms associated with production were distributed.

These approaches are not necessarily competing explanations because they may address different aspects of the same historical process. A commercially valuable plant, for example, might be collected and transferred through botanical networks, cultivated under particular environmental conditions, and subsequently incorporated into a colonial production system. Its historical consequences could depend upon plant biology, cultivation practices, labour arrangements, political institutions, and economic demand. Understanding the resulting development may therefore require evidence extending across several historical and scientific fields.

The identification of these relationships does not, however, establish that every factor is equally important in explaining a particular outcome. An adequate explanation must demonstrate the relevance of the processes invoked and determine which relationships are necessary to account for the available historical evidence. Interdisciplinary integration is valuable when it improves the reconstruction and explanation of a historical development, rather than merely assembling a larger collection of potentially relevant influences.

### Botanical knowledge and colonial power

The movement of plants during imperial expansion cannot be understood solely as a history of scientific exchange (Drayton 2000). Botanical institutions operated within political and economic relationships that influenced which organisms were collected, whose knowledge was recognised, and how cultivation and production were organised. Colonial botanical networks could draw upon established local expertise while attributing scientific authority to imperial institutions, thereby transforming not only the circulation of plants but also the recognition and ownership of knowledge concerning them.

The development of plantation economies connected biological cultivation with land appropriation, labour coercion, commercial investment, and unequal access to resources. These relationships demonstrate why the environmental history of plant redistribution must extend beyond the movement of organisms between geographical regions. The conditions under which plants were collected, cultivated, and commercialised formed part of their historical significance, while the institutions governing those activities influenced both environmental outcomes and the distribution of economic benefits.

An environmental history concerned exclusively with biological transfers would risk obscuring these political and social relationships. Conversely, an institutional or economic history that neglected biological characteristics and environmental conditions might inadequately explain the success, failure, or consequences of particular transfers. The explanatory task is therefore to reconstruct how these processes interacted under specific historical circumstances, without assuming that their relative importance was uniform across different plants, places, or periods.

### Unintended environmental consequences

Plant redistribution also illustrates the distinction between historical intentions and environmental outcomes. Plants introduced for food, ornament, forestry, or other purposes may establish themselves beyond cultivation, and some subsequently become naturalised or spread extensively through their receiving environments. Their ecological consequences depend upon the organisms involved, local environmental conditions, subsequent disturbances, and changing management practices. The original intention to cultivate a plant may explain its introduction without adequately accounting for its later distribution or environmental effects.

These developments require historically and biologically specific interpretation. The introduction of an organism does not automatically establish ecological harm, just as its successful establishment does not necessarily demonstrate a beneficial adaptation from the perspective of the wider environment. Claims concerning invasion, degradation, or ecological benefit must be supported by evidence appropriate to the organisms and circumstances being investigated. Environmental history can contribute by reconstructing the relationships among original intentions, subsequent biological processes, and the changing human responses through which environmental outcomes developed.

### The contribution of the botanical case

The botanical and historical research associated with *Plants, People, Planet* provides an antecedent basis for examining these relationships across agriculture, horticulture, forestry, and the history of science. Its proposed *Age of Plants* synthesis draws attention to the extensive historical consequences of cultivation and plant redistribution, particularly where the biological properties of plants intersect with human institutions and economic activities. Such an approach has potential integrative value, although much of the relevant historical synthesis is already represented in established environmental-historical scholarship.

The present investigation does not establish that the PPP synthesis offers a distinctive explanatory improvement over the strongest available historical interpretations. Its potential contribution must therefore be evaluated through particular historical questions and evidence, rather than inferred from the breadth of the relationships it seeks to bring together. Within the present article, the botanical case serves a more specific purpose: it demonstrates why the historical investigation of plants may require biological, institutional, economic, political, and environmental evidence, while showing that the relevance of each perspective must be established in relation to the historical problem.

The history of plants consequently illustrates both the possibilities and the limitations of interdisciplinary environmental explanation. Biological processes and human institutions may be deeply connected in particular historical developments, but identifying those connections is only the beginning of explanatory enquiry. Their historical significance depends upon demonstrating how they operated, which outcomes they influenced, and whether their integration improves the explanation of the evidence.

## Nature, Culture, Colonialism, and Historical Interpretation

The expansion of environmental history was accompanied by disagreements concerning the relationship between material environments and the human interpretations through which they were understood. Some historians emphasised the reconstruction of environmental processes, including changes in vegetation, climate, soils, water systems, organisms, and ecological relationships. Others investigated the cultural categories through which societies described nature, distinguished cultivated from wild environments, and assigned value to particular landscapes. These approaches could appear to be in tension because they differed in their explanatory priorities and in the kinds of evidence they regarded as especially significant.

An approach treating nature simply as an independently existing material reality risked overlooking how environmental descriptions were shaped by cultural assumptions, political interests, and scientific concepts. Conversely, an approach concerned principally with nature as a cultural construction could neglect ecological and material processes operating independently of particular human interpretations. Relational perspectives introduced a further challenge by questioning whether nature and society should be treated as separate domains whose interactions subsequently required explanation. Landscapes, agricultural systems, settlements, and other historical phenomena might instead be investigated as relationships already constituted through human activities, organisms, institutions, and material processes.

These disagreements cannot be resolved merely by declaring that both nature and culture are important. Different approaches may identify different explanatory units, mechanisms, sources, and standards of interpretation. Their value depends upon the historical questions they illuminate and the adequacy of the evidence supporting their claims. Environmental history's intellectual development consequently involved not only the incorporation of environmental processes into historical scholarship, but also sustained criticism of the categories through which those processes and their relationships with human societies were represented.

### Wilderness and historical landscapes

William Cronon's discussion of wilderness challenged the assumption that wilderness could be understood simply as nature existing outside human history (Cronon, 1995). Ideas of wilderness were themselves historically formed and could obscure longstanding human relationships with landscapes, including Indigenous occupation and management. Landscapes described as untouched nature might therefore possess histories of human activity that prevailing cultural categories rendered difficult to recognise.

This argument does not require denying the material existence of forests, mountains, or ecological processes. Rather, it concerns the historical meanings assigned to environments and the consequences of interpreting particular landscapes as independent of human activity. Such interpretations can influence conservation policies, land-management practices, and the authority accorded to different accounts of environmental change. The historical investigation of wilderness consequently connects the reconstruction of material landscapes with criticism of the concepts through which those landscapes have been understood.

### South Asia: environmental categories and political authority

South Asian environmental historiography demonstrates how the classification and management of environments can become inseparable from histories of political power. Colonial forest administration did not simply respond to pre-existing environmental conditions; it also established categories, regulations, property arrangements, and restrictions that affected how landscapes were used and understood. Administrative descriptions of forests and other environments could become instruments through which access to resources was controlled and particular forms of land management were authorised or prohibited.

Guha's research on Himalayan forests and the wider scholarship on colonial resource management show why environmental change must be examined alongside conflicts over authority, livelihood, and access to land (Guha, 1989; Gadgil & Guha, 1992). The designation of territory as forest, wasteland, or protected land could have material consequences for the people and practices associated with it. Environmental categories were therefore not necessarily neutral descriptions of independently existing conditions; they could also become historically consequential institutions through which political relationships shaped environmental use.

### Africa: the politics of environmental interpretation

Fairhead and Leach's research in Guinea provides a particularly important demonstration of the historical consequences of environmental interpretation (Fairhead & Leach, 1995, 1996). Established accounts of environmental degradation were connected not only to descriptions of vegetation, but also to administrative assumptions concerning local land use and to policies justified through those assumptions. The interpretation of forest islands as remnants of environmental destruction contributed to a wider understanding of local practices as damaging to the landscape.

Reconstructing the history of these environments required investigation of both ecological change and the historical production of environmental knowledge. The evidence challenged inherited assumptions about the relationship between local activities and forest formation, revealing how administrative authority could sustain interpretations that did not adequately represent the processes involved. The case demonstrates why scientific and administrative expertise must remain open to historical criticism. The authority of a source may influence the acceptance of an environmental interpretation, but it does not by itself establish the accuracy of the history that interpretation presents.

### Latin America: beyond narratives of decline

Latin American environmental historiography has investigated relationships among colonialism, capitalism, conservation, resource extraction, and the transformation of landscapes. These enquiries have also raised questions about the interpretative assumptions through which environmental change is described. As Mark Carey (2009) observed in his historiographical review, accounts dominated by environmental decline may obscure other historically significant relationships, including adaptation, environmental creation, consumption, cultural landscapes, and changes in environmental knowledge.

Such perspectives encourage historians to examine environmental transformations without assuming that every change constitutes degradation or that historical developments can be adequately represented through a single narrative of loss. They also raise questions about the geographical boundaries of historical investigation. Political borders may be inadequate for explaining rivers, species movements, climatic processes, or commodity networks, whose histories extend across administrative territories and operate at different spatial and temporal scales. The boundaries of an environmental history must therefore be selected in relation to the processes being investigated rather than accepted simply because they correspond to existing political divisions.

### Australasia: settler histories and Indigenous environments

Environmental history in Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand has developed through relationships among Indigenous histories, settler colonialism, agricultural science, and distinctive ecological conditions. Robin and Griffiths (2004) emphasised the importance of scientific institutions and agricultural knowledge in shaping environmental policy and historical enquiry in Australasia, while also recognising differences between the environmental and historical circumstances of the two countries. Their work illustrates why a shared experience of settler colonialism does not establish a uniform history of environmental change or scientific investigation.

Histories organised principally around European settlement may obscure longstanding Indigenous relationships with landscapes. The environments encountered by settlers were not necessarily untouched natural settings, and their histories could include varied forms of occupation, burning, cultivation, harvesting, water management, and other practices. These relationships must be investigated within their particular regional and chronological circumstances rather than represented through a single general account of Indigenous environmental management.

Victor Steffensen provides an Indigenous-authored account of environmental practice in *Fire Country*, describing the development of his understanding of cultural burning through relationships with Elders and close attention to particular landscapes (Steffensen, 2020). His account demonstrates the importance of situated knowledge, continuing practice, and relationships through which environmental understanding is transmitted. It does not, however, justify attributing one uniform fire regime to all Aboriginal peoples or regions. The historical significance of Indigenous environmental practices must be established through appropriate evidence and attention to the distinct peoples, places, and traditions involved.

### Indigenous knowledge and the historical archive

Indigenous historiography raises questions extending beyond the inclusion of additional evidence in established environmental histories. Colonial archives may document administrative decisions, scientific investigations, and official interpretations while inadequately representing the knowledge, practices, experiences, and responsibilities of the peoples whose environments were being governed. Historical reconstruction may therefore require attention to oral histories, language, material evidence, community-held knowledge, and place-based forms of understanding that are not adequately represented within conventional documentary records.

These sources also challenge assumptions about the categories through which historical knowledge is organised. Ambelin Kwaymullina and Blaze Kwaymullina (2010), for example, examine Aboriginal understandings of knowledge, time, and space in relation to law, offering an Indigenous-authored intellectual perspective relevant to the interpretation of historical categories. Their argument should not be converted into a generalised claim about the land-management practices of all Aboriginal peoples. Its significance lies in demonstrating why historical interpretation may need to engage with different intellectual traditions rather than simply incorporate selected observations into an existing explanatory framework.

Such engagement requires appropriate methods, contextual interpretation, and recognition of the relationships through which knowledge is maintained. Indigenous knowledge should not be treated as a homogeneous body of ecological information or merely as data available for extraction from the responsibilities, authorities, and practices within which it has meaning. Different Indigenous peoples possess distinct intellectual traditions, historical experiences, and systems of authority, and these differences must remain visible in historical scholarship.

Indigenous-authored historical research also challenges the assumption that Indigenous history must be organised principally around colonial encounter and dispossession. Shino Konishi (2019) examines Indigenous critiques of settler-colonial historiography and the possibilities of extra-colonial histories centred on Indigenous worlds. This approach does not diminish the importance of colonial history, but broadens the questions through which Indigenous histories may be investigated, including histories whose significance is not determined primarily by their relationship to European settlement.

The contribution of Indigenous scholarship is consequently not simply the provision of alternative evidence for an otherwise unchanged historical narrative. It may require reconsideration of the categories used to describe environments, the historical baselines against which change is measured, and the authority assigned to different forms of knowledge. These questions are central to environmental history's development as a field concerned not only with reconstructing environmental change, but also with examining how environmental histories are produced and whose interpretations are recognised.

### The limits of a unified environmental history

The international and intellectual diversity of environmental history makes it difficult to represent the field as a single, uniform programme. Different traditions have emphasised ecological processes, political conflict, colonial knowledge, environmental values, scientific institutions, and Indigenous relationships with place. Their contributions overlap, but they are not interchangeable, and their differences reflect distinct historical problems, intellectual commitments, and evidential circumstances.

Environmental history's explanatory significance lies partly in its capacity to bring these questions into historical investigation without eliminating the distinctions necessary for rigorous enquiry. Its development has enlarged the range of relationships considered historically important while exposing the limitations of interpretations that treat environmental knowledge as independent of the institutions, categories, and political circumstances through which it is produced. The field's intellectual unity is therefore qualified rather than absolute: it consists in a shared concern with changing human–environment relationships, pursued through diverse and sometimes competing approaches to historical explanation.

## Environmental History, Deep Time, and the Anthropocene

Environmental history has also challenged conventional assumptions about the temporal boundaries of historical explanation. Political events may unfold over days or years, institutions may persist for centuries, and environmental processes such as forest regeneration, soil formation, climatic variation, and geological change may operate over substantially longer durations. These different temporal processes are not necessarily independent. Their consequences may converge within particular historical developments, making it necessary to investigate relationships that extend beyond the timescales conventionally associated with political events or institutional change.

The significance of this perspective lies not simply in the study of longer periods, but in the recognition that processes operating at different rates may contribute to the same historical outcome. An agricultural crisis, for example, may involve immediate weather conditions, established farming practices, longer-term changes in soils, and institutional decisions extending across generations. Explaining such an event requires more than identifying each potentially relevant influence. The historian must establish how these processes interacted, which relationships were causally significant, and how evidence obtained at different temporal resolutions can be combined into an adequate reconstruction.

Developments in environmental reconstruction have expanded the possibilities of such investigation. Scientific evidence can extend historical enquiry beyond the periods and circumstances documented in written records, allowing historians to investigate environmental changes that were not directly observed or recorded by the societies affected by them. Yet the enlargement of historical time also introduces interpretative difficulties. Geological, ecological, archaeological, and documentary records may describe different processes at different resolutions, while their uncertainties and geographical coverage may vary considerably. Their integration therefore requires careful reasoning about evidential relevance and causal relationships rather than the assumption that a longer timescale necessarily produces a better historical explanation.

### Climate change and planetary history

Anthropogenic climate change has intensified these questions by making relationships between human activities and planetary processes increasingly consequential for historical interpretation. Dipesh Chakrabarty's *The Climate of History: Four Theses* (2009) argued that climate change challenges the longstanding separation between human and natural history, raising questions about how historians should understand human activities whose consequences extend into Earth-system processes (Chakrabarty, 2009).

Human activities can now be investigated in relation to planetary transformations whose effects extend beyond the lifetimes and immediate experiences of the populations and institutions that contributed to them. This development raises questions about historical agency, collective responsibility, and the relationships between differentiated human societies and processes operating at planetary scales. The significance of these questions is not that human history has suddenly become connected to nature, but that particular forms of human activity have acquired consequences that require historical and scientific investigation across unusually extensive spatial and temporal relationships.

The concept of the Anthropocene represents one response to these developments. It expresses the proposition that human activities have become sufficiently consequential for planetary systems to warrant consideration in geological as well as historical terms. However, the evidence that human activities have significantly altered Earth-system processes must be distinguished from the question of whether the Anthropocene should receive formal recognition as a geological epoch. The historical and scientific significance of anthropogenic planetary change does not depend upon resolving that stratigraphic question.

A further distinction concerns the attribution of causation. Identifying humanity as a planetary influence does not establish that humanity constitutes a unified historical actor whose members contributed equally to the processes involved. Industrialisation, fossil-fuel consumption, colonial expansion, economic institutions, and resource use have developed unevenly across societies and historical periods. An explanation that attributes planetary transformation to humanity without examining these differences may obscure the political and economic histories necessary to understand how particular changes occurred.

Environmental history consequently has an important role in connecting planetary processes with the differentiated activities and institutions through which human environmental impacts developed. Its contribution is not to replace historical specificity with an undifferentiated account of human influence, but to investigate how processes operating at extensive spatial and temporal scales became connected to particular histories of production, consumption, settlement, technology, and political power.

### Historical responsibility and environmental causation

The distinction between environmental causation and historical responsibility becomes especially important when the consequences of human activities extend across societies and generations. A climatic process may affect populations that contributed little to its causes, while collective environmental outcomes may arise from activities distributed among institutions, technologies, populations, and historical periods. Establishing how a particular environmental change occurred is therefore not identical to determining who bears responsibility for its consequences.

Historical explanation must reconstruct the relevant causal relationships before questions of responsibility can be adequately assessed, although the two enquiries may draw upon overlapping evidence. The identification of a physical or biological mechanism does not by itself establish the distribution of responsibility among historical actors, just as an account of political or economic institutions does not necessarily explain the environmental processes through which their activities produced consequences.

Earth-system science and historical scholarship consequently investigate overlapping but distinguishable questions. Earth-system science examines physical, chemical, and biological processes at planetary scales, while historical scholarship reconstructs differentiated human activities, institutions, experiences, and interpretations. Their integration may enrich explanation by connecting processes that neither field could adequately reconstruct in isolation, but it does not eliminate their different purposes, methods, or evidential requirements.

Environmental history's contribution lies in making these relationships subjects of systematic historical investigation while preserving the distinctions necessary for their explanation. The enlargement of historical time and geographical scope is valuable where it reveals relevant processes and relationships, not because greater temporal or spatial extent constitutes an explanatory achievement in itself.

## Environmental History and the APS Framework

Environmental history has several conceptual affinities with the Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework, particularly in its attention to biological activity, changing relationships, and the spatial and temporal dimensions of explanation. These affinities must nevertheless be interpreted within the different intellectual purposes of the two enterprises. APS is concerned with biological organisation and with the attribution of agency, process, and explanatory scale in accounts of living systems. Environmental history investigates changing relationships between human societies and environments through time, using evidence and methods appropriate to particular historical questions. Their principal explanatory problems are therefore not identical, and the achievements of environmental history do not depend upon APS.

### Agency

Environmental history has demonstrated that non-human organisms may be historically consequential. Plants, pathogens, animals, and other organisms can influence human activities through their biological characteristics, interactions, and responses to environmental conditions. The movement of cultivated plants, the spread of pathogens, and changes in ecological relationships may become important elements in explaining historical developments, even where the organisms involved were not the principal subjects of earlier historical narratives.

Historical causal significance, however, does not by itself establish biological agency. Within APS, biological agency is understood as viability-oriented organisational activity, whose attribution depends upon the organisation and activity of living systems rather than merely upon their consequences for other entities. A plant may qualify as a biological agent under appropriate organisational criteria and may also participate in historically consequential environmental processes. By contrast, a river, drought, or geological event may influence historical outcomes without thereby becoming a biological agent in the APS sense. The distinction is therefore between the causal contribution of an entity or process to a historical outcome and the organisational criteria required for attributing biological agency.

Environmental historians and scholars working in the environmental humanities may employ the term *agency* in broader relational or material senses. Such usage should not automatically be regarded as mistaken, since its meaning and explanatory function depend upon the questions and conceptual commitments of the enquiry in which it appears. The APS definition is more restrictive because it addresses a different theoretical problem: the conditions under which biological organisation warrants the attribution of agency. Distinguishing these usages allows productive conceptual comparison without requiring environmental history to adopt the terminology or theoretical commitments of APS.

### Process

Environmental history investigates transformations that cross conventional divisions between nature and society. Cultivated landscapes, species distributions, agricultural systems, and environmental institutions persist and change through ongoing relationships among organisms, material conditions, and human activities. Their histories cannot always be adequately reconstructed by treating the entities involved as static objects or by separating environmental processes from the social relationships through which they acquire historical significance.

This emphasis has affinities with the processual orientation of APS, which examines biological organisation through the activities and relationships that sustain and transform living systems. Nevertheless, similarity in explanatory orientation does not establish theoretical dependence. Environmental historians developed their approaches through distinct intellectual traditions and historical investigations, and their explanations need not employ APS terminology to remain valid or informative. The significance of any proposed connection must therefore be assessed through the particular explanatory work that the comparison performs.

### Scale

Environmental history frequently connects local developments with regional, continental, and planetary processes, while also investigating relationships among events and transformations operating over different durations. The movement of organisms, the development of agricultural systems, and the consequences of climatic change may require investigation across spatial and temporal boundaries that do not correspond to conventional political or institutional divisions.

APS likewise treats scale as an important dimension of explanation, but the relevant distinctions are not necessarily the same. Geographical extent and historical duration must be distinguished from the organisational scales involved in biological explanation. A process operating across a continent is not thereby a higher level of biological organisation, just as a development extending across centuries does not automatically constitute a distinct organisational level.

In both historical and biological investigation, the explanatory significance of scale depends upon the question being asked and the processes being reconstructed. The identification of multiple scales is not sufficient to establish explanatory advance; it must be shown why particular spatial, temporal, or organisational distinctions are necessary to understand the phenomenon under investigation.

### Explanatory independence

The relationship between environmental history and APS is consequently best understood as one of potential conceptual dialogue rather than explanatory subsumption. Environmental history provides independently developed investigations that may illuminate questions concerning biological activity, environmental relationships, persistence, and explanatory scale. APS, in turn, may offer conceptual distinctions that prove useful when examining some of these cases, particularly where different meanings of agency or scale might otherwise be conflated.

The existence of such possibilities does not establish that APS provides a general theory of environmental history or that its terminology improves explanations already available within historical scholarship. Any claim of distinctive explanatory contribution would require a specific investigation showing how APS distinctions clarify a historical problem or improve an explanation when compared with the strongest relevant alternatives.

Environmental history's achievements remain independently intelligible and assessable. Its contribution to the history of ideas lies in the development of scholarly practices that enlarged and, in particular cases, transformed historical explanation. Its conceptual relationship with APS may be worth investigating, but that relationship neither constitutes the historical significance of environmental history nor provides independent confirmation of APS's explanatory superiority.

## Conclusion — What Environmental History Changed

Environmental history emerged from a long and diverse history of enquiry into relationships between human societies and their environments. Natural history, geography, ecological science, landscape studies, agricultural and botanical scholarship, and varied traditions of environmental knowledge had already established that human activities and environmental conditions were closely connected. The formation of environmental history as a recognisable academic field during the later twentieth century therefore marked neither the discovery of environmental influence nor the invention of an entirely new logic of historical reasoning. Its distinctive contribution lay in the development and consolidation of scholarly approaches that made changing human–environment relationships increasingly central to historical investigation and explanation.

The significance of this development becomes clearest when the enlargement of historical enquiry is distinguished from changes in historical explanation. Environmental history brought greater attention to forests, soils, organisms, climatic processes, and ecological transformations, while incorporating forms of scientific evidence that could reveal aspects of the past inadequately represented in conventional historical records. These developments expanded the subjects and resources of historical scholarship, but their explanatory importance depended upon the historical questions they addressed. The inclusion of environmental evidence did not necessarily transform an established interpretation; its value was demonstrated when that evidence clarified causal relationships, challenged inherited assumptions, or supported a more adequate reconstruction of particular events and processes.

The historical investigations considered in this article illustrate different forms of such achievement. Crosby's analysis of biological exchange demonstrated how the movement of organisms contributed to the consequences of transatlantic expansion. Cronon's investigation of colonial New England brought Indigenous and colonial land use into a connected account of ecological and economic transformation. Fairhead and Leach's research in Guinea challenged influential interpretations of environmental degradation, showing how local practices could contribute to forest formation while exposing the administrative assumptions through which contrary interpretations had acquired authority. These studies did not establish a single environmental-historical method, but they demonstrated how attention to environmental processes and the production of environmental knowledge could change what historians were justified in concluding.

The international development of environmental history further revealed that environmental change cannot always be separated from the political and intellectual circumstances through which it is interpreted. Colonial administration, scientific institutions, economic interests, and inherited categories have influenced both the transformation of landscapes and the authority accorded to particular explanations. Indigenous-authored scholarship introduces additional questions concerning historical evidence, intellectual authority, relationships with place, and the categories through which environmental histories are constructed. These contributions cannot adequately be represented as variations within a single intellectual tradition or merely as supplementary information added to an established historical narrative.

The history of plants and botanical institutions provides a particularly revealing illustration of these relationships. Cultivation, biological redistribution, scientific investigation, imperial expansion, labour, commerce, and ecological transformation have frequently formed connected historical processes, although their relative importance has varied across particular circumstances. The integration of botanical, institutional, economic, and environmental perspectives can therefore enrich historical understanding when it demonstrates relationships necessary to explaining the evidence. Such integration is not itself a new explanatory theory, and its contribution must be assessed in relation to the substantial scholarship that has already investigated these connections.

Environmental history has also enlarged the spatial and temporal relationships considered relevant to historical explanation. Environmental processes may operate across political boundaries and over durations extending beyond the immediate circumstances of human decisions, while contemporary planetary change raises questions that connect differentiated human histories with Earth-system processes. Yet greater spatial or temporal scope does not automatically produce better explanations. Historical understanding continues to depend upon the identification of relevant processes, the reconstruction of their relationships, and careful assessment of the evidence through which causal claims are supported.

Environmental history's enduring contribution is consequently neither an unprecedented intellectual revolution nor simply the addition of nature to an otherwise unchanged account of the past. It is a substantial, historically varied reorientation of enquiry through which environmental processes, human activities, and the production of environmental knowledge became increasingly prominent in the investigation and explanation of particular developments. Its strongest achievements demonstrate that the histories of human societies and their changing environments cannot always be adequately reconstructed in isolation, and that the knowledge through which those histories are understood must itself remain open to historical criticism.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How did environmental history emerge from earlier intellectual traditions, and what distinctive contribution did it make to the scope, evidence, and interpretation of historical explanation?

### Architectural Role

This article examines environmental history as a development in the history of ideas. It distinguishes the longstanding recognition of environmental influences from the emergence of scholarly practices that made changing human–environment relationships explicit subjects of historical investigation, causal reconstruction, and critical interpretation.

Its principal contribution is to distinguish the expansion of historical subjects and evidence from demonstrable changes in historical explanation.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

Historical geography, natural history, ecological science, landscape studies, agricultural and botanical history, and earlier traditions of environmental knowledge provide the intellectual and evidential setting.

These are historical antecedents and conceptual resources, not necessary stages in a single developmental sequence. Intellectual precedence, demonstrated historical influence, and retrospective conceptual affinity must be distinguished.

### Subsequent Explanatory Developments

Environmental history provides a setting for investigating biological exchange, ecological transformation, colonial environmental knowledge, Indigenous environmental histories, botanical institutions, environmental governance, and the relationships between human history and planetary change.

These developments are historically diverse and do not constitute a necessary progression towards a unified theory of environmental history or APS.

### Related Explanatory Questions

When does the inclusion of environmental evidence change an established historical explanation rather than merely enlarge its subject matter? What evidence warrants attributing historical change to biological or ecological processes? How should competing interpretations of environmental change be assessed? How do political institutions, colonial categories, and Indigenous knowledge affect historical reconstruction? What spatial and temporal scales are appropriate to particular historical questions?

### Position Within APS

Environmental history provides independently developed examples relevant to APS's concerns with biological activity, process, and explanatory scale.

Historically consequential environmental causation must be distinguished from biological agency, which APS defines as viability-oriented organisational activity. Geographical and historical scales must likewise be distinguished from the organisational scales relevant to biological explanation.

Environmental history neither depends upon APS nor establishes its explanatory superiority. Any distinctive contribution from applying APS distinctions to historical cases would require explicit comparison with the strongest available alternative explanations.
