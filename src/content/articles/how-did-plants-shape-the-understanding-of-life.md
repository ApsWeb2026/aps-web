# How Did Plants Shape the Scientific Understanding of Life?

## Introduction â€” Why plants matter to the history of biological explanation

What has the study of plants contributed to the scientific understanding of Life? The question differs from asking how botany became a discipline, how plants were classified, or how useful species were discovered. It asks whether, and in what circumstances, investigations of plants changed the kinds of explanations that could be given of living things. Plants have repeatedly supplied both familiar examples of life and troublesome cases for theories framed around animals. They grow and reproduce, exchange materials with their surroundings, maintain differentiated structures, and respond to changing conditions. Yet many of these activities occur without the conspicuous locomotion, nervous systems, or behaviour that have commonly made animal life appear paradigmatic.

The historical significance of plants cannot be reduced to the claim that investigators gradually discovered them to be more active than previously supposed. Many traditions had long recognised the practical importance of plant growth, seasonal change, environmental sensitivity, and reproduction. Nor was there a single sequence in which ancient observation yielded to modern experiment and finally to contemporary theories of cognition. Knowledge was produced in gardens, fields, medical settings, laboratories, colonial encounters, and communities whose practices and intellectual priorities were not identical. The question is how particular investigations made plant processes describable, comparable, experimentally tractable, and relevant to wider accounts of living organisation.

This article argues that plants contributed to biological understanding in several distinguishable ways. They helped articulate the criteria by which living beings were recognised; supported comparative accounts of structure, growth, and reproduction; prompted new techniques for studying physiological processes; challenged sharp distinctions between plant and animal capacities; and continue to expose uncertainties about the attribution of agency, cognition, and consciousness. These contributions were neither uniform nor cumulative in a simple sense. Different investigators asked different questions, employed different instruments, and worked within different philosophical and institutional settings.

The account draws on the earlier historical investigations preserved in **Plants, People, Planet**â€”especially its essays on Theophrastus, the history and philosophy of plant science, Charles Darwin, and plant cognitionâ€”while revisiting their interpretations against a more demanding historical standard. The cases are selected not because they form a universal or continuous chronology, but because they allow a comparison between an explanatory problem, evidence supplied through the study of plants, and the conclusions contemporaries were prepared to draw. The objective is not to recruit the past to a present theoretical programme. It is to establish what plants made visible, questionable, or explainable at particular moments in the history of biological inquiry.

## Ancient investigations â€” Plants and the classification of living beings

In ancient Greek natural philosophy, plants were central to the problem of distinguishing living from non-living things and identifying capacities shared among different kinds of organisms. In *De Anima* II.2â€“4 (413aâ€“415a), Aristotle's discussions of soul did not use the modern sense of an immaterial personal self. The soul was associated with the capacities through which a living body performed its characteristic activities. Nutrition, growth, and reproduction were particularly important because they were attributed to plants as well as animals. Plants therefore served as a limiting case: an account of life could not require sensation or voluntary movement if beings without those capacities were nevertheless alive.

This is a conceptual contribution of considerable importance. A definition of life based exclusively on the conspicuous properties of animals would omit organisms that ancient investigators had good reasons to regard as living. By attending to plants, an account of life could distinguish basic nutritive and reproductive activities from other capacities without assuming that every living being exhibited the same range of functions. The distinction should not be mistaken for a modern biological definition or for evidence that Aristotle anticipated later accounts of metabolism. Its significance lies in the structure of the problem he addressed: what capacities are sufficient to characterise a living being, and which are peculiar to particular kinds of living beings?

Theophrastus approached plants through sustained attention to their diversity, forms, habitats, cultivation, and reproductive characteristics. Works conventionally known as **Historia Plantarum** and **De Causis Plantarum** offered overlapping but distinguishable descriptive and explanatory projects. Comparison among trees, shrubs, herbs, cultivated plants, and plants of different localities created opportunities to ask which features were stable, which varied with circumstances, and how particular growth forms might be explained. Theophrastus did not simply enumerate species in a modern taxonomic sense. He investigated plant differences in relation to growth, propagation, environment, and the practical knowledge of those who cultivated or used them.

The earlier PPP essay on Theophrastus rightly places weight on the breadth of this botanical enterprise. A more cautious historical reading, however, should avoid calling him the founder of an uninterrupted scientific discipline or presenting later botanical research as the straightforward fulfilment of his programme. His categories, purposes, and standards of explanation belonged to a different intellectual world. His contribution was to make plant diversity an object of extended comparison and causal inquiry within that world.

Plants also reveal the limits of simple contrasts between observation and theory. Descriptions of form already depend on decisions about what is comparable; explanations of growth depend on assumptions about causes and characteristic activities. Ancient plant inquiry thus demonstrates that recognising a plant as alive and explaining how it lives are related but different achievements. That distinction remains useful throughout the history considered here.

## Multiple traditions of plant knowledge

A history restricted to named European natural philosophers and laboratory investigators would misrepresent both the geographical distribution of plant knowledge and the conditions under which botanical sciences developed. People have long investigated plants in order to cultivate crops, manage landscapes, prepare medicines, recognise seasonal changes, produce materials, and maintain relationships with places. Such knowledge may involve careful comparison, long-term observation, controlled practice, classification, and explanation. Its intellectual organisation cannot be assessed simply by asking whether it resembled a European botanical textbook.

At the same time, it would be misleading to combine all non-European traditions into one category of â€˜Indigenous knowledgeâ€™ or to treat them as interchangeable stores of useful observations. Knowledge practices differ among peoples, environments, histories, and institutions. Their authority, transmission, and purposes are often embedded in relationships among people, plants, land, and responsibility that are not captured by the distinction between theoretical and practical science. An adequate historical account must therefore be specific about whose knowledge is being discussed and how it was produced and represented.

Australian histories provide an instructive case. Aboriginal peoples developed detailed knowledge of plants and their seasonal, ecological, medicinal, and material significance over long periods. European explorers and collectors encountered landscapes already understood, used, and managed by their inhabitants. The botanical collections and descriptions that entered European scientific networks did not emerge in an informational vacuum. They were shaped by encounters, local guidance, naming practices, labour, and the unequal power relations of colonisation. The surviving European records may nevertheless obscure the contributions of those from whom information was obtained.

A particularly revealing case comes from northern Queensland during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In her detailed study of the Queensland frontier, Marcia Langton (2016, pp. 221â€“240) examines the relationship between colonial botanical work, Aboriginal plant-food economies, and the expansion of pastoral settlement. Her central concern is not simply the transfer of useful botanical observations to Europeans. It is the simultaneous documentation of Aboriginal uses of plants and the destruction of the ecological and economic conditions that sustained those uses. The spread of cattle, displacement from country, and growing dependence on rations belong to the history she reconstructs, rather than forming a detachable background to botanical discovery.

Langton's study draws attention to the colonial documentation of Aboriginal plant knowledge and the economic uses of native vegetation. Such records provide evidence of botanical knowledge held by Aboriginal communities, but they also require interpretation in the context of pastoral expansion, competition for plant resources, and the disruption of Aboriginal food economies (Langton 2016). The historical significance of these records therefore extends beyond the identification of useful plants to the circumstances under which knowledge was collected, represented, and circulated. Their existence does not, by itself, establish that European investigators adopted Aboriginal explanations of Life.

Langton also contrasts such records with botanical investigation directed towards pasture improvement and the economic interests of settlers (pp. 234â€“236). The same colonial institutions that gathered and classified information about native vegetation operated within a society transforming access to land and food. Botanical knowledge therefore cannot be treated as a politically neutral inventory that simply accumulated as scientific understanding advanced. The questions asked, the plants valued, and the knowledge credited were shaped by institutions and unequal power. Langton's study provides a specific Indigenous-authored historical interpretation of this process; claims extending beyond her analysis would require direct examination of the underlying Roth and Bailey records.

Terri Janke's study of Indigenous knowledge and the commercialisation of plants (2018) raises a related, though distinct, question about recognition, control, and appropriation. The historical and contemporary issues should not be collapsed: Langton reconstructs colonial transformations of botanical knowledge and Aboriginal economies, while Janke addresses the recognition of Indigenous knowledge in commercial and legal settings. Together they show why histories of botanical inquiry must attend not only to what was learned about plants, but also to whose knowledge was recorded, whose authority was acknowledged, and who benefited from its circulation.

The recovered PPP articles on First Australians and Robert Brown offer further leads to Australian botanical encounters, but do not independently establish the roles of particular Aboriginal informants. The Queensland case must not be made representative of all Indigenous plant knowledge: it is a geographically and historically delimited example of how knowledge, colonial authority, and environmental transformation intersected.

The resulting lesson is methodological rather than celebratory. To understand how plants shaped scientific knowledge, one must investigate not only experiments and publications but also the social routes by which plant observations became authoritative evidence. Some traditions entered the written record through their own institutions; others were reframed within colonial systems of collection, naming, property, and expertise. Their relationships cannot be reconstructed responsibly by placing them along a single ladder of scientific progress.

## Early modern transformations â€” Anatomy, experiment, and vegetative powers

Early modern investigations of plants were conducted within changing combinations of natural philosophy, medicine, horticulture, anatomy, and experimental practice. The transformation was not simply the abandonment of old beliefs in favour of observation. Earlier concepts of vegetative powers, generation, and material processes continued to be debated, while instruments and techniques opened new ways of examining plant structures and operations. Plants were significant because they made problems of growth, nourishment, reproduction, and the relationship between visible form and internal activity difficult to avoid.

Francis Bacon's experimental natural history, including *Sylva Sylvarum* (1627), encouraged attention to processes, operations, and the systematic accumulation and testing of observations. The relevance of plants within Baconian inquiry must nevertheless be established through particular texts, not inferred from a general association between Bacon and experimental science. In seventeenth-century discussions, questions about the powers of plants also intersected with disputes over whether sensitivity, appetite, or perception could be attributed beyond animals. The meanings of these terms varied, and they should not be translated directly into contemporary claims about consciousness.

Guy de La Brosse, whose *De la nature, vertu, et utilitÃ© des plantes* (1628) connected botanical properties with chemical and medicinal inquiry, was also associated with proposals for a royal medicinal garden in Paris. Chemical techniques formed part of the periodâ€™s investigation of plant properties, although particular experiments by La Brosse are not assessed here. Margaret Cavendish, notably in *Observations upon Experimental Philosophy* (1666) and *Grounds of Natural Philosophy* (1668), approached nature through a different philosophical framework in which matter and perception were understood in ways that complicate modern divisions between the animate and inanimate. Her arguments belong to a debate over the nature of matter and living activity, not to an experimental demonstration of plant experience.

These examples are valuable precisely because they do not form a single research programme. They show that plants could be mobilised in competing accounts of matter, powers, sensation, and organic function. Baldassarri's 2026 introduction to a special issue on plant life helps establish the historiographical relevance of these debates. It does not license detailed claims about a separate specialist contribution that has not been examined. The article should therefore explain the questions and settings of early modern plant inquiry without attributing a unified doctrine to Bacon, La Brosse, or Cavendish.

Anatomical investigation provides a complementary line of development. Microscopic and comparative studies of plant tissues offered new descriptions of internal organisation, while studies of reproduction and generation prompted disagreements about what visible structures meant and how they operated. These investigations made it increasingly difficult to treat the plant merely as a static form. Yet it would be equally mistaken to suggest that the use of instruments automatically produced correct causal explanations. New observations could be interpreted through older assumptions, and different researchers often disagreed about what they saw.

The historical contribution of this period lies in the diversification of questions and practices. Plant life became a site at which natural philosophers, physicians, gardeners, and experimental investigators contested the relationships among structure, material process, and characteristic activity. This is a more defensible claim than describing the seventeenth century as the moment when plants were first recognised as active or sentient.

## Plant physiology â€” Making living processes experimentally investigable

### Three testable changes in explanatory practice

**Water movement and quantitative measurement.** Stephen Hales's **Vegetable Staticks** (1727) investigated the movement of sap and relations between plants and air through quantitative, instrument-assisted experiments. The explanatory comparator was an account resting principally on visible growth or the inherited language of vegetative powers. Hales's work made the rates and conditions of plant water movement subjects of measurement, although the existence of a published experimental programme does not establish that every mechanism he proposed was correct. The significance claimed here concerns the documented quantitative experimental programme, not independently verified numerical results or the correctness of every proposed mechanism.

**Plants and the transformation of air.** Jan Ingenhousz's **Experiments upon Vegetables** (1779) explicitly investigated how plants altered air in sunlight and in darkness. Its very experimental contrastâ€”light versus shade, and differences among plant partsâ€”allowed explanations of plantâ€“air relationships to be tied to manipulable conditions rather than merely to the observation that plants flourish in suitable surroundings. It would be anachronistic to ascribe a fully modern biochemical theory of photosynthesis to Ingenhousz. The comparison of conditions is established by the original work; the account does not rely on unverified numerical results or priority claims.

**Localised sensitivity and growth response.** In 1880, Charles and Francis Darwin tested the sensitivity of root tips and the bending of regions above them. Chapter XI of *The Power of Movement in Plants* (1880, pp. 523â€“545) explicitly compares earlier experimental claims by Ciesielski and Sachs, then describes attempts to discriminate among them. Here the strongest comparator is not a hypothetical belief that plants never move, but rival explanations of where stimulation is detected and how curvature follows. This is a concrete instance of plant evidence adjudicating a physiological dispute. The Darwins' interpretation must not be confused with a modern account of plant signalling or a demonstration of a nervous system.

These cases show different kinds of explanatory changeâ€”quantification, experimental discrimination of environmental conditions, and localisation of response. They are not interchangeable proofs of a single doctrine of Life.

The emergence of experimental plant physiology altered the evidential conditions under which living activity could be studied. Growth, movement, gas exchange, water relations, and responses to environmental variation could be approached as processes whose conditions and rates were open to investigation. Plants did not become living only when instruments registered their activities. Rather, methods increasingly allowed investigators to isolate questions about the mechanisms, circumstances, and limits of those activities.

A central issue was the difference between observing a response and explaining it. A leaf's movement or a shoot's change in orientation could be described, but explanation required identifying relevant conditions, comparing alternatives, and determining what kind of causal relation the evidence supported. Experiments on light, water, gravity, and other environmental factors made it possible to distinguish some processes that ordinary observation might group together. Quantification, repeatability, and instrument design mattered because they changed what counted as an adequate answer to a physiological question.

The history was neither uniform nor wholly internal to botany. Investigators drew on chemistry, physics, medicine, agriculture, and natural philosophy. Explanations of plant nutrition, for example, were transformed by the developing study of gases and chemical exchange, but these developments depended on particular experimental systems and conceptual assumptions. Similarly, the identification of structures did not by itself explain the processes they supported. The distinction between anatomy and physiology remained productive because the two fields could provide different kinds of evidence about the same living organism.

Augustin-Pyramus de Candolle offers a particularly revealing case. A recent study by Quentin Hiernaux (2026) identifies de Candolle as an important figure for examining historical distinctions among excitability, irritability, and sentience. This observation is limited to the published abstract of Hiernauxâ€™s study; neither its full argument nor the relevant de Candolle passages has been independently assessed for this article. These terms must be read within their contemporary conceptual settings. They do not establish that de Candolle demonstrated subjective experience in plants, nor do they justify translating every nineteenth-century reference to sensibility into the language of modern plant cognition.

The larger point is that the study of plants put pressure on explanatory categories derived from animals. What does it mean for a living system to respond when it lacks muscles, locomotion, or a nervous system? How should an investigator distinguish a mechanical consequence of environmental change from a regulated physiological process? Which similarities between plant and animal responses are experimentally significant, and which are only analogies? Such questions required methods capable of identifying mechanisms and circumstances, rather than resting on verbal resemblances.

Physiological experimentation also revealed limits. A measurable response may establish sensitivity to a stimulus without demonstrating a particular functional interpretation. A change that benefits a plant in one context may not show that the plant selected that outcome, still less that it was conscious of doing so. The history of plant physiology is therefore important not because it progressively confirmed stronger attributions of mentality, but because it sharpened the relation between observed phenomena and explanatory claims.

This distinction is crucial to the article's thesis. Plant physiology made activities of living organisation experimentally investigable across forms of life that differ markedly in appearance and behaviour. It enlarged the repertoire of phenomena to which general biological explanation had to answer. The result was not one new definition of life, but a more differentiated set of questions about the material processes and conditions of living activity.

## Evolution and continuity â€” Darwin and the biological significance of plant movement

Charles Darwin's work on plants is often remembered less prominently than his writings on natural selection and human descent, but it formed an important part of his investigation of living processes. Studies of climbing plants, insectivorous plants, fertilisation, and movement raised questions about adaptation, variation, and the relationship between form and activity. Plants offered experimental opportunities to investigate phenomena that did not fit simple pictures of organisms as passive structures or of biological action as necessarily animal-like.

In **The Power of Movement in Plants** (1880), Charles and Francis Darwin examined movements associated with growth and environmental conditions. The book brought together observations and experiments on diverse plant structures and investigated patterns of movement, including responses to light and gravity. Its explanatory importance lay partly in the effort to relate visible movements to underlying growth processes and to compare different kinds of movement within a broader account. The Darwinsâ€™ more general theory of circumnutation was disputed, and the significance of particular experiments must be distinguished from the validity of that broader interpretation. It was not a demonstration that plants possessed animal nervous systems or conscious intentions.

The Darwinian setting also altered the significance of comparison. Similarities and differences among organisms could be investigated against questions about descent, adaptation, and the historical emergence of biological traits. This did not eliminate the need to distinguish analogous effects from homologous structures or shared mechanisms. On the contrary, evolutionary explanation made the evidential basis of such comparisons more important. A plant and an animal might both respond to environmental changes while doing so through materially different processes.

Darwin's plant investigations also show how explanatory work can arise from an apparently peripheral subject. If an account of adaptation, responsiveness, or functional organisation is intended to apply broadly to living beings, it must accommodate organisms whose activity is not organised around rapid locomotion. Plant movements became an opportunity to test and revise explanatory expectations rather than merely an exception to an animal-derived rule.

Contemporary accounts sometimes read Darwin's experiments as precursors to theories of plant intelligence. Such retrospective interpretations require caution. Paolo Pecere's 2026 historical and epistemological discussion is valuable because it brings attention to the history of plant cognition claims and to the different standards by which such claims have been assessed. The existence of historical analogies does not establish the validity of a modern cognitive attribution. Nor should the language used in Darwin's descriptions be detached from the experimental and theoretical context in which it appeared.

The recovered PPP essay on Darwin provides a broad historical account of his botanical work; the PPP article on plant cognition preserves the later conceptual debate. Their relationship illustrates a useful editorial distinction. The first can help identify the original botanical investigations and their setting. The second can help reconstruct how later writers have interpreted those investigations. Neither replaces close reading of Darwin's own text or critical comparison with modern historiography.

Plants thus helped broaden evolutionary inquiry beyond conspicuous animal behaviour. Their movements, reproductive strategies, and environmental relationships provided evidence with which general explanations of adaptation and organismal activity could be examined. This was a contribution to the testing of biological ideas, not a historical guarantee of any one present-day theory of agency or cognition.

## Electrical responses and physiological continuity â€” The Bose case

Jagadish Chandra Bose's investigations provide a distinctive case in the history of attempts to compare plant and animal physiology. Working with experimental apparatus designed to register small and rapid changes, Bose studied responses to stimulation and sought to investigate the physical and physiological characteristics of plant activity. His programme belongs within a wider history of instrumentation, experimental physiology, and debates over the comparability of living systems.

Bose's **Plant Response as a Means of Physiological Investigation** (1906) and surviving Royal Society archival material document the existence and scope of this research. His work is historically important because it challenged investigators to consider whether responses associated with animals could be studied through comparable experimental procedures in plants. The question was not merely whether plants moved visibly, but whether changes in their state could be recorded and interpreted under controlled conditions.

The history must, however, distinguish experimental observations from Bose's broader interpretations and from later claims made on his behalf. The registration of electrical or mechanical responses is not equivalent to proof of consciousness. Neither is the observation of a similar signal in two organisms sufficient to establish that their underlying mechanisms or functional significance are identical. Instruments produce traces that require interpretation; their sensitivity does not remove the need for independent controls, replication, and competing explanations.

Bose's reception is consequently part of the historical case, not an incidental footnote. The Royal Society's archival records include Bose's unpublished 1903 manuscript (AP/76/1) and several contemporary referee reports. The catalogue description of John Scott Burdon-Sanderson's February 1904 report (RR/16/10) records methodological objections concerning the timing of observations and Bose's interpretation of the relationship between mechanical and electrical responses. Although the catalogue contains an introductory recommendation for publication, it also records Burdon-Sanderson's substantial reservations about the paper's suitability for *Philosophical Transactions*. These findings concern the archival descriptions; they should not be mistaken for a complete reassessment of Bose's experimental results. This establishes a specific contemporary criticism at the level of the archival description; the complete report and relevant experimental passages have not been examined here. A responsible account should neither dismiss his investigations because some interpretations were contested nor celebrate them as decisive evidence for plant mentality. His contribution is better framed as an attempt to expand the experimental comparison of physiological responses across organisms, with an evidential legacy that requires careful discrimination.

This case also exposes a general philosophical difficulty. If plants and animals show some analogous forms of responsiveness, what follows? At most, the observation establishes a phenomenon requiring explanation. Further conclusions about shared mechanisms, regulation, adaptive significance, cognition, or experience each demand additional evidence. Bose's work is historically revealing because it makes the movement from measurement to interpretation unusually visible.

## Contemporary consequences â€” Agency, cognition, and the boundaries of Life

Modern plant biology has made available detailed investigations of signalling, development, environmental responsiveness, physiological integration, and interaction with other organisms. These studies have stimulated renewed philosophical debate about how to describe plant activity. Some authors regard aspects of plant regulation as warranting terms such as agency or cognition; others argue that such terminology obscures important distinctions among responsiveness, control, learning, representation, and conscious experience. The historical record does not settle this debate, but it clarifies why the concepts are contested.

One recurring difficulty is the temptation to move too quickly between levels of description. A plant may alter its growth in response to environmental conditions. That observation may support a physiological explanation involving signal transduction, growth regulation, and material constraints. Whether the same phenomenon should be called â€˜decision-makingâ€™, â€˜cognitionâ€™, or â€˜intelligenceâ€™ is a further conceptual question. Whether it involves subjective experience is different again. The categories cannot be treated as interchangeable merely because each suggests activity rather than passivity.

The history traced here offers reasons to resist both exaggeration and dismissal. It would be mistaken to assume that plants are passive simply because their activity differs from animal movement. It would be equally mistaken to infer mental capacities from every adaptive-looking response. The evidential challenge is to identify what a proposed concept adds to an explanation and how that addition could be tested against alternatives. Historical controversies over plant sensibility and physiological continuity show that disputes about terminology are often also disputes about methods and standards of proof.

These issues are relevant to the Agencyâ€“Processâ€“Scale framework, but the relationship must remain bounded. In APS, Life is defined in terms of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation; biological Agency concerns viability-oriented organisational activity; and explanatory Scale concerns the extent and organisation of processes relevant to a particular question. These are contemporary conceptual commitments of APS, not propositions demonstrated by the historical cases examined in this article. The historical material can illuminate why investigators need to distinguish living organisation, its activities, and the explanatory scope of their descriptions. It cannot independently establish the correctness or distinctiveness of APS.

A further distinction concerns explanatory gain. Describing a plant as an agent may draw attention to regulatory activity, but the description earns scientific value only when it identifies something not adequately captured by an available alternative. A historical example of responsive growth is evidence of responsive growth; it is not automatically evidence that an agency-based explanation is superior to a mechanistic or physiological account. The appropriate comparison depends on the explanandum and on the causal detail each account supplies.

The study of plants therefore remains important to the philosophy of biology not because it requires every living system to be described in one vocabulary, but because it exposes the limits of vocabularies developed around a narrower range of organisms. Plant biology has repeatedly compelled investigators to reconsider what is being explained, which observations count as evidence, and how much follows from a proposed analogy. Those are continuing methodological contributions, whatever conclusion is ultimately reached about plant cognition or consciousness.

## Conclusion â€” What plant investigations changed

Plants did not enter the history of biological thought as inert objects that eventually became recognised as alive. Their growth, reproduction, environmental relationships, and practical significance were understood in many traditions long before the emergence of modern botany. What changed were the questions asked of those phenomena, the techniques used to investigate them, the conceptual distinctions brought to bear upon them, and the institutions through which particular accounts became authoritative.

In ancient natural philosophy, plants helped clarify which capacities could be treated as fundamental to living beings without requiring all the properties associated with animals. Botanical comparison made diversity, growth, and environmental variation objects of sustained inquiry. Early modern natural philosophy and anatomy multiplied competing explanations of vegetative powers and material processes. Experimental physiology transformed visible and sometimes barely perceptible responses into phenomena whose conditions and mechanisms could be investigated. Darwin's botanical work extended the comparative reach of evolutionary explanation, while Bose's investigations made the relation between measurement, physiological analogy, and interpretation a particularly sharp problem. Indigenous histories remind us that the production and circulation of botanical knowledge cannot be explained solely through European publications and institutions.

These developments do not add up to an inevitable march from an erroneous conception of passive plants towards a final theory of active life. They form a varied history of observation, practice, experiment, disagreement, and reinterpretation. Some investigations produced enduring results; others raised questions that were only partly answered or interpreted their evidence too strongly. The historical significance of plants lies partly in their capacity to reveal those limits. More precisely, Hales, Ingenhousz, and the Darwins exemplify distinct advances in measuring, manipulating, and locating the conditions of living activity; none independently establishes a universal account of Life.

The answer to the article's central question is therefore qualified but substantial. Plant investigations helped shape biological explanation by broadening the phenomena that theories of life had to accommodate and by sharpening the methods through which living activity could be investigated. They did not dictate a single definition of Life, nor do they validate a particular contemporary philosophy of biology. They made the explanatory demands of biology more exacting. That is their enduring place in the history of ideas.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How did investigations of plants contribute to changing scientific understandings of Life, and in what ways did they alter the questions, evidence, methods, and explanations employed in biological inquiry?

### Architectural Role

This is an **Extension** article within the **History of Ideas** cluster.

Its function is to examine the historical contributions of plant investigation to biological explanation across several intellectual traditions and investigative practices.

It does not define Life or biological Agency, establish a canonical APS concept, or claim that historical developments culminated in APS.

### Central Explanatory Claim

Plants contributed to the development of biological explanation not simply by supplying additional organisms for investigation, but by challenging assumptions about the characteristics of living beings and by making growth, nutrition, reproduction, physiological activity, movement, and environmental responsiveness subjects of increasingly systematic inquiry.

These contributions arose through different and sometimes competing traditions. They did not constitute a single cumulative progression towards a modern theory of Life.

### Explanatory Contribution

The article distinguishes several historically significant contributions:

1. **Conceptual:** Plant investigations helped clarify which characteristics could be considered fundamental to living beings without assuming that animal characteristics were universal.

2. **Comparative:** Botanical observations expanded the range of organisms and processes that biological explanations had to accommodate.

3. **Experimental:** Developments in plant physiology made previously difficult-to-investigate activities measurable, manipulable, and open to causal analysis.

4. **Evolutionary:** Botanical investigations contributed to broader explanations of adaptation, movement, development, and continuity between living forms.

5. **Historiographical:** Indigenous knowledge, colonial encounters, and changing scientific institutions reveal that the production and recognition of botanical knowledge cannot be understood through European experimental traditions alone.

6. **Methodological:** Disputes concerning plant responsiveness, cognition, and consciousness illustrate the continuing need to distinguish observation, analogy, interpretation, and warranted explanatory conclusions.

### Relationship to APS

APS characterises Life in terms of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and biological Agency in terms of viability-oriented organisational activity.

The historical investigations examined here provide context for understanding why questions about organisation, activity, regulation, and the boundaries of Life remain important.

However, the historical evidence does not independently establish these APS definitions. Nor does it demonstrate that APS explains the historical cases better than competing theoretical frameworks.

The relationship is therefore one of historical illumination and conceptual relevance, not retrospective theoretical confirmation.

### Explanatory Boundaries

The article does not attempt to provide a comprehensive global history of botany, a complete history of plant physiology, or a definitive account of plant cognition or consciousness.

Its historical cases are selective. Their significance depends on the particular questions, sources, practices, and interpretations examined.

Recognition of plant responsiveness does not, by itself, establish cognition, consciousness, or biological Agency in the APS sense.

Historical contributions must be assessed within their own intellectual and institutional circumstances rather than judged solely by their resemblance to contemporary concepts.

### Relationship to Other APS Articles

The article complements the History of Ideas treatments of Aristotle, Theophrastus, cell theory, and scientific technology.

It also provides historical context for APS articles addressing plant Agency, plant cognition, biological organisation, and the conceptual distinction between Life and Agency.

Those articles undertake conceptual or diagnostic tasks that the present historical account does not attempt to replace.

### Status of the Argument

The article advances a historically qualified interpretation of the contribution of plant investigations to biological explanation.

Its conclusions remain open to revision where further primary-source examination, international historiography, or comparative historical scholarship warrants a different interpretation.

Its architectural role is **Extension**, and its publication status remains **draft** pending final scholarly and technical verification.
