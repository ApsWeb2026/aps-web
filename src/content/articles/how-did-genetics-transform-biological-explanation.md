**Where This Article Fits**

*This History of Ideas article follows the experiments that made hereditary differences traceable from generations of offspring to chromosomes and cellular processes. It connects the series’ accounts of [cell theory](/articles/how-did-the-cell-become-the-unit-of-life/) and [evolutionary explanation](/articles/how-did-evolution-transform-biological-explanation/) through a question shared by both histories: how could researchers determine what was inherited and what it did?*

## Introduction

A pea plant raised from a round seed can produce wrinkled seeds. Two characteristics that usually travel together in a fruit-fly cross can occasionally be inherited separately. A strain of bread mould can grow when supplied with a nutrient that an ordinary strain can make for itself. All three observations can be investigated genetically, but each gives investigators grounds for a different inference. The pea crosses reveal patterns of transmission. The fruit-fly crosses help locate hereditary differences. The mould experiments connect an inherited difference to a biochemical process.

The history of genetics is often told as the discovery of the gene. Following what researchers could infer from their experiments gives a fuller account of the change. A hereditary difference could be followed through generations before its material basis was known. Its relative position on a chromosome could be inferred before its molecular structure could be identified. Its biochemical effects could be investigated through changes in growth. These achievements became connected through work in breeding, cytology, biochemistry, and molecular biology. The word *gene* carried different commitments as those connections developed. ([Rheinberger and Müller-Wille 2022](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/gene/))

## What crosses made visible

Breeders and hybridists had long investigated what offspring inherit. Mendel’s pea experiments made selected differences amenable to a particular kind of analysis. He began with varieties that bred consistently for contrasting characteristics, controlled the crosses, and counted the resulting forms across generations. For seed shape, he reported 5,474 round or somewhat rounded seeds and 1,850 wrinkled seeds from hybrid plants, close to three to one. ([Mendel 1866](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5068836/))

The ratio was only part of the evidence. When Mendel grew plants from round seeds and followed their offspring, some lines continued to produce round seeds, while others produced both round and wrinkled seeds. The same visible form could therefore conceal different hereditary constitutions. Following another generation made that distinction experimentally accessible. Mendel could account for the pattern by proposing that the alternatives brought together in a hybrid separated in the formation of reproductive cells. The evidence concerned how the alternatives were transmitted. Identifying their physical basis required other kinds of evidence. ([Mendel 1866](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5068836/))

The later formation of Mendelian genetics gave these methods and results new uses. Mendel’s work belonged to the study of hybrids, while early twentieth-century geneticists made the transmission of hereditary differences a central research problem. The continuity lies in the experimental leverage of crosses and counts. The historical change lies partly in the questions researchers brought to them. ([Rheinberger and Müller-Wille 2022](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/gene/))

## How patterns of inheritance became maps

Once researchers could follow several hereditary differences through the same crosses, they could ask how often those differences travelled together. In fruit flies, Sturtevant used the frequencies with which sex-linked factors were separated in inheritance to infer their relative positions. If the factors lay in linear order, the separation frequencies for different pairs could be compared to work out which factor lay between the others. The 1913 paper used this reasoning to arrange six factors along a chromosome. ([Sturtevant 1913](https://doi.org/10.1002/jez.1400140104))

The map was an inference from breeding results. It represented relative order and distances estimated from recombination, which are distinct from physical distances measured along DNA. The explanatory achievement was to show how an otherwise puzzling pattern of association among inherited traits could yield evidence about chromosome organisation. A map also gave researchers a way to anticipate patterns in further crosses. ([Sturtevant 1913](https://doi.org/10.1002/jez.1400140104))

A study in maize by Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock added a different line of evidence in 1931. Using chromosomes with distinguishable physical features, they showed that recombination of genetic markers was accompanied by the exchange of visible chromosome features. The fly crosses and the maize study illustrate how breeding evidence and cytological observation could bear on the same explanation. The first inferred relative position from transmission. The second connected genetic crossing-over to a physical exchange. ([Creighton and McClintock 1931](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.17.8.492))

## From tracking a difference to explaining its effects

Knowing that a difference is inherited and locating it still leaves open how it affects the organism. Beadle and Tatum addressed that question through the nutritional requirements of the fungus *Neurospora*. Their 1941 study examined mutants that could grow when supplied with particular nutrients but failed to grow on a minimal medium. Crosses let them investigate whether a requirement segregated as a hereditary difference. Supplying a nutrient could restore growth and help identify the biochemical process affected. ([Beadle and Tatum 1941](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.27.11.499); [Beadle 1958](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/beadle-lecture.pdf))

Growth made the link between inheritance and biochemical activity experimentally accessible. If a strain grew after a supplement was supplied, the result helped narrow where to look for a defect. Establishing the precise reaction and the role of a particular product called for further biochemical evidence. The method was powerful because it allowed researchers to move between a hereditary difference, a change in the conditions of growth, and a hypothesis about a specific part of metabolism.

Later research widened the question of gene action. Jacob and Monod’s account of regulation distinguished genes involved in producing proteins from regulatory elements and products that affect when and how much protein is made. Work by Berget, Moore, and Sharp showed that a mature adenovirus messenger RNA contained segments corresponding to separated parts of the genome. The latter example concerns RNA processing in infected cells and illustrates why a mature transcript need not correspond to a single uninterrupted DNA segment. These studies made the organisation of expression itself an object of explanation. ([Jacob and Monod 1961](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-2836(61)80072-7); [Berget, Moore, and Sharp 1977](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.74.8.3171))

## How the explanations fit together

Transmission, location, and action pose different questions about the same hereditary difference. A cross may give a good prediction of which offspring will show a trait. A map can tell investigators where to look for a relevant difference. An account of gene action must explain how that difference makes a difference under specified cellular, developmental, or environmental conditions. The three explanations can support one another because they establish different relationships.

This history also clarifies where APS enters the discussion. APS asks how genetic activity participates in living organisation. The mould case makes that question concrete. The inherited requirement affects growth under one nutritional condition, while supplying the missing nutrient allows growth under another. The explanation brings the hereditary difference and the cell’s biochemical activity into relation with the conditions under which the organism functions. The experiment already supplies that connection. An APS interpretation can make its organisational significance explicit, and any claim of additional explanatory content would need to be assessed against the biochemical and genetic explanation of the same result. See [Information in Biology — An APS Clarification](/articles/information-in-biology-an-aps-clarification/).

## Conclusion

Genetics changed what biologists could ask of evidence about inheritance. Crosses made it possible to distinguish visible resemblance from hereditary constitution. Mapping turned departures from independent inheritance into evidence about chromosome organisation. Biochemical and molecular studies opened the processes through which inherited differences affect organisms to experimental inquiry. The power of genetics lies partly in how these inquiries became connected and partly in the precision with which each could answer its own question.
