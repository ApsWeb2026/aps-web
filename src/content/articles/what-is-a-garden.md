**Where This Article Fits**

*This History of Ideas article uses a familiar question to explore how philosophical inquiry proceeds. Drawing on garden history, horticulture, and philosophy, it follows an everyday concept through ordinary examples, difficult cases, and attempts at definition. It also asks how a garden's history and the practice of gardening shape what people see in it.*

## Introduction

The path is still there, although you have to know where to look. A shrub has spread across it, grass has crept into the beds, and a chipped gnome is almost hidden under the leaves. Nobody has tended this suburban garden for years. How much longer will it be a garden?

There is a small trap in the question. In describing the place, I have already called it a garden. I could just as easily have said that it *used to be* one. The two descriptions draw attention to different things. One invites us to see what has persisted through years of neglect. The other asks what has been lost. A visitor might see a patch of scrub where someone who remembers the place can still make out the old beds.

How do we ordinarily use the word *garden*? What would justify drawing a firmer boundary when a case is difficult? And what makes a garden significant to the people who make, tend, or experience it? Those questions travel together for some of the way. The pleasure of the inquiry lies in finding out where they lead. We may end up with a better definition, but the journey matters more than the sentence we arrive at. A good journey will change what we notice when we come back to this same patch of ground.

## A Familiar Garden

Let’s roll up our sleeves and begin with an ordinary example. Behind a house there is a patch of ground with a path, a few fruit trees, and a vegetable bed. Someone planted the trees, still waters the vegetables, and has decided where the path should go. A fence runs around the property. If we were explaining the word to someone hearing it for the first time, this would be a good place to begin.

Several features of the example offer themselves as parts of a definition. A garden seems to be an area set apart for growing plants, often close to a house and looked after by someone. This is broadly the picture given by the horticultural reference work *Hortus Third*, which describes the garden in its historical sense as a relatively small plant-growing area, commonly enclosed and associated with a residence (Liberty Hyde Bailey Hortorium 1976, p. 494). Enclosure has a long association with the word and with garden-making. Edward Hyams’s history (1971) places familiar domestic examples in a much wider history of gardens and gardening. That wider view gives us good reason to ask which features of our example belong to a particular tradition.

Take the house away and leave the rest. A vegetable allotment at the other end of the suburb is still readily understood as a garden. A botanic garden may be larger than the suburb’s public park. The size of a domestic plot and its proximity to a home help us picture a familiar garden. They have little force as requirements for gardens generally.

The fence looks more promising. We can walk around a garden, enter it, and leave it. Yet a landscape garden may use a sunken barrier, or ha-ha, to keep grazing animals out while allowing the eye to travel uninterrupted into the surrounding country. There is a physical boundary here, even though the view gives little sense of enclosure. The example makes us ask which kind of boundary we had in mind. A fence, a legal boundary, the extent of planting and care, and the limits of a designed view can all fall in different places. In the domestic example they were easy to run together.

## What Can a Difficult Case Tell Us?

Living plants seem a stronger candidate. Trees, flowers, and vegetables differ in obvious ways, yet growing plants is central to what most of us picture when we think of gardening. Consider, though, a dry garden made with stones and raked gravel. Suppose that the composition contains no living plants and belongs to a tradition in which it is understood as a garden. If we accept it as a garden in the relevant sense, then the presence of plants cannot be a universal requirement. The case still leaves plenty of room for plants to be central to the history and ordinary practice of gardening. We learn something about the reach of the proposed requirement and something about the strength of the familiar association.

Now turn the test around. Imagine a field of cabbages, sown and tended by people. There are cultivated plants, regular care, and a well-defined extent. In many contexts we would simply call it a field. Yet a nearby enterprise growing vegetables for sale might quite naturally be called a market garden. It would be tempting to settle the issue by saying that a garden is smaller, more varied, or more intimate than a field. Each suggestion deserves a test. What would we say of a large commercial plot with a single crop, or a small, diverse field that nobody locally calls a garden? The established expression *market garden* is evidence about usage, but the expression alone gives us little basis for deciding which features make the enterprise a garden in other contexts.

Names raise a further difficulty. A tiny garden scene made of artificial materials in a matchbox can represent a garden with remarkable fidelity. Calling it a miniature garden may be an entirely natural thing to do. The small scene nevertheless gives us a reason to ask whether the word applies in the same way to a representation and to a place people can make, tend, and inhabit. Before letting a surprising example decide an argument, we need to know what it is an example of.

We can now make a more discriminating comparison. A deliberately sown meadow may be cut once or twice a year, with plants allowed to seed and spread in between. A neglected shrubbery may receive about as much attention from people in a given year. Yet the restraint in the meadow can be part of the gardening. The gardener chooses when to cut and what to leave, and some results are left to the plants, the soil, and the season. In the shrubbery, the lapse of care may mark the end of a practice whose effects are still visible.

Hugh Johnson (1979) puts human control and continued care at the centre of gardening. That proposal has real force. It draws attention to the work by which a garden comes into being and to the ease with which its character can change when the work ceases. The meadow helps us refine the idea. Care can take the form of selective intervention, including a deliberate decision to wait. The neglected shrubbery presses a different question. Even when care has ended, its history may still help us understand why the place is the way it is and why we continue to call it a garden.

## What Are We Asking a Definition to Do?

We have been doing several things under the heading of definition. We have asked how people use a word, what connects the cases they recognise, and what makes a particular place the kind of place it is. These inquiries can inform one another. When someone calls the shrubbery a former garden, that is evidence of how one speaker uses the expression. The judgement may also direct our attention to a feature of the place, such as the disappearance of the beds. To find out whether that use is widespread, we would need evidence from other speakers or records of usage. To find out what happened to the beds, we might need to look at the site or consult its history.

The purpose of a definition affects what counts as success (Gupta 2024). A dictionary entry gives readers enough information to understand and use a word. A philosophical definition that aims to describe existing usage takes on a further burden. It needs to make sense of the relevant cases and of the distinctions speakers draw among them. A stipulation assigns a meaning for an inquiry. We might stipulate that, for a particular survey, a garden is any site meeting a stated set of criteria. Whether those criteria are wise depends on the survey’s purpose.

Being stipulative and offering necessary and sufficient conditions are independent matters. The first concerns how a meaning is assigned. The second concerns what the proposed conditions are supposed to establish. If cultivated plants are necessary, every garden must contain them. If cultivation within a defined area is sufficient, every place with those features qualifies. A descriptive proposal can make either claim and answer to how the word is used. Our dry garden and cabbage field show the two ways such a proposal can come under pressure. A case on which judgements divide invites a different response. We may need to establish the facts, examine differences in usage, or accept that the application of the word in that case is unsettled.

One possibility is that gardens are connected by overlapping similarities. Wittgenstein’s discussion of games offers a model (Wittgenstein 2009, §§66–71). The family resemblance among gardens might involve cultivation, deliberate arrangement, care, enclosure, or a history of human use, with different features carrying weight in different cases. The proposal is worth exploring because it explains how a dry garden and a vegetable garden can both be intelligible instances. It still owes us an account of which similarities matter. A farm, a park, and a restored patch of bush can share several of the same features. Simply counting resemblances would give little guidance about why some of them count for more than others.

A further possibility is to sharpen the concept for a task. Suppose we were establishing a heritage register and needed to decide whether former gardens with surviving layouts were eligible. A rule that gave weight to documented design and surviving features could make those decisions more consistent. Its suitability would depend on what the register was meant to preserve and on what evidence applicants could reasonably supply. Carnap called this kind of deliberate refinement *explication*. The revised concept should be precise enough to do its work, useful for the stated purpose, and sufficiently close to the familiar concept for the refinement to make sense. Contemporary work on conceptual engineering asks related questions about when we have reason to revise the concepts we use (Gupta 2024; Cappelen and Plunkett 2020).

Our question can therefore receive several legitimate kinds of answer. A register needs criteria that people can apply consistently. An essay on gardens can make progress by showing which features explain familiar cases, why other cases are difficult, and where greater precision would help. The dry garden and the old shrubbery have earned their place in the inquiry because they show what we might lose by insisting on a sharp boundary before we know what it is for.

## How We Read a Garden

Suppose we find three records of our overgrown suburban garden. A plan shows a straight path from the kitchen door to the vegetable beds. A photograph shows chairs beneath the fruit tree. Someone who grew up there remembers summer evenings spent picking beans and carrying water from the back tap. Was this principally a productive garden, or had it become a place for sitting outdoors? The answer may depend on when we look, whose experience we consider, and what each record allows us to see.

The six volumes of *A Cultural History of Gardens* follow the same lines of enquiry across different periods (Leslie and Hunt 2013). Design asks how the place was laid out. Garden type asks which traditions or purposes help us understand it. Planting asks what was grown, where, and how it changed. Use and reception ask what people did there and how they responded to the place. Meaning asks what it came to signify. Verbal and visual representations help us investigate those questions, and they show how people chose to describe or depict the garden. The larger landscape brings into view relationships that extend beyond the plot.

Let’s see what those questions do for our three records. The plan makes the garden look like a place organised around growing food, but we would have to trace the old path and look for the edges of the beds to see how closely the garden followed the plan. The photograph records a moment of leisure. Its framing leaves the vegetable beds out of view, so it tells us little about how much of the plot was still given over to food. The recollection brings the work of growing food back into the picture. Someone else in the household might remember those same evenings chiefly for the company beneath the tree. The garden could have served both purposes at once, or its balance of uses could have changed as the tree grew and the beds received less attention.

The neighbouring properties might help explain that change. Suppose a tree next door eventually shaded the vegetable beds. What looks on the plan like a decision about where to grow food may have become, years later, a question of where there was still enough sun. The ha-ha made this relationship with the wider landscape easy to see in a grand garden. In a suburban plot, the influence may be as ordinary as shade falling across a fence.

These questions give gardeners and garden historians an explanatory grammar. They help us decide what to look for, what to ask, and what we can infer from the answers. They also sharpen our opening question. The path can survive after the beds disappear, and someone can remember the garden as a place of work long after the photograph becomes the family’s favourite image of it. To ask whether the garden has survived is now to ask which parts of its design, planting, use, and significance have endured, and how we know.

## What Gardens Come to Mean

Think again of the chairs beneath the fruit tree. Whoever planted the tree may have wanted fruit, and its shade may later have made it a place to sit. The photograph shows where someone put the chairs on one occasion. To understand what that corner of the garden came to mean, we would want to know who sat there, whether they came back, and what they remembered of it afterwards.

David E. Cooper’s *A Philosophy of Gardens* (2006) takes the significance of gardens as a philosophical question in its own right. The distinction between use and meaning gives us a way into that question. A path in a memorial garden may be designed to lead visitors to a place of remembrance. A path through a vegetable garden may be laid out for harvesting and become, over years of use, the route someone takes whenever they want a moment to themselves. In one case, the purpose helps explain the design. In the other, a meaning emerges from a use the designer may never have imagined.

The essays collected by Dan O’Brien (2010) also invite us to consider what it is like to be the person doing the gardening. Think again of the sown meadow. A visitor might see a pleasing arrangement of grasses and flowers. The gardener who comes back week after week may recognise which plants have spread, what the last cut encouraged, and where waiting another fortnight would make a difference. The same planting is understood through different kinds of attention. Over time, the practice of tending it can become part of what makes the place significant.

## Back to the Old Path

At first, the height of the grass seemed to tell us how far the place had gone towards becoming a former garden. Now we can ask more of what we see. If the old path follows the route shown on the plan, we have some evidence that the design was carried out. If the vegetable beds have disappeared, the recollection of picking beans helps us recover a use the photograph leaves out. And if a neighbouring tree now shades the old beds, the garden’s surroundings may help explain why that use declined. Each clue tells us something different about what has endured and what has changed.

In familiar cases, a garden is a place deliberately shaped or tended, usually through the cultivation or arrangement of plants. Our examples give that account some room to breathe. A dry garden shows what deliberate arrangement can achieve with stones and gravel. A meadow garden shows that care can include restraint. A cultivated field makes us ask what other features, alongside cultivation, help us recognise a place as a garden.

So is the shrubbery still a garden? The old planting may survive after regular care has ceased. The design may be legible to someone who knows where to look. A person who grew up there may still speak of *the garden* after a visitor has come to see it as scrub. These judgements draw on different aspects of the same place, and there may be a long period in which both *overgrown garden* and *former garden* are defensible.

The gnome is still there. We began by asking how much neglect a garden could withstand. We can now ask which parts of this garden have endured, how we know, and why they matter to the people who remember or use it. That is what made the journey worth taking.
