## Introduction

Every organism dies.

No matter how successful, resilient, or well adapted an individual may be, its continuity is ultimately temporary. Cells age. Bodies deteriorate. Organisms perish. If biological persistence depended solely upon the survival of individuals, life would repeatedly disappear with the organisms in which it is realised.

Yet life persists.

It persists not merely for years or generations but across immense spans of evolutionary time. Lineages continue. Biological organisation is repeatedly reconstituted. New forms emerge while others disappear. The living world therefore displays a distinctive combination of historical continuity and transformation: individual organisms are temporary, yet organised life remains connected across generations.

This observation defines the central problem addressed by evolutionary explanation.

How can organised biological continuity extend beyond the lifetimes of the individuals through which it is realised, while remaining capable of historical transformation?

APS approaches evolution through this problem. Evolution is understood as the historical transformation of organised persistence across generations. This does not replace the mechanisms and processes studied by evolutionary biology. Rather, it identifies the broader explanandum within which questions about inheritance, variation, adaptation, fitness, natural selection, and evolutionary novelty can be distinguished and related.

This article has a different role from *Evolution as the Historical Transformation of Organised Persistence*. That anchor article establishes the APS conception of evolution itself. The present article is the principal synthesis article of the Evolutionary Dynamics cluster. Its task is to clarify how different evolutionary questions bear upon the common problem of historical continuity through change.

That task requires an important methodological control. The order in which inheritance, variation, adaptation, fitness, natural selection, and evolutionary transformation are discussed here is explanatory rather than chronological. It does not by itself establish that these phenomena form a universal causal pathway, that each is a necessary intermediary for the next, or that every evolutionary explanandum requires all of them. Their biological relations must be specified and warranted for the explanatory target at issue.

The evolutionary organisation of life should therefore be understood as an explanatory architecture, not as a sequence imposed upon evolutionary history.

## Evolution as Historical Transformation of Organised Persistence

Evolution is often described as change through time. This is correct as far as it goes, but change alone does not identify what is distinctively biological about evolution. Mountains change through time. Rivers alter their courses. Climates fluctuate. Biological evolution concerns living systems whose organisation is not merely altered but historically continued, reproduced, differentiated, and transformed across generations.

The central APS explanandum is therefore continuity through historical change.

This formulation places organised persistence at the centre of the evolutionary question without treating persistence itself as an evolutionary mechanism. Organisms sustain viability during their own lifetimes through organised activity. Evolutionary explanation addresses a different temporal problem: how forms of biological organisation become historically continuous and transform across generations.

Historical continuity does not mean that organisation remains unchanged. On the contrary, evolutionary history is possible because continuity and transformation coexist. Lineages remain historically connected while their organisation changes. Novel structures, behaviours, developmental relations, ecological interactions, and other biological differences can arise within histories that remain continuous enough for those differences themselves to acquire evolutionary histories.

Evolution is therefore neither persistence without change nor change without persistence. It concerns the historical transformation of organised persistence.

This formulation also prevents the evolutionary synthesis from being reduced to any single familiar process. Inheritance, variation, adaptation, fitness, and natural selection answer different explanatory questions. Their relations can be biologically important without being identical, interchangeable, or components of one universal causal sequence.

The task of an APS evolutionary synthesis is consequently to distinguish these explanatory roles before asking how they are related in particular evolutionary cases.

## Historical Continuity Across Generations

The first question concerns continuity itself.

How can biological organisation remain historically connected when individual organisms disappear?

Inheritance is central to answering this question. In APS, inheritance concerns the reliable reconstitution of viable organisation across generations. What persists is not an unchanged material object passing intact from one generation to another. Biological continuity is repeatedly regenerated through processes by which subsequent organisms acquire and realise organisation connected to the histories from which they arise.

This makes inheritance important to evolutionary explanation because it provides a relation between successive generations. Developmental capacities, physiological organisation, behavioural dispositions, and other features of organisms do not arise independently of biological history. They are realised within lineages in which aspects of organisation are repeatedly reconstituted.

Inheritance should not, however, be made to carry more explanatory weight than this establishes. To identify cross-generational continuity is not yet to demonstrate that every evolutionary process depends upon inheritance in the same way, or that inheritance is the first member of a universal causal sequence leading to evolutionary transformation. Such claims require specification of the biological relation and the explanandum for which it matters.

Explaining evolutionary transformation therefore includes the question of how historical connection across generations is established and maintained. Inheritance supplies one of the central explanatory resources for understanding how biological organisation is reconstituted across generations rather than beginning anew with each organism.

Historical continuity thereby becomes available for transformation.

## Difference, Variation, and Organisational Possibility

Continuity alone does not explain evolutionary change. Evolutionary histories also contain differences.

Variation concerns the differences that arise within continuing biological histories. Organisms and lineages do not reproduce organisation with perfect identity. Differences occur in structures, developmental trajectories, physiological capacities, behaviour, ecological relations, and other features of living organisation.

From an APS perspective, variation is therefore not the opposite of continuity. Difference arises within continuity. A lineage can remain historically connected while the organisation realised within it changes.

This distinction is important because it prevents evolutionary novelty from being treated as though it must interrupt persistence. New biological possibilities can arise through changes within already continuing organisation. Some differences may be transient. Others may become historically consequential. Still others may participate in transformations that alter the future organisation of lineages.

Variation identifies the presence and generation of such differences; it does not by itself specify their historical consequences.

For the same reason, novelty and innovation should not be treated as a separate mandatory stage through which evolution must pass. They describe forms or degrees of difference that may become important in particular evolutionary histories. Novel developmental arrangements, ecological relations, behaviours, physiological capacities, or other organisational configurations can enlarge the possibilities available to living systems. Whether and how such possibilities become historically consequential is a further explanatory question.

The evolutionary significance of variation therefore lies in opening possibilities for historical difference without predetermining what happens to those differences.

## Adaptation and Viability Under Changing Conditions

A different question concerns the relation between biological organisation and changing conditions.

Living systems persist only while their organisation remains compatible with viability. Conditions change within organisms, across environments, through development, and over longer historical periods. Biological organisation may consequently have to change if persistence is to continue.

APS uses adaptation, in its organisational sense, for continuity-preserving reorganisation under changing conditions. The concept therefore begins with a present-tense biological problem: how can an organised living system remain viable when the circumstances relevant to its persistence change?

This question should be distinguished from the separate question of how adaptive organisation acquires evolutionary consequences.

Adaptation can matter to evolutionary explanation. Forms of organisation that sustain viability under particular conditions can participate in historical trajectories, and changes in adaptive relations can affect the possibilities available to subsequent generations. But the architectural placement of adaptation within an APS synthesis does not establish that adaptation is produced by a preceding sequence of inheritance, variation, and selection, nor that adaptation is a necessary intermediary between variation and later evolutionary transformation.

Present viability-oriented reorganisation, historical retention, and evolutionary consequence are different explanatory relations. In particular cases they may be connected, but those connections require biological warrant.

This distinction allows adaptation to retain its importance without turning it into a universal stage of evolutionary change. It also clarifies why adaptation cannot be equated with natural selection. Adaptation concerns the relation between organisation, changing conditions, and continued viability. Natural selection concerns a different explanatory problem: differential historical stabilisation.

## Fitness and Differential Historical Continuity

Evolutionary histories contain not only continuity and difference but differences in continuity.

Some lineages, organisational configurations, or variants persist and proliferate differently from others. Fitness concerns this differential historical continuity.

This formulation distinguishes fitness from immediate viability. Viability concerns whether organised living systems can continue to exist under relevant conditions. Fitness concerns historical differences in continuity among biological variants, lineages, or forms of organisation across generations.

Fitness can therefore characterise an evolutionary relation without being treated as an independent force that produces the very differences it describes. To say that one variant has greater fitness than another is to identify a difference relevant to historical continuity; it does not by itself specify all the causal processes responsible for that difference.

This distinction matters because evolutionary explanation often moves too quickly between describing differential outcomes and explaining their production. APS keeps these tasks separate. Fitness can identify or characterise differential continuity while the biological causes of that differential continuity remain matters for further explanation.

The distinction also prevents fitness from collapsing into natural selection. Differential historical continuity is relevant to selection, but the concepts perform different explanatory work. Fitness concerns differences in historical continuity. Natural selection concerns the differential historical stabilisation associated with such differences under specified biological conditions.

Whether fitness in a particular analysis is purely descriptive, predictive, dispositional, causal, or related to some other explanatory role cannot be settled merely from its position within APS architecture. Those questions require the appropriate biological and theoretical warrant.

## Natural Selection and Differential Historical Stabilisation

Natural selection occupies an important but bounded position within evolutionary explanation.

APS characterises natural selection as differential historical stabilisation. Differences among biological variants can be associated with differences in their contribution to subsequent historical continuity. Across generations, some forms of organisation consequently become more historically established while others become less prominent or disappear.

This does not make selection the whole of evolution.

Selection does not, merely by definition, explain how biological organisation is inherited, how differences arise, how organisms remain viable, how developmental possibilities are generated, or how ecological conditions are constituted. These may be relevant to particular cases of selection, but their relevance and relation must be specified rather than inferred from a general evolutionary architecture.

Likewise, the existence of selection does not require us to treat the full APS vocabulary of inheritance, variation, adaptation, fitness, development, ecology, and organised persistence as a list of universal biological prerequisites. The minimal conditions required for a particular selection process and the broader processes needed for a satisfactory explanation of that case are distinct questions.

Selection is therefore best understood within this synthesis as answering a specific historical question: how do differences become differentially stabilised across generations?

That question can be connected to inheritance, variation, fitness, adaptation, development, ecology, and other biological processes. But those connections are explanatory relations to be established in particular contexts, not consequences of placing the concepts beside one another within APS.

## From Distinct Relations to Evolutionary Transformation

The preceding sections have been presented in an order, but that order must not be mistaken for an evolutionary pathway.

Inheritance addresses cross-generational continuity. Variation addresses difference within continuing histories. Adaptation addresses continuity-preserving reorganisation under changing conditions. Fitness addresses differential historical continuity. Natural selection addresses differential historical stabilisation. Each identifies a different explanatory problem.

Evolutionary transformation is the broader historical explanandum to which these questions may contribute.

This means that APS does not require evolution to proceed through a fixed sequence in which inheritance produces variation, variation produces adaptation, adaptation determines fitness, fitness produces selection, and selection finally produces evolutionary transformation. Such a sequence would combine distinct kinds of relation—conceptual, causal, temporal, historical, and explanatory—without demonstrating that they coincide.

Nor does rejecting that fixed sequence imply that the processes are independent. Biological cases can involve strong causal and dependency relations among them. Inheritance can matter to the persistence of differences. Variation can matter to differential outcomes. Adaptive organisation can affect historical continuity. Selection can alter the distribution of variants through time. The point is not to deny these relations but to identify them accurately.

The APS synthesis therefore operates at a different level. It asks which explanatory question each concept answers, what relation is being proposed between concepts, what biological evidence warrants that relation, and whether the relation is required for the explanandum under investigation.

Evolutionary organisation emerges as an explanatory architecture in this sense: a structured way of relating different questions about historical continuity and transformation without treating their arrangement as evidence for a universal mechanism or causal chain.

## Development, Ecology, Cognition, and Social Organisation

Evolutionary explanations frequently extend beyond the concepts discussed so far. Developmental processes, ecological relations, cognition and behaviour, and forms of social organisation can all matter to evolutionary history.

Their relevance, however, is target-dependent.

Development matters when the production, expression, modification, or inheritance of biological organisation depends upon developmental processes relevant to the evolutionary explanandum. Developmental organisation can constrain or enable evolutionary possibilities relevant to particular explananda, but this does not require every evolutionary explanation to expand into a complete account of development.

Ecology matters when organism–environment relations, resources, competitors, symbiotic interactions, climatic conditions, or other ecological circumstances materially affect the historical process being explained. Ecological conditions can alter viability and differential continuity, while evolving organisms can in turn modify ecological relations. The explanatory extent required depends upon the case.

Cognitive and behavioural organisation can likewise become evolutionarily relevant when the activities of organisms alter survival, reproduction, ecological interaction, social transmission, or other relations bearing materially upon historical continuity. Where cognitive capacities are present, their forms can themselves have evolutionary histories, but this does not make cognition a universal component of evolutionary explanation.

Social organisation can introduce additional forms of historical continuity, including behavioural and cultural transmission. Where these processes affect the evolutionary explanandum, they belong within the explanation. Where they do not, their existence elsewhere in the organisation of life does not require their inclusion.

The same principle governs all four interfaces. APS does not expand evolutionary explanation merely because biological domains are interconnected. Additional explanatory extent is warranted when materially realised relations make a difference to the specified evolutionary problem.

Evolution can therefore interact with development, ecology, cognition, and social organisation without these domains becoming obligatory stages or components of one universal evolutionary architecture.

## Evolutionary Organisation Is an Architecture, Not a Mechanism

The evolutionary domain contains many mechanisms and processes, but evolutionary organisation is not itself another mechanism alongside them.

Its function within APS is architectural. It identifies the explanatory relations required to understand how organised biological continuity can extend across generations while undergoing historical transformation.

This distinction is important. Calling evolutionary organisation an architecture does not mean that inheritance, variation, adaptation, fitness, natural selection, development, ecology, cognition, and social organisation together form a single integrated causal system in every evolutionary case. Nor does it establish that all of these must be represented whenever evolution is explained.

Architecture concerns the organisation of explanatory questions and relations. Mechanistic and causal claims concern what materially produces, constrains, enables, or changes a particular biological outcome. The first can guide inquiry into the second, but it cannot substitute for it.

The APS evolutionary architecture therefore asks a series of distinguishable questions. What maintains historical connection across generations? What differences arise within that continuity? How does organisation remain viable as relevant conditions change? Where does historical continuity differ? What becomes differentially stabilised? Which additional developmental, ecological, behavioural, cognitive, or social relations materially affect the case? And how do the warranted answers bear upon the historical transformation being explained?

Different evolutionary explananda may require different subsets of these questions and different relations among their answers.

The unity of the evolutionary domain consequently lies not in a single mechanism or compulsory process sequence but in a common explanatory problem: the historical transformation of organised persistence.

## The Evolutionary Organisation of Life

Individual organisms are temporary.

Life is historically continuous.

The contrast identifies the problem with which this article began, but it should not be converted into the claim that one universal architecture is biologically necessary for all evolutionary change. Historical continuity is realised through particular biological processes whose relations vary with the systems and explananda under investigation.

APS uses evolutionary organisation to make those explanatory relations explicit.

Inheritance helps explain how organisation is reconstituted across generations. Variation identifies differences arising within continuing histories. Adaptation concerns continuity-preserving reorganisation under changing conditions. Fitness concerns differential historical continuity. Natural selection concerns differential historical stabilisation. Novelty and innovation concern the emergence of new biological possibilities. Developmental, ecological, cognitive, behavioural, and social relations enter evolutionary explanations where they materially affect the historical process at issue.

These concepts do not become more explanatory merely by being assembled into a sequence. Their explanatory contribution depends upon the question being asked and upon the biological relations that can actually be warranted.

What unifies them within this synthesis is therefore the explanandum rather than a predetermined causal order.

Evolution concerns the historical transformation of organised persistence. It asks how living organisation remains connected across generations while becoming different from what preceded it. Evolutionary history is the history of that continuity and transformation.

This is why APS treats evolutionary organisation as a major domain of biological explanation. The evolutionary problem begins where individual persistence ends: with the historical continuation and transformation of organised life beyond the lifespan of any particular organism.

## Conclusion

Every organism dies, yet life continues.

Evolutionary explanation addresses how biological organisation remains historically connected across generations while undergoing transformation.

APS characterises evolution as the historical transformation of organised persistence. Within that explanandum, inheritance, variation, adaptation, fitness, and natural selection perform distinguishable explanatory roles. Inheritance concerns cross-generational reconstitution. Variation concerns difference within continuing histories. Adaptation concerns continuity-preserving reorganisation under changing conditions. Fitness concerns differential historical continuity. Natural selection concerns differential historical stabilisation.

These distinctions provide an explanatory architecture, not a universal biological sequence.

The order in which the concepts are presented does not establish temporal succession, causal priority, necessary dependency, or explanatory superiority. Nor does their inclusion in one synthesis establish that every evolutionary explanation requires every concept. Those relations must be determined for the biological explanandum under investigation.

Development, ecology, cognition, behaviour, and social organisation can extend evolutionary explanation when their materially realised relations make a difference to the target being explained. Their inclusion is therefore explanatory and evidential rather than automatic.

The evolutionary organisation of life is consequently neither a single mechanism nor a predetermined pathway from continuity to transformation. It is the organisation of distinct explanatory questions around a common historical problem.

Individual organisms are temporary, but organised life has a history.

Evolution is the historical transformation of that organised persistence.
