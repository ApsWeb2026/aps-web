## Introduction - How Do We Know Something Is Alive?

Most living things are easy to recognise. Dogs, trees, birds, insects, and bacteria are routinely identified as living systems without difficulty. Yet biology encounters harder cases. Are viruses alive? Are dormant seeds alive? What about bacterial spores, artificial systems, or hypothetical forms of life unlike those currently known on Earth?

Such questions reveal that recognising life is not always straightforward. Even with a proposed definition, scientists must determine how it applies to particular systems and what observations would justify the conclusion. A definition does not automatically provide a reliable diagnostic procedure.

Discussions of life frequently concentrate on metabolism, reproduction, evolution, information, or other candidate defining characteristics. Each may matter biologically, but scientific practice requires a further distinction: **what Life is**, **how a particular system is assessed**, and **what evidence supports that assessment**.

APS separates these tasks while recognising their dependence on one another. Diagnosis must be guided by a definition, and a diagnostic judgement must be supported by evidence. Neither the definition nor the evidence should be confused with the judgement itself.

## Why Defining Life Is Not Enough

A definition specifies the kind of organisation under investigation. Diagnosis asks whether a particular case satisfies that definition. Evidence consists of the observations, comparisons, and interventions that support or undermine the diagnosis.

The distinction is familiar elsewhere in science. Defining a disease is not the same as diagnosing it in a patient: the definition identifies the target, while diagnosis requires interpretation of findings. Similarly, identifying the organisational conditions proposed to distinguish life does not by itself settle whether a virus, dormant seed, or laboratory-produced system meets those conditions.

A definition may also be scientifically useful without settling every borderline case. Cleland and Chyba (2002), for example, questioned the assumption that a satisfactory definition of life could simply precede and determine empirical investigation. APS takes the relation between conceptual characterisation and investigation seriously: a proposed definition guides inquiry, but the evidence can expose ambiguities or weaknesses in its application.

## What Life Is in APS

APS defines **Life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

*Viability* concerns the conditions under which a system's organisation can continue, including changes that support, undermine, or restore those conditions. *Constraint closure* concerns the reciprocal dependence of organisational constraints: constraints help regulate processes through which the constraints themselves are produced, maintained, or re-established. The definition therefore concerns a materially realised form of organisation, not the mere presence of complexity, activity, or endurance.

**Biological agency** identifies the viability-oriented organisational activity of living systems. Regulation, boundary maintenance, repair, and reorganisation can be expressions of that activity. Agency is not an additional causal force standing above biological mechanisms, nor does its identification by itself provide a complete definition of Life (Spencer 2026).

**Organised persistence** identifies a related but different phenomenon: the continuity of organisation through material, environmental, and developmental change. It is something biological explanations seek to account for. It is not a substitute for the definition of Life, and it should not be assumed to result from agency alone. Mechanisms, constraints, environmental dependencies, and histories may all matter to its explanation.

This distinction matters diagnostically. A system's continued existence is not sufficient evidence that it is alive; nor is an isolated episode of repair or regulation. We must investigate whether the relevant activities and continuity arise within viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

## Definition, Diagnosis, and Evidence

[[box:aps-box-definition-diagnosis-and-evidence]]

The three questions can be stated directly.

**Definition:** What is Life? APS proposes viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

**Diagnosis:** Does the system under investigation exhibit that organisation, and with what degree of confidence can the judgement be made?

**Evidence:** Which observations, interventions, comparisons, and records support, qualify, or challenge the diagnostic judgement?

A feature that provides evidence must not be mistaken for the definition. Nor should a diagnostic procedure be treated as though it were itself a definition. Homeostasis, growth, responsiveness, and repair may all be informative, but their evidential force depends on what they reveal about the organisation of the particular system.

Three overlapping investigative perspectives are especially useful. **Activity evidence** concerns regulation, repair, and responses to disturbance. **Organisational evidence** concerns how constraints and processes depend upon and sustain one another. **Continuity evidence** concerns maintenance, recovery, and re-establishment of organisation through time. These are categories of inquiry, not three independently necessary tests or a sufficient checklist for life.

Diagnosis is consequently an evidence-based judgement rather than the mechanical application of a single observable signature. Some cases may remain unresolved because the available evidence does not establish whether the relevant organisational conditions are met.

## Why Appearance Can Mislead

Some nonliving systems display properties commonly associated with life. Fire can spread, consume resources, and propagate. Crystals can grow. Hurricanes can exhibit organised dynamics, feedback, and persistence. Such examples show why growth, movement, or apparent self-maintenance cannot independently establish life.

The distinction is not that nonliving systems are incapable of feedback or self-organising behaviour. Some plainly exhibit both. Rather, these observations do not, without further investigation, establish **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation** of the kind APS proposes as characteristic of Life.

The distinction must be established through investigation rather than assumed from the identity of the example. In a self-maintaining chemical or physical system, researchers would need to determine whether the constraints responsible for maintaining its organisation are reciprocally produced or sustained by that organisation, and whether the resulting dependencies maintain identifiable viability conditions. Where the evidence is incomplete, APS should withhold a categorical diagnosis. Organisational closure and viability orientation are proposed diagnostic requirements, not labels that make the distinction automatically.

The converse problem also arises. Dormant seeds and bacterial spores may show little visible activity while retaining organisation that supports subsequent recovery under suitable conditions. An assessment based only on immediate metabolic intensity or visible movement would therefore be misleading.

Appearance can prompt investigation, but it does not settle the organisational question.

## Biology Uses Multiple Lines of Evidence

Biological judgements usually depend on converging observations rather than a single feature. Metabolism, reproduction, growth, repair, and environmental responsiveness may each contribute relevant evidence. Yet no one of these observations is an unrestricted universal test: some living systems temporarily cease reproducing or enter states of sharply reduced activity, while some nonliving systems exhibit striking forms of growth or regulation.

APS therefore asks what the observations reveal about the organisation under investigation. Does regulation contribute to maintaining conditions relevant to the system's viability? Are the constraints sustaining that regulation themselves produced, maintained, or restored within a reciprocally dependent organisation? Does the apparent continuity reflect the persistence of that organisation, or merely the persistence of a pattern imposed by external conditions?

These questions cannot always be answered by observation alone. Experimental intervention, comparison across conditions, and investigation of the mechanisms involved may be required. Evidence can strengthen a diagnosis without making it certain, and contrary findings can justify withholding or revising a judgement.

## The Viability and Organisation Questions

The central diagnostic question is:

> **Does this system exhibit viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation, and what evidence warrants that assessment?**

This question includes two connected concerns. First, we ask whether the system has conditions relevant to its own continued functioning, and whether its activity contributes to maintaining or re-establishing those conditions. Second, we ask how the constraints that organise this activity are produced and maintained, and whether the required reciprocal dependencies are present.

These concerns should be investigated without presupposing that the system is alive. Viability-relevant conditions can be examined through their effects on organisational continuity, while constraint relations can be investigated through the processes by which they are maintained and through their dependence upon one another. The diagnostic judgement concerns whether these separately investigated relationships together warrant attribution of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. It must not follow merely from describing an already recognised organism in APS terminology.

A bacterium, a tree, and a mammal sustain viability in very different ways. Their differences matter, but they do not require unrelated definitions of Life. Equally, the existence of regulation or a stable pattern in an artificial or physical system does not automatically establish the organisation required by the definition.

A diagnostic assessment must also specify the system being evaluated. A process occurring within a living cell may contribute to the cell's viability without being an independently living system. Ecological dependencies may sustain organisms without establishing that every ecological assemblage is itself a living agent. The unit of diagnosis therefore matters as much as the scale at which evidence is gathered.

## Perturbation Reveals Organisation

Perturbation can be particularly informative because systems that appear similar under stable conditions may respond differently when resources, temperature, structural integrity, or environmental relationships change.

Responses involving compensation, repair, reorganisation, or recovery can reveal processes relevant to viability. Investigation may show how constraints are maintained, how a system responds to the loss of a component, or whether organisational capacities can be re-established after disturbance.

But perturbation is **evidence**, not a definition or an infallible test. Nonliving systems can also compensate for changes or maintain dynamic patterns. Conversely, a living system may fail to recover from a sufficiently severe disturbance, or may not display an observable response during dormancy. A failed response does not by itself establish that the system was never alive.

The diagnostic value of perturbation depends on the experimental context, the system's history, the severity of the disturbance, and the organisational relationships revealed. Its purpose is to discriminate between competing explanations of apparent persistence, not to reward every visible response with an attribution of life.

## Difficult Cases

**Viruses.** Viruses participate in biological processes and evolutionary histories, but their replication ordinarily depends on host-cell machinery. This raises questions about the relevant unit of organisation, the autonomy of viability maintenance, and whether viral processes participate in another system's living organisation rather than constituting an independently constraint-closed living system. APS supplies questions for assessment; it should not be represented as having settled every philosophical or biological dispute over viral life.

**Dormant seeds.** A viable dormant seed may exhibit very little visible activity. Its status cannot be determined simply by measuring current activity. Diagnosis must consider the organisation preserved through dormancy, the conditions under which activity can resume, and evidence of recoverable viability. A dead seed may resemble a viable dormant seed while lacking that capacity.

**Bacterial spores.** Spores likewise demonstrate why continuity and recoverability matter. Their specialised structures and reduced activity require investigation of preserved organisational dependencies and the possibility of re-establishing active life processes, not a simplistic requirement for continuous visible regulation. Dormancy also requires a temporal qualification. A system may retain the organised capacity for renewed viability-maintaining activity without continuously exhibiting the full range of that activity. Evidence for living status may therefore depend on the system's developmental history, the preservation of relevant structures and constraints, and its capacity for reactivation under appropriate conditions. This does not establish that every inactive structure is alive; it identifies a diagnostic problem that cannot be resolved by observing present activity alone.

**Artificial systems.** The origin or material composition of a system should not decide the question in advance. An artificial system displaying sophisticated feedback, adaptation, or self-repair would still require evidence of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. These capacities alone are insufficient, and an attribution of biological agency must be distinguished from a demonstration of explanatory gain.

**Protocells and origin-of-life systems.** Transitional systems may display some organisational dependencies without clearly satisfying all the conditions proposed in APS. Their importance lies partly in allowing researchers to investigate how constraint relations, viability conditions, and organised continuity arise together. Ambiguous results should remain ambiguous rather than being forced into a binary classification.

These cases do not all present the same problem. Dependence on a host, preserved capacity during dormancy, and the emergence of organisational closure are different diagnostic challenges. A common conceptual framework need not produce the same answer in every case.

## Life Is a Scientific Question

No isolated molecule, behaviour, or observable signature provides an automatic verdict on life. Diagnosis requires a definition, an identified system, relevant evidence, and a defensible interpretation of that evidence.

This does not make diagnosis arbitrary. Scientific judgements can be compared, challenged, and improved through better observations, experimental interventions, and more precise accounts of organisation. In difficult cases, uncertainty is a legitimate outcome when the available evidence does not warrant a stronger conclusion.

APS offers a way of structuring this investigation. Its concepts do not replace molecular, physiological, developmental, ecological, or evolutionary explanations. Nor does the successful application of APS vocabulary automatically demonstrate explanatory superiority over alternative accounts. Such claims require a specified explanandum and comparison with the strongest relevant explanations.

## How Do We Know Something Is Alive?

We determine whether something is alive by investigating whether it exhibits viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and by evaluating the evidence supporting that judgement.

Regulation, repair, environmental responsiveness, and recovery after disturbance can all contribute evidence. So can investigations of the reciprocal dependencies among the constraints sustaining a system. Organised persistence matters because it is a phenomenon to be explained, not because endurance alone defines Life.

A diagnosis may be well supported, qualified, or unresolved. Its strength depends on the evidence available and on the adequacy of competing interpretations. This is why an apparently inactive seed can remain living, while an impressively self-organising nonliving system need not qualify as alive.

## Why This Matters

Distinguishing definition, diagnosis, and evidence helps clarify debates concerning viruses, dormant organisms, synthetic biology, and possible extraterrestrial life. It prevents evidence from being mistaken for definition and discourages confident classifications based on isolated traits.

It also clarifies the contribution APS can legitimately make. APS proposes an organisational conception of Life and a structured set of questions for evaluating particular systems. Whether an APS-guided analysis yields distinctive explanatory advances in a particular case remains a further empirical and comparative question.

## Key Point

**Life is defined as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.** Diagnosing Life means assessing whether a particular system exhibits that organisation. Evidence comes from the investigation of viability-related activity, reciprocal organisational dependencies, and continuity through change. No single observable signature is sufficient by itself, and difficult cases may warrant qualified or provisional judgements.
