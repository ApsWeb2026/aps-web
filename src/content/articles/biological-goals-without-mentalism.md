## Introduction

Biological systems are routinely described in goal-directed terms. Cells repair damage, organisms acquire resources and avoid harmful conditions, developmental processes restore or produce organised form, and physiological systems compensate for perturbations. Such descriptions capture a genuine feature of living organisation: biological activity is not indifferent among all possible states and trajectories. Some conditions are maintained, some restored, some approached, and others actively avoided.

The explanatory difficulty begins when this directedness is treated as if it necessarily implied a represented future state, an intention to attain it, or a mind capable of conceiving it. Those implications do not follow from biological goal-directedness itself. Living systems can exhibit organised directionality without representing their goals, deliberating about alternatives, or acting under the guidance of mental states.

At the same time, eliminating mentalistic interpretation does not settle what biological goal-directedness is. The observation that a system repeatedly converges on an outcome establishes a pattern requiring explanation; it does not by itself identify the mechanism producing that pattern or explain why the outcome has biological significance. A rigorous account must therefore distinguish the directed form of activity from both its material production and its grounding within living organisation.

Within APS, that grounding is supplied by viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Viability conditions establish differences that matter to continued organised persistence, but a biological goal is not simply another name for a viability condition. It is a state or trajectory whose attainment, maintenance, restoration, or avoidance is privileged within viability-oriented organisation. Goal-directedness refers to the organised activity through which such differential orientation is expressed under changing conditions.

This distinction permits biological goals to be treated as real without making them mental. It also prevents goal language from functioning as a substitute for explanation.

**Where This Article Fits**

*This article is a clarification within the broader APS account of biological agency. It does not redefine life, agency, viability, normativity, function, purpose, or cognition. Its narrower task is to explain what warrants describing biological activity as goal-directed and how such directedness can be biologically significant without presupposing represented ends or psychological intention.*

*The foundational APS claim is that life consists in viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation enacted through biological agency. Viability specifies conditions under which that organisation can continue, while normativity concerns the asymmetrical significance of differences relative to those conditions. Function concerns the contribution of processes to viable organisation, and purpose concerns the orientation of living organisation toward its continued persistence. Goal-directedness belongs within this architecture but is not interchangeable with any of these concepts.*

*The distinction is particularly important at the boundary between agency and cognition. Biological agency is already directed because living activity is organised relative to conditions of persistence. Cognition requires more: integrated biological significance must modulate activity across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities in ways not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation. Goal-directed behaviour therefore cannot, by itself, establish cognition, intelligence, representation, intention, or mind.*

*The present article consequently addresses a limited explanatory question: when biological activity maintains, restores, approaches, or avoids particular states or trajectories, what makes that directedness biological rather than merely an observer's description of convergence on an outcome?*

## The Explanatory Problem of Goal-Directedness

Goal language has remained difficult for biology because it appears to capture something important while carrying conceptual commitments that biological explanation may not require. Organisms and their constituent processes display regulation, repair, developmental stability, plasticity, adaptive response, and compensatory reorganisation. These phenomena invite descriptions in which systems act towards outcomes rather than merely undergoing sequences of events.

Classical disputes about teleology therefore concerned not only whether goal language was permissible but what could make it scientifically explanatory. Accounts based on natural selection sought to explain apparent purposiveness through the historical production of adaptations, while teleonomic approaches attempted to retain the language of directed organisation without invoking final causes or conscious purposes. Organisational and autonomy approaches subsequently shifted attention towards the self-maintaining organisation of living systems and the mutually dependent constraints through which such organisation persists.

Contemporary debates have widened the problem again. Work on basal cognition, morphogenesis, diverse intelligence, biological agency, and goal-directedness has emphasised the remarkable capacities of cells, tissues, organisms, and developmental systems to compensate for perturbation, reach similar outcomes from different starting conditions, reorganise activity after disruption, and respond flexibly to changing circumstances. These phenomena have renewed interest in teleological description and, in some approaches, encouraged the extension of cognitive or mentalistic vocabulary beyond systems traditionally regarded as cognitive.

The evidential importance of these phenomena does not determine their explanatory interpretation. Plasticity, repair, error correction, adaptive regulation, or convergence on an outcome can establish that a system displays organised directedness, but the same observation may be compatible with different accounts of why the activity is directed and what, if anything, warrants calling the outcome a biological goal.

Recent work makes this distinction particularly clear. Desmond treats goal-directedness as an explanandum rather than defining agency by it, asking whether particular instances are adequately explained by natural selection or instead require organism-level "individual selection" according to a preference metric. This strategy usefully separates the observation of directed behaviour from the explanation offered for it. The APS account makes a related separation but locates the principal explanatory question elsewhere: beyond identifying convergence or the mechanisms that produce it, what grounds the privileged state or trajectory as biologically significant within the organisation of the living system?

The issue is therefore not whether directedness exists. It is how directedness, mechanism, and biological significance are related.

## Teleology, Teleonomy, and Contemporary Goal-Directedness

Teleological language traditionally explains phenomena by reference to ends: a process occurs for the sake of some outcome, or a structure exists because of what it accomplishes. In biology this language has always been difficult to eliminate completely. Hearts pump blood, repair processes restore damaged organisation, developmental systems produce characteristic forms, and organisms act in ways that maintain conditions necessary for continued life. Biological explanation repeatedly encounters processes whose intelligibility appears to depend upon the outcomes to which they contribute.

Modern biology has nevertheless supplied several ways of interpreting such language without invoking conscious foresight. Evolutionary accounts can explain the historical establishment of traits through differential reproductive success. Teleonomic accounts can describe apparently purposive organisation in terms of inherited programmes, regulatory mechanisms, or selected functions. Organisational accounts can ground directedness in the self-maintaining relations through which living systems constitute and preserve themselves. These approaches do not necessarily answer the same explanatory question, and their terminology should not be treated as interchangeable.

The distinction becomes especially important when goal-directedness is identified by behavioural or developmental convergence. A system may reach a similar state from different starting conditions, compensate for interference, or return towards a characteristic trajectory after perturbation. Such behaviour can provide evidence of regulation and can motivate goal-directed description. Yet convergence alone does not establish why the convergent state matters biologically. Nor does specifying a feedback mechanism automatically supply that grounding. A control architecture may explain how a target variable is restored while leaving open why that target has the status of a biological goal.

The APS account therefore neither eliminates teleological language nor accepts it at face value. Goal language is retained where it identifies genuine organisation of activity, but its explanatory content must be reconstructed in terms of materially realised biological dependencies. The central issue is not whether a system looks as though it is pursuing a goal, but whether the privileged outcome can be related to the endogenous organisation through which the system sustains its continued existence.

This requirement also places a limit on the use of mentalistic vocabulary. Flexible response, compensation, error correction, learning-like change, and apparently intelligent problem solving may all be experimentally important. Their presence does not, without further evidence, establish representation, intention, cognition, or mind. The same discipline that prevents physical convergence from being mistaken for biological goal-directedness also prevents biological goal-directedness from being mistaken for cognition.

## Three Questions: Directedness, Mechanism, and Biological Grounding

A more precise treatment of biological goals begins by separating three questions that are easily collapsed.

The first concerns **directedness**. What state or trajectory does the activity maintain, restore, approach, or avoid? Repair may restore structural integrity; homeostatic regulation may maintain variables within viable ranges; chemotaxis may produce movement relative to environmental gradients; development may repeatedly converge on characteristic morphology despite perturbation. These patterns identify outcomes around which activity is organised.

The second concerns **mechanism**. What processes and constraints generate, regulate, and restore that directed activity? Feedback, signalling, gene regulation, metabolic organisation, mechanical interactions, physiological control, developmental dynamics, and organism–environment coupling can all contribute to explaining how a system reaches or maintains an outcome. A mechanistic account is indispensable where the relevant mechanisms can be identified, but specification of mechanism does not by itself answer every question raised by goal-directed description.

The third concerns **biological grounding**. Why is this state or trajectory privileged within the living system rather than merely being a recurrent physical outcome? Here the explanatory target changes. The issue is not simply what causes convergence but what makes attainment, maintenance, restoration, or avoidance of the relevant state significant to the continued organisation of the system.

APS locates this grounding in viability-oriented organisation. A living system persists only within a constrained range of material and relational conditions. Differences relative to those conditions are therefore not equivalent: some sustain organised persistence, some threaten it, and some require compensatory activity if continuity is to be preserved. Biological goal-directedness becomes intelligible when activity is organised relative to these viability-relevant asymmetries.

The distinction prevents two opposite errors. One is to infer mentality from directedness: because activity converges on an outcome, the system must represent or intend that outcome. The other is to eliminate the biological problem by redescribing directedness entirely in causal-mechanical terms: because a mechanism producing convergence has been identified, nothing remains to explain about why that outcome is biologically privileged. APS rejects both inferences. Material mechanism and biological significance concern related but distinguishable explanatory dependencies.

This distinction also imposes an evidential constraint on APS itself. A recurrent or preferred outcome cannot simply be declared viability-relative because it occurs within a living organism. Viability grounds biological normativity, but it does not follow that every preference expressed by a living agent is directly a viability-relative biological norm. Where a particular state, preference, or trajectory is claimed to have viability-relative significance, that relation requires independent explanatory warrant.

## From Viability to Biological Goals

The APS treatment of biological goals can therefore be stated as an explanatory architecture rather than as an identity between goals and viability conditions.

**Viability** concerns the conditions within which a living organisation can continue to exist as that organisation. Because persistence is conditional, differences in internal and external conditions can bear differently upon continued organisation.

**Normativity** arises from this asymmetry. Conditions that sustain, restore, or extend organised persistence differ biologically from conditions that disrupt or destroy it. This normativity does not require an observer to assign value to the system, nor does it require the system to represent its own viability.

**Evaluation and significance** concern the modulation of living activity by viability-relevant differences. A difference becomes biologically significant when it makes a difference to the organisation of activity relative to conditions of persistence. Evaluation in this sense need not be conscious or representational.

**Constraint-regulated organisation** materially coordinates this activity. Living systems maintain themselves through processes and constraints whose continued operation depends upon, and contributes to, the organisation they sustain. Regulation, repair, compensation, development, and adaptive reorganisation occur within this materially realised organisation rather than being imposed upon it from outside.

**Goal-directedness** describes the resulting organisation of activity towards the maintenance, restoration, attainment, or avoidance of particular states and trajectories under changing conditions. It is therefore more than mere physical convergence, but less than psychological intention. Its biological status depends upon the relation between the directed activity and the viability-oriented organisation in which it occurs.

A **biological goal**, on this account, is a state or trajectory whose attainment, maintenance, restoration, or avoidance is privileged within viability-oriented organisation. Viability conditions ground the asymmetries through which such privileging becomes biologically intelligible; they should not simply be identified with the goals themselves.

These relations are not stages in a temporal sequence or levels in a hierarchy. They are different explanatory aspects of the same organised activity. Viability identifies the conditions of continued persistence; normativity identifies the asymmetry generated by dependence upon those conditions; evaluation and significance concern how relevant differences modulate activity; constraint-regulated organisation concerns the material organisation through which such modulation occurs; and goal-directedness concerns the directed pattern of activity thereby produced.

This architecture also establishes an important claim ceiling. It does not follow that every preferred outcome of every living organism is a biological goal in this specific sense. Learned, affective, social, aesthetic, or other preferences may be realised by a viability-oriented organism without their particular content being directly specified by viability. Treating any such preference as a viability-relative biological goal requires evidence for that dependency rather than inference from the mere fact that a living agent expresses it.

The resulting account preserves a substantive place for biological goals while keeping their explanatory status disciplined. Goal-directedness is neither an observer's convenient metaphor nor evidence by itself of a mind behind biological activity. It is a feature of organised living processes whose significance depends upon the material relations through which continued persistence is maintained.

## Purpose, Function, and Goal-Directedness

Purpose, function, and goal-directedness are closely related in biological explanation, but they should not be treated as synonyms. Each identifies a different aspect of organised living activity.

Within APS, purpose concerns the orientation of living organisation towards continued viable persistence. It does not denote a consciously conceived end or an externally assigned objective. A living system is purposive in the biological sense because its organisation is constituted through activity that sustains, restores, and reproduces the conditions under which that organisation can continue.

Function concerns contribution to viable organisation. A biological process or structure has a function insofar as its activity contributes to the organisation through which viability is maintained. Functional explanation therefore asks what contribution a process makes within the organised system of which it is a part.

Goal-directedness concerns the orientation of activity towards particular states or trajectories. It becomes visible when a system maintains a condition despite disturbance, restores organisation after damage, approaches conditions that support persistence, or avoids conditions that threaten it. A biological goal is the state or trajectory privileged through this directed organisation of activity.

These concepts therefore answer related but distinguishable questions. Purpose concerns the overall orientation of living organisation; function concerns the contribution made by a component or process within that organisation; goal-directedness concerns the organisation of activity towards particular outcomes. Their explanatory relations arise from the same viability-oriented organisation, but one should not be used as a substitute for another.

This distinction is particularly important when a process contributes to several outcomes or when the same observable outcome can be produced through different mechanisms. Identifying a function does not necessarily identify a goal, and observing convergence on a goal does not by itself establish the function of every process involved in producing it. Both claims require evidence concerning their respective organisational dependencies.

Evolutionary history adds another explanatory dimension without eliminating these distinctions. Natural selection can explain why traits and regulatory capacities with particular consequences became historically stabilised within populations. It does not follow that the present organisation of goal-directed activity is exhausted by its selection history. Conversely, showing that an organism presently regulates activity towards a viability-relevant outcome does not establish how the relevant organisation originated or evolved. Historical and organisational explanations can therefore be complementary without being interchangeable.

## Normativity and the Grounding of Biological Goals

Goal-directedness becomes biologically intelligible because living organisation is not indifferent among all possible conditions. A cell can remain viable within some ranges of temperature, pH, energy availability, ionic balance, structural integrity, and other conditions, while sufficiently large departures undermine or destroy its organisation. Comparable asymmetries occur at organismic and other biologically organised scales.

These differences are normative in the restricted biological sense used by APS. They establish conditions under which organisation can continue and conditions under which it cannot. No conscious judgement is required for such differences to matter biologically. Their significance follows from the dependence of organised persistence upon them.

This provides the grounding required for biological goals. A system may maintain a viable physiological range, restore damaged tissue, move towards resources, withdraw from harmful conditions, or reorganise development following perturbation because these activities occur within an organisation whose persistence depends upon differential relations to its conditions of existence. The resulting directionality is therefore endogenous to the organisation rather than merely attributed by an external observer.

Normative grounding should nevertheless be distinguished from the particular content of every preference that a living agent may express. The fact that viability grounds biological normativity does not entail that every learned, affective, social, aesthetic, or other preference is itself directly viability-relative. A preference for one harmless stimulus over another, enjoyment of a particular activity, or a socially acquired valuation may be realised by a viability-oriented organism without the preferred outcome thereby constituting a biological goal in the restricted APS sense.

This claim ceiling matters because otherwise viability could become explanatorily indiscriminate. If every outcome selected by an organism were classified as viability-relative merely because the organism selected it, no observation could count against the attribution. A specific claim that an outcome is a biological goal therefore requires evidence connecting its privileged status to viability-oriented organisation.

The same evidential discipline applies to alternative explanations. Failure to establish that a behaviour is explained by fitness, fixed regulation, or another comparator does not by itself establish a viability-relative explanation. Positive evidence is still required for the proposed dependency. Goal-directed explanation should therefore identify not merely what competing accounts fail to explain, but what evidence warrants the biological grounding being proposed.

## Constraint Closure and Endogenous Directedness

The distinction between biological and externally assigned goals becomes clearer when goal-directedness is considered in relation to constraint closure.

Living systems depend upon organised processes that constrain one another in ways that contribute to the maintenance of the organisation as a whole. Metabolic processes generate resources required for regulatory and structural constraints; those constraints in turn organise metabolic activity and other processes in ways that preserve the conditions under which the system can continue. The resulting organisation is materially open while exhibiting a form of organisational closure among the constraints that sustain it.

Goal-directedness occurs within this organisation. Regulation is not simply imposed upon an otherwise passive material system. The regulatory processes themselves belong to, depend upon, and contribute to the organisation whose persistence is at stake. When activity restores a viable state following perturbation, the target is therefore related to the conditions under which the regulating organisation itself can continue.

This distinguishes biological goal-directedness from many engineered control systems without requiring a simplistic contrast between organisms and machines. A thermostat, for example, can regulate temperature around a set point and can therefore display a limited form of target-directed control. The control architecture explains how deviations are detected and corrected. But the set point does not thereby acquire biological normativity: the continued existence of the thermostat as an organised system does not ordinarily depend upon maintaining the room at that temperature, and the target has been specified within an externally designed functional context.

The relevant distinction is therefore not that biological systems use feedback while machines do not, nor that organisms possess mysterious intrinsic purposes unavailable to artefacts. It concerns the organisational relation between the regulated outcome and the persistence of the system whose activity produces that regulation.

This distinction also leaves open the possibility that artificial, synthetic, or hybrid systems could acquire forms of endogenous goal-directed organisation. APS does not define biological directedness by material composition alone. The relevant question is whether the system exhibits viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation in which regulated states and trajectories are internally related to the maintenance of the organisation itself. That question must be answered empirically rather than by assigning biological status from surface resemblance to organismic behaviour.

## Goal-Directedness Without Mentalism

Biological goal-directedness does not require a system to represent a future outcome. A bacterium moving in relation to a chemical gradient, a plant altering growth in relation to light, a cell repairing membrane damage, or a developmental system compensating for perturbation can exhibit directed activity without evidence that the relevant goal exists as an internally represented proposition or imagined future state.

The absence of representation does not make the directedness unreal. It changes the kind of explanation required. Instead of asking what future state the system has in mind, the explanatory task is to identify how present organisation makes some states and trajectories significant and how material processes regulate activity accordingly.

This is one reason that terms such as "decision", "choice", "preference", "memory", and "problem solving" require care when applied to minimal biological systems. They can sometimes identify experimentally important features of behaviour, especially when systems discriminate among alternatives, integrate multiple inputs, alter responses in relation to prior conditions, or compensate flexibly for perturbation. Yet such vocabulary can also import psychological implications that the evidence does not establish.

Desmond's account of organismic agency illustrates the value of keeping these issues separate. By treating goal-directedness as something requiring explanation and proposing "individual selection" among alternatives as one candidate explanation, his analysis does not infer agency merely from the appearance of purposive behaviour. Positive evidence for an organism-level preference metric is required before individual selection can displace or supplement competing explanations.

The APS treatment differs in explanatory architecture but accepts the underlying evidential discipline. Observed convergence, flexible regulation, or preferential behaviour is not sufficient to establish the proposed biological dependency. Where a goal is claimed to be viability-grounded, the relation between the privileged state or trajectory and viable organisation must itself be warranted.

This also prevents mentalistic language from doing explanatory work that should instead be performed by biological analysis. To say that a cell "wants" to restore its morphology or that a bacterium "decides" where to move may be harmless shorthand in some contexts. It becomes misleading when the metaphor is treated as evidence for a psychological mechanism or when it obscures the material and organisational dependencies through which the directed behaviour is produced.

Biological goals can therefore be real without being mental goals. Their reality consists in the organised asymmetry of living activity: some states and trajectories are maintained, restored, approached, or avoided because of their relation to the continued organisation of the living system.

## Goal-Directedness and the Boundary of Cognition

Goal-directedness is necessary for understanding biological agency, but it should not be used as a sufficient criterion for cognition. This distinction is especially important in contemporary debates in which regulatory flexibility, adaptive behaviour, developmental compensation, or problem-solving capacity are sometimes treated as evidence that cognition extends throughout living systems.

Within APS, biological agency is already viability-oriented. A living system regulates its activity under conditions in which some differences matter to its continued persistence. Such regulation can produce remarkably flexible and context-sensitive goal-directed behaviour without establishing that the organisation has become cognitive.

Biological agency becomes cognitive when integrated biological significance modulates activity across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities in ways not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation. The relevant transition therefore cannot be inferred merely from greater behavioural complexity, a larger repertoire of responses, or more impressive convergence on a target state.

This criterion separates several phenomena that are easily conflated. A regulatory process may respond to deviation from a viable range without cognition. A developmental system may restore morphology following perturbation without thereby representing the morphology as a future goal. An organism may select among behavioural alternatives in a context-sensitive way while the evidence remains insufficient to establish the temporally integrated evaluation required for cognition.

The question is therefore not whether the system behaves "as if" it knows what it is doing. It is whether the organisation of activity depends upon integrated significance across viability-relevant possibilities rather than being adequately explained by immediate regulation, fixed conditional response, or other non-cognitive organisation.

This distinction does not diminish the biological importance of minimal forms of goal-directedness. On the contrary, it makes possible a more discriminating investigation of how increasingly integrated forms of evaluation arise without treating all biological regulation as cognition or reserving meaningful directedness exclusively for organisms with minds.

Goal-directedness thus belongs on the agency side of the agency–cognition boundary unless additional evidence establishes the dependencies required for cognition. It may contribute to cognition, and particular forms of preferential or anticipatory activity may become important evidence in cognition research, but goal-directedness alone does not cross that boundary.

## Artificial and Engineered Goal-Seeking

Artificial systems make the distinction between observable goal-seeking and biological grounding especially clear. Control systems, optimisation algorithms, autonomous robots, and artificial intelligence can all produce behaviour organised around target states. Some can compensate for disturbance, learn from prior performance, modify strategies, and select among alternatives in ways that superficially resemble biological goal-directedness.

These capacities should be described accurately rather than dismissed because they occur in artefacts. Target-directed control is a genuine organisational property where it is materially realised. The explanatory question is what kind of directedness is present and how its goals are grounded.

In many engineered systems, goals are externally specified through design, training objectives, reward functions, set points, or human use. The system may possess sophisticated mechanisms for attaining those targets while lacking any organisation in which the targets are conditions of its own viable persistence. In such cases, goal-seeking behaviour does not establish biological agency.

This is why complexity, autonomy of operation, learning capacity, or behavioural flexibility is insufficient by itself to establish the APS life criterion. A system can optimise effectively without being viability-oriented in the biological sense. It can correct errors without the errors constituting failures relative to endogenous biological normativity. It can preserve a target variable without participating in the constraint-closed organisation through which its own conditions of persistence are constituted.

The distinction is not intended to establish an immutable boundary between naturally evolved organisms and artificial systems. Synthetic systems could in principle present more difficult cases, particularly where self-maintenance, material autonomy, repair, endogenous regulation, and organisational closure become increasingly integrated. Such cases should be diagnosed by examining their organisation rather than decided by origin or substrate alone.

Artificial goal-seeking therefore provides a useful control case. It demonstrates that directed behaviour and even sophisticated target pursuit do not by themselves establish the biological grounding of a goal.

## What Goal Language Contributes to Biological Explanation

If biological mechanisms can be described without invoking intention, it might seem that goal language could simply be eliminated. That conclusion would be premature.

Goal-directed description can identify an explanatory pattern that is not captured merely by listing local causal interactions. When a system reaches similar outcomes from different starting conditions, compensates for disturbance, reorganises activity following damage, or maintains a variable despite changing environmental inputs, the relation between changing processes and a comparatively stable outcome can itself be biologically important.

The explanatory value of goal language lies in identifying this organised orientation while leaving open the mechanisms through which it is realised. A goal-directed description can therefore guide investigation by asking what is being maintained or restored, what perturbations the system can compensate for, what range of alternative trajectories remain compatible with the outcome, and what organisational dependencies make that outcome significant.

Such language becomes uninformative when the goal merely redescribes the observed result. To say that a system reaches state X because its goal is X explains nothing unless the attribution identifies additional dependencies that account for why activity is organised around X. Goal terminology should therefore increase explanatory discrimination rather than merely redescribe regularity.

APS supplies one route to such discrimination by relating biological goals to viability-oriented organisation. The resulting explanation is strongest when the proposed goal can be connected to independently established conditions of persistence, when the mechanisms producing or restoring the relevant state are materially specified, and when alternative explanations are tested rather than assumed away.

This requirement also preserves the distinction between organisational and evolutionary explanation. Natural selection may explain why a capacity for maintaining or attaining a particular state became historically established. Mechanistic analysis may explain how the relevant regulation is materially produced. APS asks in addition how the state or trajectory functions within the present viability-oriented organisation of the living system. These explanatory contributions may converge, but their convergence should be demonstrated rather than presupposed.

Goal language is therefore scientifically useful when it identifies a real explanatory dependency: organised activity is differentially oriented towards states or trajectories that matter to the continued organisation of the system. It is scientifically weak when it substitutes metaphor, intentional vocabulary, or outcome description for that dependency.

## Conclusion

Biology has good reason to describe living systems as goal-directed. Organisms and their constituent processes maintain conditions, restore organisation after disturbance, approach resources, avoid threats, compensate for perturbation, and often reach similar outcomes through different trajectories. These patterns are neither adequately dismissed as mere metaphor nor sufficient evidence for represented goals, intentions, or minds.

A rigorous treatment requires three questions to remain distinct. What state or trajectory is maintained, restored, approached, or avoided? What material mechanisms and organisational relations produce that directed activity? And what grounds the privileged outcome as biologically significant?

Within APS, the third question is answered through viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Viability establishes conditions upon which organised persistence depends; normativity arises from the asymmetrical significance of differences relative to those conditions; evaluation and significance concern the modulation of activity by such differences; and goal-directedness concerns the organisation of activity towards states and trajectories whose maintenance, restoration, attainment, or avoidance is thereby privileged.

Biological goals are therefore not identical to viability conditions. They are viability-grounded states or trajectories around which living activity is organised. Nor does the viability grounding of biological normativity imply that every preference expressed by a living agent is itself directly viability-relative. Particular goal attributions require evidence for the dependency being claimed.

This account permits goal-directedness to be biologically real without mentalism. It also prevents goal-directed behaviour from being treated as automatic evidence of cognition. Cognition requires additional organisation in which integrated biological significance modulates activity across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities in ways not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation.

The explanatory value of biological goal language consequently depends upon discipline. Observed convergence is not yet mechanism; mechanism is not yet biological grounding; failure of one explanation does not authenticate another; and directed behaviour is not yet cognition. When these distinctions are maintained, goal-directedness can contribute to biological explanation without invoking final causes, psychological intentions, or merely redescribing the outcomes biology seeks to explain.

## Key Point

Biological goal-directedness is the organised orientation of living activity towards the attainment, maintenance, restoration, or avoidance of particular states or trajectories. In APS, such goals are grounded in viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation: viability conditions establish the biological asymmetries through which outcomes can matter, but they are not simply identical to the goals themselves. Goal-directedness therefore requires neither represented ends nor conscious intention, and its presence alone does not establish cognition.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

What makes biological activity genuinely goal-directed without requiring represented ends, intentions, or minds, and what grounds particular states or trajectories as biological goals rather than merely observed outcomes?

### Architectural Role

This article establishes the relationship among viability, normativity, biological significance, constraint-regulated organisation, and goal-directedness within APS. It distinguishes three explanatory questions that should not be collapsed: the observable directedness of biological activity, the mechanisms through which that directedness is produced or restored, and the biological grounding that makes particular states or trajectories significant to the living organisation.

The article clarifies that viability conditions ground biological goals but are not simply identical to them. A biological goal is a state or trajectory whose attainment, maintenance, restoration, or avoidance is privileged within viability-oriented organisation. Goal-directedness therefore identifies an organised orientation of living activity without implying representation, intention, cognition, or mind.

### Position within APS

This article is a clarification article within the Cognition and Mind cluster. It does not independently redefine Life, Biological Agency, Viability, Normativity, Function, Purpose, or Cognition. Its role is to establish what warrants biological goal attribution and to distinguish goal-directed organisation from both mere physical convergence and mentalistic interpretation.

Within APS, viability establishes conditions upon which organised persistence depends, and normativity concerns the asymmetrical significance of differences relative to those conditions. Biological Evaluation and Significance concern the modulation of living activity by viability-relevant differences, while constraint-regulated organisation materially realises that modulation. Goal-directedness concerns the resulting orientation of activity towards states or trajectories whose attainment, maintenance, restoration, or avoidance is privileged within that organisation.

These dependencies are not a chronology, developmental succession, evolutionary progression, hierarchy, or sequence of levels. Nor does observable convergence, feedback control, adaptive flexibility, preference, target restoration, or behavioural complexity by itself establish a biological goal. A particular goal attribution requires warrant for the relation between the privileged state or trajectory and viability-oriented organisation.

Goal-directedness also remains distinct from cognition. Biological agency can be goal-directed without being cognitive. Cognitive attribution requires the additional condition that integrated biological significance modulates activity across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities in ways not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation.

### Corpus Significance

This article is the principal APS clarification of biological goal-directedness without mentalism. Its corpus function is to explain how biological goals can be real and explanatory without being represented future states or intentional objectives, while preventing viability, purpose, function, goal-directedness, and cognition from being treated as interchangeable concepts.

The article also establishes an important claim ceiling for subsequent APS work: viability grounds biological normativity, but it does not follow that every preference expressed by a living agent is itself directly viability-relative or constitutes a biological goal. Particular goal attributions require independent explanatory warrant. This preserves the discriminatory role of viability while providing a controlled basis for comparing biological goal-directedness with evolutionary, cognitive, and artificial forms of target-directed explanation.
