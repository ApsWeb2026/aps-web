**Where This Article Fits:**

*This Extension article examines how biological agency enters evolutionary explanation. It follows an activity from the historically formed capacities that make it possible, through its contribution to present viability, to any effects it has on conditions relevant to evolution. Its particular concern is what an explicit agency analysis adds once those effects have been explained.*

*[Biological Agency as the Activity of Self-Maintenance](/articles/biological-agency-as-the-activity-of-self-maintenance/) establishes the APS account of agency. [Agency as the Defining Activity of Life](/articles/agency-as-the-defining-activity-of-life/) develops its relation to life, and [Agency and Organised Persistence](/articles/agency-and-organised-persistence/) examines the relation between present activity and continuity through change. [Agency in Contemporary Biology](/articles/agency-in-contemporary-biology-the-emerging-agency-tradition-and-the-aps-framework/) situates APS within contemporary debates about agency.*

*[Evolution, Taxonomy, and the Reorganisation of Biological Explanation](/articles/evolution-taxonomy-and-the-reorganisation-of-biological-explanation/) addresses the broader questions of lineage continuity, historical transformation, and what evolves. The present article connects these inquiries through a field experiment in which a change in the behaviour of individual lizards helped explain a reversal in natural selection.*

## Introduction: Why Did Selection Reverse?

When Losos et al. (2006) introduced a terrestrial predator to experimental islets occupied by brown anoles (*Anolis sagrei*), selection initially favoured lizards with longer hindlimbs. In the second half of the study, shorter hindlimbs were favoured. By then the lizards had moved from the ground to higher, narrower perches. The researchers interpreted that shift in habitat use as the explanation for the reversal. Both phases took place within a single generation. Had the researchers measured selection only at the end of the year, they might have missed the initial advantage of longer hindlimbs altogether.

The case connects a change in what animals do with a change in the conditions under which they survive. That connection makes it possible to ask three questions separately. How did the lizards acquire the capacities through which they responded to the predator? How did exercising those capacities change the relationship between limb length and survival? What does identifying the response as biological agency add to the published explanation of the reversal?

The first question concerns the historical dependence of present activity. The second concerns its evolutionary consequences. The third concerns explanatory gain. The same episode brings all three questions into view, although the evidence needed to answer each is different.

## The History of a Capacity and Its Present Exercise

Darwin's account of natural selection gave biology a historical explanation of how inherited differences that affect survival and reproduction can accumulate over generations (Darwin 1859, ch. 4). The origin of an adaptive feature could be explained without supposing that the eventual result had been intended. That achievement left room for a separate inquiry into what an activity does for a living system in the present. Explaining how a capacity arose and explaining how its exercise contributes to continued functioning concern different relations, even when both explanations concern the same organism.

Mayr (1961) gave this distinction a familiar form in his account of proximate and ultimate causation. A behavioural or physiological explanation identifies the conditions and processes through which a response occurs. An evolutionary explanation can ask why the organism has the capacity to respond in that way. Which explanation is needed depends on the question. An account of how a lizard changes perch in the presence of a predator may be adequate for a study of its immediate response. An account of why this population has the capacities that make such a response possible calls for historical evidence specific to those capacities.

The distinction can become misleading if it is used to rule present activity out of historical explanation. Laland et al. (2011) argue that developmental and behavioural processes can alter the conditions under which subsequent evolutionary processes occur. It is possible, then, to follow the relationship in both directions. Evolutionary history helps explain the organisation through which an activity becomes possible. The effects of that activity can enter the conditions through which later evolutionary change occurs. Keeping the questions distinct makes the connection easier to investigate, because the causal relation required in each direction can be specified.

## From Present Activity to Evolutionary Consequence

APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation** and biological agency as **viability-oriented organisational activity** (Spencer 2026). Agency can be expressed through internal maintenance, regulation, and repair, as well as through activity in relation to the surroundings. The attribution depends on evidence that the activity contributes to the viability of the living system under the conditions at issue. In the lizard case, the pertinent question is how the shift in habitat use bears on the animal's exposure to predation. Applying the concept of agency here requires no inference about what the lizard consciously intends.

The evolutionary significance of organismal activity has a substantial literature of its own. Odling-Smee, Laland, and Feldman (2003) examine how organisms modify or stabilise environmental conditions that can influence selection, including conditions encountered by descendants. Laland et al. (2015) place such effects alongside development, plasticity, and inheritance in the extended evolutionary synthesis. Walsh (2015) argues for an organism-centred account in which the activities of organisms help shape evolutionary outcomes. The *Anolis* case concerns a shift in habitat use. It illustrates the broader point that animals can change the conditions they encounter through their activity, whether or not they physically modify the habitat.

To connect that activity to an evolutionary explanation, four claims need to be established separately. First, the response is enacted through capacities with a history. A claim about how those capacities evolved calls for historical evidence. Second, exercising them under particular conditions can contribute to viability. Evidence about predator exposure or survival bears on this attribution. Third, the activity can change a dependency relevant to evolution. Here, the shift in perch use is implicated in a change in the relationship between hindlimb length and survival. Fourth, a claim about a realised change across generations requires evidence connecting differences in survival and reproduction to transmission and a change in the population.

The field study documents a reversal in selection within one generation. The authors also proposed a longer-term expectation about hindlimb evolution if the new conditions persisted. That expectation is a further claim. Its evaluation requires evidence about how selection, reproduction, and inheritance combine across generations. Making the distinction explicit lets the result bear its full weight without asking the experiment to establish an outcome it did not measure.

Agency characterises the activity by its relation to viability. The evolutionary mechanism is specified through the material pathway linking activity to differences in exposure, survival, reproduction, or inheritance. In this case, that means identifying the effect of predator introduction on habitat use and explaining why the relationship between limb length and survival changed as the lizards occupied different perches. An agency attribution has explanatory value for that evolutionary question insofar as it helps establish a dependency in the pathway.

## What Persists Within a Lifetime and Across Generations?

The shift from present activity to historical change can make it seem as though evolution is organismal agency considered over a longer interval. The difference is clearer once we ask what persists. A lizard continues as a living individual through metabolism, regulation, repair, and changes in its relations with its surroundings. A lineage continues through reproduction and descent as its constituent organisms come and go. Both involve continuity through change. The continuity has a different bearer and depends on different processes in each case.

The APS **Process** perspective directs attention to the activities and transformations through which organisation persists, changes, or is lost. In an evolutionary inquiry, it can help specify how a developmental pattern recurs, how an environmental modification is carried forward, or how a lineage continues while its characteristics change. Evolutionary transformation is the phenomenon to be explained. Process is a perspective from which to investigate the continuity that makes a particular transformation identifiable.

**Scale** directs attention to the spatial and temporal extents over which the relevant dependencies operate. In the field study, perch use and predator exposure concern relations among organisms and features of the habitat. Selection is estimated through differences in survival among lizards over successive intervals. A claim about evolutionary response would extend the inquiry through reproduction and descent. Those extents matter because of the relations they allow the investigator to track. The persistence of a lineage through descent, by itself, supplies no evidence that the lineage acts to sustain its viability. An agency attribution at any extent requires evidence of viability-oriented organisational activity in the system identified.

Agency, Process, and Scale thus give investigators complementary questions to ask about the same biological situation. The questions connect when evidence shows how an activity sustains a living organisation, how the relevant dependency changes, and how far its consequences extend.

## What Does Agency Add to the Explanation?

DiFrisco and Gawne (2025) challenge the claim that an appeal to agency advances evolutionary research when evolved physiological and developmental mechanisms already account for the phenomena. Svensson (2026) questions the place of agency and natural purpose in modern evolutionary biology. For APS, the force of these objections can be assessed at a particular explanatory target. If an account of agency makes a difference, it should be possible to say which dependency it identifies, which inference it changes, or which possibilities it allows investigators to distinguish.

In the *Anolis* case, the matched target is the reversal in selection on hindlimb length following predator introduction. The published explanation already treats the shift from the ground to higher, thinner perches as a causal part of the account. It connects the animals' response to the predator with the changing circumstances in which differences in limb length mattered for survival. Restating the shift as viability-oriented activity connects an agency attribution to the evolutionary account. The comparison supplies no additional causal dependency or discriminating prediction for the reversal.

A different study might ask how lizards adjust their use of perches as risk varies, or under what conditions they continue to use a perch despite increased exposure. Such a study would concern the regulation of behaviour and would require comparison with the strongest account of that target. Its potential interest leaves the verdict on the reversal unchanged.

There is also a question about communication between inquiries. A physiological or behavioural account can establish what an activity contributes to viability, while an evolutionary account can trace which effects of that activity enter selection or reproduction. A shared vocabulary may make it easier for investigators to identify how those findings connect. A demonstrated improvement in communication would be a worthwhile result in its own right. Distinctive explanatory gain requires a demonstrated difference at the matched target.

## Conclusion

The *Anolis* experiment shows how rapidly the consequences of a trait can change when organisms alter their behaviour. The lizards' response helped change the conditions under which hindlimb length mattered for survival. The evolutionary history of the capacity to respond, the response's contribution to present viability, and the population's possible change across generations remain questions with their own evidential demands.

This is the explanatory role of agency examined here. Historically formed organisation makes present activity possible. The effects of that activity can, through identifiable material relations, influence the conditions under which future evolutionary change occurs. In the lizard case, the existing explanation already captures the behavioural dependency responsible for the reversal. Following the activity through both directions of dependence clarifies where a future agency analysis would have to make a difference.
