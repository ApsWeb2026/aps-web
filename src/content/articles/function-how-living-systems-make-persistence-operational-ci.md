> **Where This Article Fits**
>
> *Function and Normativity — Why Biological Organisation Matters* addresses the conceptual question of what biological function is within APS. It treats function as a present-tense, viability-relative contribution: a structure, process, or activity is functional insofar as it contributes to the persistence of a viability-oriented, constraint-closed living system.
>
> The present article begins from that account but asks a different question. Its concern is not primarily what makes an activity functional, but how multiple functional contributions are organised within the continuing activity through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.
>
> This places the article between the APS accounts of Function and Organised Persistence. Function identifies viability-relative contribution. Organised Persistence concerns the continuing organisation through which living systems remain viable despite material turnover, perturbation, development, and environmental change. The present article examines how functional contributions participate in that continuity.
>
> The article does not assume that organisational explanation is superior to mechanistic, causal, evolutionary, or other established forms of biological explanation. Different explanatory approaches may address different explananda, and mechanistic or evolutionary explanations may be sufficient for questions directed specifically at material realisation or historical origin.
>
> The narrower APS question pursued here is whether, when the explanandum concerns the coordination of functional contributions within continuing living organisation, additional organisational relations need to be specified. Whether identifying those relations produces explanatory gain cannot be established by APS architecture alone. It requires comparison against an appropriate alternative explanation of the same explanandum and evidence that the proposed organisational dependencies are materially implemented.

## From Function to Operational Organisation

Biological functions are commonly understood as contributions made by particular structures, processes, or activities. Hearts pump blood, leaves photosynthesise, kidneys regulate internal chemistry, membranes sustain gradients, enzymes catalyse reactions, and immune processes contribute to defence and repair. Such descriptions identify important causal and biological contributions.

Within APS, function is the viability-relative contribution a structure, process, or activity makes to the persistence of a viability-oriented, constraint-closed system. This is a present-tense relation. Evolutionary history may help explain why a structure arose, was retained, or changed, but that history is not itself what constitutes its present function in the APS sense. Likewise, identifying the mechanism through which a contribution is realised answers an indispensable question without necessarily answering every question that may be asked about the contribution.

The present article concerns a further organisational problem. A functional contribution is usually realised within conditions partly generated and maintained by many other activities. Cardiac contraction contributes to circulation only within relations involving vascular structure, respiration, metabolism, regulatory activity, and material exchange. Photosynthesis contributes to plant persistence within relations involving water transport, nutrient acquisition, gas exchange, development, repair, and environmental conditions. The relevant functions remain distinguishable, yet their contributions take effect within a materially organised living system.

Function as **operational organisation** refers to this organisation of functional contributions within continuing living activity. The phrase does not provide a second definition of Function. Rather, it identifies the explanatory target of this article: how viability-relative contributions are coordinated, maintained, altered, and sometimes re-established as the living system persists through change.

This distinction also clarifies the relation between Function and Purpose in APS. Purpose concerns the viability-oriented organisation of living activity without requiring intention, foresight, or external design. Function concerns particular viability-relative contributions made within that organisation. Neither concept is treated as an independent force or explanatory level, and Function need not be derived from a prior entity called Purpose. They are different analytic questions concerning the same living organisation.

The following sections examine several relations through which functional contributions may be organised: temporal coordination, functional integration, mechanistic realisation, constraint modulation, functional equivalence, adaptive reorganisation, and organism–environment relations. These are not separate dimensions or levels of biological reality. They are analytically distinguishable features of materially realised living organisation whose explanatory relevance depends upon the explanandum under investigation.

## Functional Organisation Through Time

Biological functions are realised through activity extended through time. Their contribution depends not only upon what occurs but often upon timing, duration, sequence, recurrence, and coordination with other processes. Functional organisation is therefore temporally structured.

Living systems undergo continual material change. Molecules are exchanged, proteins are produced and degraded, cells divide and die, tissues remodel, physiological states vary, and environmental conditions fluctuate. Yet functional contributions may continue despite these changes because the organisational relations through which they are realised are maintained or reconstructed.

This continuity should not be understood as the preservation of an unchanging state. Circulation, metabolism, repair, regulation, growth, reproduction, and environmental exchange occur through changing processes whose coordination must itself be maintained. Short-lived events may participate in processes operating over minutes or hours; physiological adjustments may extend across days; development and reproduction unfold over much longer periods. Their explanatory relevance arises from how these temporal relations contribute to the viability of the organism under particular conditions.

Material continuity and organisational continuity therefore need not coincide. The persistence of an organism does not require preservation of identical constituents. It depends upon materially realised organisation remaining sufficiently continuous for viability-oriented activity to continue. This is why replacement, repair, turnover, and reorganisation can coexist with biological continuity.

Temporal organisation is especially important when perturbation disrupts existing functional relations. Restoration does not necessarily reproduce the previous material state. Repair may substitute components, regulation may alter physiological conditions, development may reorganise tissues, and behaviour may change organism–environment relations. What matters for the present explanandum is whether functional contributions continue to be organised in ways that preserve or re-establish viable activity.

Function is therefore enacted rather than stored. Functional contribution exists through processes occurring within particular organisational conditions, and those conditions themselves may require continual maintenance. The explanatory problem is not simply what a structure does at one moment, but how relevant contributions remain organised through changing circumstances.

## Functional Integration

Many biological functions make their contributions within relations with other functional activities. Circulation depends upon materially relevant relations with respiration and metabolism; nutrient acquisition affects metabolic possibilities; repair alters the conditions under which tissues continue to operate; regulation modifies the activity of multiple processes; and behaviour can change the environmental conditions within which physiological processes occur.

Functional integration refers to such coordination among contributions. It does not mean that all processes form one undifferentiated network or that every contribution depends equally upon every other. Relevant integration is specific to the explanandum. The task is to identify which contributions interact, what dependencies connect them, how those relations are materially realised, and what difference they make to viability.

This conception avoids treating integration as an additional controlling entity. No separate organising force need be invoked above the biological processes themselves. Functional integration is realised through the processes, constraints, material structures, regulatory relations, and organism–environment interactions through which contributions affect one another.

This also explains why local disturbance can have broader functional consequences without invoking causal action by a higher level. A change in one process may modify a constraint or resource condition upon which other processes depend. Those altered conditions can then change local causal activity elsewhere in the system. What appears from one analytic perspective as system-wide reorganisation remains materially implemented through specific biological relations.

Functional integration also provides a basis for understanding malfunction. Because function is viability-relative, a process can fail to make the contribution normally relevant under particular conditions. Regulation may become inadequate, repair may fail, coordination may deteriorate, or an activity may continue causally while no longer supporting viable organisation.

Malfunction therefore involves an asymmetry between conditions that support persistence and conditions that undermine it. No mental evaluation or external judgement is required for that asymmetry to be biologically real.

The biological significance of a functional contribution is consequently context-dependent without being arbitrary. The relevant context consists of materially organised conditions affecting viability. Functional integration asks how those conditions are jointly maintained by activities whose contributions may be distinct but interdependent.

## Mechanisms as Realisers of Functional Organisation

Biological functions are materially realised through mechanisms. Cardiac pumping depends upon organised muscular contraction and electrical activity. Enzymatic functions depend upon molecular interactions. Stomatal regulation depends upon changes in guard-cell physiology. Immune functions involve interacting cellular and molecular processes. No account of functional organisation can dispense with the material processes through which these activities occur.

Mechanistic explanation may be sufficient when the explanandum concerns how a particular biological activity is produced. APS does not add organisational terminology merely because a mechanism has been identified. The additional organisational question arises when the explanandum concerns how the realised process contributes to viability or how its contribution is coordinated with other activities through changing conditions.

A mechanism can therefore be considered in more than one explanatory relation. Its internal organisation may explain how an activity is produced. Its relation to other processes may explain how that activity contributes to a wider functional outcome. Its contribution may also change when organisational conditions change, even if some of its local causal properties remain similar.

This distinction is particularly important where comparable functional contributions can be realised through different mechanisms. If different material arrangements can contribute to the same viability-relevant outcome, then explaining the material implementation of one arrangement and explaining the persistence of the contribution across alternative arrangements are different explanatory tasks.

Mechanistic and organisational analyses should therefore not be treated as competitors by default. For explananda concerning both material realisation and viability-relative contribution, the analyses may be complementary. For explananda concerned only with one of those questions, the additional analysis may provide little or no gain.

The relation is consequently target-relative. APS does not claim that organisation is intrinsically deeper than mechanism or that mechanisms become biologically real only through an organisational description. Rather, it asks which materially implemented relations must be specified for the explanandum at hand.

## Functional Organisation Through Constraint Modulation

Functional integration raises a further question: how are the conditions enabling coordinated activity maintained as components turn over, resources change, perturbations occur, and physiological demands vary?

APS addresses this question through constraint modulation.

Constraints, in this context, are materially implemented conditions that channel biological activity. Membranes maintain gradients; tissue arrangements influence transport and interaction; vascular systems organise resource distribution; regulatory circuits alter rates and thresholds; extracellular structures affect cellular behaviour; and behavioural activity can modify environmental conditions relevant to physiology. These constraints do not replace the underlying physical and chemical processes. They help establish the conditions under which those processes occur.

Constraint modulation concerns activity through which such conditions are maintained, altered, restored, or reorganised. Physiological regulation can shift internal conditions. Repair can reconstruct damaged relations. Development changes the constraints within which later processes operate. Immune activity may alter local tissue conditions. Behaviour may change exposure, resource availability, temperature, or other environmental variables relevant to continued functioning.

This account preserves local causation. Every relevant biological event remains materially realised through physical, chemical, and biological interactions. APS does not explain a local process by introducing a separate organisational cause acting from another level. Wider organisational relations matter when they are themselves materially implemented and thereby alter the conditions within which local causal processes occur.

Constraint modulation therefore provides one way to specify the dependence of functional contributions upon organised conditions. A process may contribute successfully only within a limited range of temperature, chemical composition, resource availability, structural relation, or regulatory state. Other activities may help maintain those conditions. Their relation can then become explanatorily relevant when the target is continued functional performance through disturbance or change.

Living stability is consequently compatible with continual reorganisation. Persistence need not consist in preserving one configuration. It may instead involve maintaining or re-establishing viability through changes in the constraints governing ongoing activity.

In this sense, constraint modulation links functional organisation with organised persistence without introducing a separate causal layer. Mechanisms realise activities; those activities may maintain or modify constraints; altered constraints change the conditions under which further activity occurs. These relations are analytically distinguishable but materially continuous.

## Functional Equivalence

Living systems often preserve functional contributions despite changes in the particular structures or mechanisms through which they are realised. Development, injury, physiological change, environmental variation, and evolutionary transformation can all alter material implementation while some organisational contributions remain sufficiently continuous for viability to persist.

Functional equivalence refers to the capacity of different structures, processes, or mechanisms to realise comparable viability-relative contributions under specified conditions. Equivalence need not mean exact identity of effect. It concerns preservation of the organisational role relevant to the explanandum.

Examples can include alternative metabolic pathways contributing to a comparable physiological outcome, different regulatory arrangements maintaining similar ranges of internal conditions, compensatory processes after damage, or evolutionary variation in structures that continue to make comparable functional contributions. In each case, the important distinction is between the identity of the mechanism and the continuity of the contribution.

This does not make organisation explanatorily prior to mechanism. If the explanandum asks how one of the alternative processes works, mechanistic analysis remains central. If the explanandum instead asks how a functional contribution persists despite changes in material realisation, comparison among those realisations and the organisational relations they preserve becomes relevant.

Functional equivalence therefore helps explain how material variation can coexist with organisational continuity. The living system need not preserve identical components in order to preserve every viability-relevant contribution. Alternative arrangements may sometimes maintain the relevant conditions through different causal routes.

This capacity contributes to robustness without implying that alternatives are merely redundant backups. Different realisations may operate under different conditions, involve different trade-offs, or become available only through development or reorganisation. Their equivalence is therefore always relative to a specified functional contribution and set of conditions.

## Adaptive Reorganisation

Functional equivalence becomes especially important when living systems reorganise activity in response to changing circumstances. Developmental transitions, physiological regulation, tissue repair, acclimation, behavioural adjustment, and responses to perturbation can all alter relations among functional contributions.

Adaptive reorganisation refers here to changes in materially implemented functional organisation that help maintain or re-establish viability under changed conditions. It does not mean that every biological change is adaptive, nor that organisational description has explanatory priority over the mechanisms through which change occurs.

Such reorganisation may involve several processes at once. Changes in metabolism can alter regulatory requirements; immune responses can modify tissue conditions; repair can change mechanical and biochemical relations; behavioural activity can alter exposure to environmental conditions; and development can transform the organisation within which later functions are realised. The relevant dependencies must be traced rather than inferred merely from the fact that the organism persists.

This perspective helps distinguish stability from fixity. A living system can remain organisationally continuous while changing materially and functionally. Stability may depend precisely upon the capacity to alter activity when existing relations no longer support viability.

Context therefore matters. The same perturbation need not produce the same functional response in different organisms, developmental states, physiological conditions, or environments. The viability-relevant organisation present at the time helps determine which adjustments are possible and which contributions become important.

Within APS, such viability-oriented reorganisation can be interpreted as an expression of biological agency: viability-oriented organisational activity through which the living system modifies the conditions of its continued functioning. This does not imply cognition, foresight, or conscious choice. Agency here refers to living activity organised relative to viability.

Adaptive reorganisation thus links present function with persistence through change. It shows how continuity can be maintained not only by preserving existing relations but also by materially reorganising them when conditions alter.

## Organism–Environment Relations

Functional organisation cannot be understood exclusively in terms of processes located inside the organism. Living systems exchange matter and energy with their surroundings, depend upon environmental resources and conditions, and frequently modify those conditions through their own activity.

This dependence does not require treating organism and environment as a single undifferentiated agent. In APS, biological agency remains centred in the viability-oriented organisation of the organism. Environmental structures and processes can nevertheless participate materially in the conditions under which the organism's functions are realised.

Nutrient acquisition depends upon resource availability. Respiration depends upon appropriate environmental conditions. Plant water relations depend upon soil, atmospheric conditions, roots, vascular processes, stomatal activity, and other materially linked processes. Behaviour can alter exposure, temperature, resource access, predation risk, and other circumstances relevant to viability.

Organisms also modify aspects of their surroundings. Roots alter local soil structure and chemistry; organisms construct nests, burrows, or protective structures; microorganisms change chemical environments; and behaviour reorganises the relation between the organism and available resources. Such effects can in turn alter conditions under which later biological activity occurs.

These relations are explanatory only when their material implementation and relevance to the explanandum can be specified. Invoking a broad environment or ecological context is not sufficient. The task is to identify which environmental relation changes which biological condition and how that difference bears upon the relevant functional contribution.

This preserves the distinction between distributed dependence and distributed agency. A function may depend upon relations extending beyond the organism's physical boundary without the environment thereby acquiring that function or becoming a biological agent in its own right.

Organism–environment relations therefore form part of the operational context within which functional contributions are maintained. They demonstrate again that viable persistence may depend upon organised relations extending across spatial and temporal extents while remaining materially implemented through specific interactions.

## Operational Persistence

The preceding sections identify several relations through which functional contributions may participate in the continuing activity of living systems: temporal coordination, integration among contributions, mechanistic realisation, constraint modulation, functional equivalence, adaptive reorganisation, and organism–environment interaction.

**Operational persistence** names the continuing accomplishment realised when these and other relevant activities collectively maintain or re-establish viable living organisation.

It is not an additional biological function, an overarching mechanism, or a separate causal principle. Nor does it imply that every organism must realise persistence through the same arrangement of processes. Operational persistence refers to continuity achieved through ongoing functional activity despite material turnover and changing conditions.

This perspective is particularly useful where the explanandum concerns persistence through change. The question is then not merely which components are present or which mechanism produces a particular effect. It is how relevant functional contributions continue to be realised as components are replaced, conditions vary, perturbations occur, and organisational relations are altered.

Temporal organisation helps specify how contributions are coordinated through time. Functional integration identifies dependencies among contributions. Mechanistic analysis establishes material realisation. Constraint modulation identifies changing conditions governing activity. Functional equivalence concerns preservation of contributions across alternative realisations. Adaptive reorganisation concerns changes that maintain or restore viable organisation. Organism–environment analysis identifies materially relevant relations extending beyond internal physiology.

These are not stages in a causal chain. Nor are they levels through which explanation must pass. They are distinct analytic questions that may become relevant to a persistence explanandum in different combinations.

Within APS, operational persistence can therefore be understood as the functional enactment of viability-oriented organisation through time. Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation; functional contributions participate in maintaining that organisation; and biological agency is the activity through which such organisation is enacted and modified.

This formulation does not imply that operational persistence replaces mechanistic or evolutionary explanation. It specifies the explanandum to which an organisational analysis is directed: the continued or re-established viability of a materially changing living system.

## Explanatory Scope and Comparative Limits

The APS account developed here has a specific scope. It provides a way of analysing biological function when the explanandum concerns how viability-relative contributions are coordinated, maintained, or reorganised within continuing living activity.

That scope should not be confused with a claim that all function questions require organisational explanation.

A mechanistic explanation may adequately answer how a particular biological activity is produced. A causal-role analysis may adequately characterise what contribution a component makes within a specified system. An evolutionary explanation may adequately address the historical origin, retention, or transformation of a trait. Selected-effect and other historical accounts address questions that are not replaced merely by redescribing present organisation.

The APS distinction is that present Function concerns current viability-relative contribution. Evolutionary history can help explain why a functional organisation exists in its present form without constituting that present contribution itself. Historical and present-tense explanatory questions can therefore be related without being collapsed or ranked universally.

Likewise, organisational analysis does not become explanatory merely by using a broader vocabulary. Saying that a process is part of organised persistence, functional integration, or viability-oriented organisation adds little unless the relevant dependency can be specified. An organisational claim requires identifiable relata, a materially implemented relation between them, and an account of how variation in that relation bears upon the explanandum.

This requirement places a substantive constraint upon APS itself. Conceptual coherence is not sufficient evidence of explanatory gain. Neither the integration of several biological phenomena nor their description through one APS vocabulary establishes that APS explains them better.

A comparative claim would instead require the same explanandum to be considered under APS and an appropriate comparator. The question would then be whether APS identifies a relevant dependency, contrast, or organisation not already captured adequately by the comparator and whether specifying it improves explanation of the target.

Several outcomes remain possible. APS may identify additional explanatory structure. It may complement an established explanation without superseding it. It may redescribe relations already adequately captured elsewhere. The comparator may prove more informative. The gain may be limited to a particular explanandum or set of conditions. Or APS may provide no relevant additional explanatory gain.

That openness is a condition of treating APS as an assessable comparative explanatory methodology rather than assuming the success of its own conceptual architecture.

The present article therefore establishes an organisational proposal about biological function. It specifies what APS means by operational organisation and identifies the kinds of materially implemented relations that may matter when functional contributions are examined in relation to organised persistence. Whether those relations yield distinctive explanatory gain remains an empirical and comparative question.

## Conclusion

Biological functions are realised through structures, processes, and activities that make viability-relative contributions within living systems. Identifying those contributions remains a fundamental biological task. So does explaining the mechanisms through which they are realised and the evolutionary histories through which they arose and changed.

The question addressed in this article is narrower: how are functional contributions organised when the explanandum concerns the continuing maintenance or re-establishment of viable living organisation?

APS approaches this question through the idea of operational organisation. Functional contributions occur through time, depend upon materially realised mechanisms, participate in relations with other contributions, operate within modifiable constraints, may sometimes be preserved through alternative realisations, can be reorganised under changing conditions, and frequently depend upon materially specific organism–environment relations.

These organisational relations do not constitute a separate causal domain. They are realised through the biological processes whose conditions and interactions they describe. Organisational explanation is therefore not a replacement for material causation but an analysis of materially implemented dependencies where those dependencies are relevant to the specified explanandum.

This perspective also explains why the continuity of function need not require continuity of identical material realisation. Living systems undergo continual turnover and change. Functional contributions can nevertheless persist when the organisational relations required for viability are maintained, reconstructed, or realised through alternative mechanisms.

Operational persistence names this continuing accomplishment. It does not add another entity to biological explanation. It identifies the maintenance and re-establishment of viability-oriented living organisation through ongoing functional activity.

Within APS, Function, Purpose, Agency, Process, Scale, and Organised Persistence therefore illuminate different questions about one living organisation rather than forming a hierarchy of explanatory causes. Function concerns viability-relative contribution. Purpose concerns the viability-oriented organisation of activity. Agency concerns viability-oriented organisational activity. Process concerns continuity despite change. Scale concerns the spatial and temporal extents across which persistence is organised. Organised Persistence concerns the continuity achieved through their materially realised organisation.

The contribution of the present account is consequently methodological as well as conceptual. It specifies an organisational explanandum and the relations that APS proposes may be relevant to it. It does not establish in advance that those relations outperform existing explanations. Such a judgement requires comparison against a matched alternative and evidence that the proposed dependencies make a genuine explanatory difference.

Function as operational organisation should therefore be understood as a testable explanatory proposal: biological functional contributions can be examined not only individually but in terms of the materially implemented relations through which they participate in maintaining and re-establishing viable living organisation. Where that analysis identifies dependencies relevant to the explanandum, it may add explanatory value. Where it does not, no APS-specific gain should be claimed.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How are biological functional contributions organised within the continuing activity through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence?

### Architectural Role

This article develops the APS account of Function from the identification of individual viability-relative contributions to the organisation through which those contributions participate in continuing living activity. It therefore connects the APS concept of Function with Organised Persistence without treating either as explanatorily prior to mechanism, evolutionary history, or other established explanatory approaches.

### Builds On

This article builds on the APS accounts of Life, Biological Agency, Viability, Constraint Closure, Function, Purpose, and Organised Persistence. In particular, it presupposes the account developed in *Function and Normativity — Why Biological Organisation Matters*, where Function is treated as the present-tense viability-relative contribution a structure, process, or activity makes to the persistence of a viability-oriented, constraint-closed system.

### Develops Into

The analysis developed here supports subsequent APS investigation of organised persistence, adaptive reorganisation, and the ways functional organisation contributes to later questions concerning biological evaluation and cognition. These are relationships among explanatory questions within APS, not a developmental chronology, causal hierarchy, sequence of biological levels, or claim that biological evaluation or cognition is required for Function.

### Related Explanatory Questions

The article is closely related to questions concerning:

- how living organisation persists through change
- how mechanisms materially realise functional contributions
- how constraint modulation alters the conditions under which biological processes operate
- how functional contributions remain stable across changing material realisations
- how organisational explanations should be compared with mechanistic, evolutionary, causal-role, and other explanations of the same biological target

### Position within APS

Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic projections of the one viability-oriented, constraint-closed living organisation examined in this article. Agency concerns what living systems do; Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change; and Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. They are not independent causes, components, dimensions, domains, or hierarchical levels.

Function as operational organisation is therefore situated within APS as an analysis of how viability-relative contributions participate in materially realised organised persistence. Whether this analysis provides explanatory gain in any particular case remains explanandum-relative and comparator-relative and must be established through specification of the relevant dependencies and empirical evidence.
