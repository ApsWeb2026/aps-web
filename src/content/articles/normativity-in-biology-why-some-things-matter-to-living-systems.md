 *This Core article introduces the APS interpretation of biological normativity: the viability-relative significance of conditions and activities for living organisation. It develops the conceptual relationship between vulnerability, regulation, and organised persistence, while leaving the more detailed attribution of biological function to *Function and Normativity — Why Biological Organisation Matters*. It also identifies philosophical questions that remain open.*

## Introduction - Normativity in Biology — Why Some Things Matter to Living Systems

Living systems are not indifferent to the conditions in which they exist. Changes in temperature, nutrient supply, membrane integrity, or ecological circumstances can support continued functioning, disrupt it, or make recovery necessary. Such differences are materially consequential even where no organism consciously judges them. Biological normativity concerns how these differences can be understood as standards of better and worse functioning, success and failure, or functional adequacy and impairment.

Within Agency–Process–Scale (APS), the starting point is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. A living system persists not simply by remaining present, but through activities and organisational relations that maintain or restore conditions compatible with its continued functioning. APS interprets the asymmetry between sustaining and undermining conditions as the basis for understanding biological significance. This interpretation does not require an observer to assign purposes or an organism to formulate judgements. Nevertheless, the existence of objective consequences for viability must be distinguished from the further philosophical claim that those consequences constitute intrinsic normative standards. The latter claim remains open to argument.

## Why Normativity Appears Problematic

The language of normativity is familiar in human affairs, where standards may be expressed as moral obligations, social expectations, explicit rules, or considered judgements. Applied to biology, similar language can seem puzzling. Physical and chemical descriptions specify what occurs under given conditions; they do not ordinarily describe a reaction as having made a mistake. Yet biological explanation routinely distinguishes functioning from malfunction, successful repair from failed repair, and conditions compatible with continued life from those that lead to breakdown.

These distinctions are not all equivalent. A physiological measurement can establish that a process has changed, whereas identifying that change as a malfunction requires a relevant standard of performance and a justified account of the system to which it applies. A change may be detrimental to one biological unit while beneficial to another. The philosophical problem is therefore not resolved by observing that organisms can survive or perish. It concerns how standards of biological functioning can be warranted by the organisation, history, and circumstances of living systems rather than simply projected onto them by investigators.

## Human Normativity Is Not the Starting Point

Human normative practices involve language, deliberation, social coordination, cultural inheritance, and historically developed institutions. These capacities make human normativity distinctive, but they should not be assumed to be prerequisites for every biologically relevant distinction between successful and unsuccessful functioning. A bacterium need not possess a moral vocabulary to regulate its activity in response to changing environmental conditions, and a plant need not deliberate in order to adjust growth or physiological activity in ways that affect its continued functioning.

APS therefore examines the biological conditions of evaluation before turning to reflective judgement. This does not mean that moral, epistemic, or social norms can be deduced from the requirements of biological persistence. Their historical and institutional development introduces forms of justification and obligation that cannot be reduced to physiological viability. The more limited claim is that living systems exhibit material asymmetries that can support an account of biological significance without first invoking human cognition.

## Norms Without Minds

Consider a living cell whose continued functioning depends on the integrity of its membrane, the coordination of metabolism, and the regulation of exchanges with its surroundings. Under some conditions these relations are maintained; under others they deteriorate, triggering compensatory responses or, if disruption becomes sufficiently severe, loss of viable organisation. The difference between these trajectories is not invented by an observer. It is reflected in the cell's material dependencies and in the consequences of perturbation.

Neither reflective awareness nor symbolic representation is needed for such dependencies to exist. What requires explanation is the additional step from describing them to judging that one trajectory counts as better *for the cell*. Organisational accounts, including those developed in biological autonomy and adaptivity research, address this step by relating performance to the maintenance of the organisation that makes the activity possible (Moreno and Mossio 2015; Di Paolo 2005). APS adopts a viability-oriented interpretation of this relationship, while recognising that the full philosophical grounding of intrinsic normativity remains contested.

## Viability, Vulnerability, and Failure

Viability refers to conditions under which a specified living organisation can continue functioning. It is not identical to mere duration or persistence: a system may remain materially present after losing the organisation that previously sustained its life. Conversely, a living system may undergo extensive material turnover, temporary impairment, or reorganisation while maintaining continuity of its biological organisation. The distinction between persistence and viable persistence therefore requires attention to the processes and constraints that make continued functioning possible.

Vulnerability is central to this account. Regulation matters because relevant conditions can vary; repair matters because organisation can be damaged; and compensatory activity matters because disturbances can place continued functioning at risk. The possibility of failure gives practical explanatory significance to differences between sustaining, destabilising, and restorative changes. Yet vulnerability alone cannot define life or establish normativity. Nonliving systems can also deteriorate or recover stable states. The biological interpretation depends on evidence of the particular viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation under investigation, rather than on the mere occurrence of change or breakdown.

## Normativity Beyond Teleology

Historical accounts of living systems have often explained biological parts and activities by reference to purposes. Philosophical and scientific treatments of biological teleology have taken diverse forms, however, and should not be equated indiscriminately with conscious design or supernatural intention. The challenge for a naturalistic account is to explain why biological functions and failures are not merely convenient descriptions imposed from outside the system.

Mechanistic analysis can identify the components, interactions, and conditions responsible for a biological outcome. It is indispensable for explaining how regulation, repair, or breakdown occurs. Nevertheless, an account of the mechanism alone may leave open the question of which outcome counts as successful functioning and why. Different theories address this issue in different ways. Selected-effects approaches appeal to relevant histories of selection, while organisational and adaptivity accounts emphasise relations between activities and the maintenance of living organisation. APS approaches the question through viability-oriented organised persistence, in substantial continuity with the latter traditions (Moreno and Mossio 2015; Di Paolo 2005).

On this interpretation, a process has biological significance insofar as its consequences bear on the conditions of continued functioning of a specified living system. This supplies an intelligible organisational framework for investigating malfunction and repair, but it does not, by itself, resolve every philosophical dispute about the origin or authority of intrinsic biological standards. In particular, it does not establish that viability orientation is a novel or superior solution relative to the strongest existing accounts.

## Mattering as Viability-Asymmetry

The expression *biological mattering* can be made precise by asking what difference a condition or process makes to the viability of a particular system. Nutrients may support metabolic organisation; toxins may disrupt it; damage may compromise an essential relation; and repair may restore conditions required for continued functioning. Such consequences are objective and open to investigation. Their significance, however, is relative to the organisation whose continued functioning is being assessed, not an unqualified property of a substance or event.

This qualification matters because biological consequences are frequently mixed. A compound harmful to a host may benefit a parasite; the proliferation of a cancer cell lineage may be detrimental to the organism containing it. A description of what sustains or undermines viability must therefore identify its subject and the relevant temporal and organisational extent. APS uses the contrast between sustaining, restoring, impairing, and undermining conditions as an explanatory guide, while requiring independent justification of the biological unit to which the contrast is applied.

## Normativity and Biological Agency

APS defines biological agency as viability-oriented organisational activity. In a living system, metabolism, development, physiological regulation, repair, behaviour, and ecological interaction can contribute to maintaining or re-establishing viable conditions. Normativity and agency are thus related descriptions of the same materially realised organisation: normativity concerns the significance of differences for viability, whereas agency concerns the organised activity through which those differences are encountered and, in some circumstances, regulated.

Agency should not be described as a separate mechanism that first receives normative standards and then converts them into action. Nor does every component contributing to an organism's viability thereby become an independent agent. Attribution of agency depends on the relevant organisational unity and on evidence of viability-oriented activity. This distinction is developed in the canonical Agency Anchor and helps prevent the explanatory language of agency from multiplying biological individuals without justification (Spencer 2026).

## Normativity and Evaluation

Biological evaluation, in the APS sense, refers to organised differentiation and modulation of activity in relation to conditions affecting viability. A microorganism may alter movement in response to a chemical gradient, while a plant may modify physiological or developmental activity in response to water availability. In such cases the explanatory question is not whether the system consciously judges its circumstances, but how the relevant conditions affect its activity and continued functioning.

This use of *evaluation* is deliberately narrower than reflective assessment. It describes material regulation in relation to viability, not an automatic attribution of cognition, representation, or experience. Some theories treat adaptive regulation as central to minimal sense-making, but the presence of regulation alone does not settle whether cognition should be attributed. APS can therefore recognise biological evaluation without identifying every regulatory response as a cognitive act (Di Paolo 2005; Thompson 2007).

## Normativity and Semiosis

The relationship between normativity and semiosis introduces a further interpretive question. Environmental cues, chemical gradients, and signals may influence biological activity because their effects are relevant to the system's organisation and prospects of continued functioning. In a suitable explanatory context, such relations can be analysed as biologically significant rather than as arbitrary correlations observed from outside.

Nevertheless, material sensitivity to a difference is not by itself sufficient to establish semiosis in every theoretical sense of that term. Accounts of signs, interpretation, and meaning impose additional explanatory requirements, and those requirements differ across traditions. APS locates the possibility of biological significance within viability-oriented organisation, while leaving the stronger attribution of sign processes to the relevant specialised account. This preserves the relationship between normativity and semiosis without collapsing one concept into the other.

## Making Normativity Empirically Tractable

A viability-oriented interpretation becomes scientifically useful when its claims can be connected to independently identifiable features of living organisation. Investigators can examine physiological limits, perturbation responses, compensatory regulation, repair, adaptive reorganisation, and thresholds beyond which viable functioning cannot be restored. Such evidence can distinguish the conditions under which an organisation is maintained from those under which it is impaired or lost.

The evidential task is more demanding than observing that a system persists. It requires specifying the relevant biological unit, identifying the organisational relations at issue, and establishing how particular changes affect its functioning. Comparisons across normal and perturbed conditions, together with mechanistic, developmental, ecological, or evolutionary evidence, can strengthen the assessment. They can also show why a component may retain an attributed function even when it fails to perform it on a particular occasion. These investigations make viability-related consequences empirically tractable, but do not automatically settle the philosophical question of intrinsic normative authority.

## Philosophical Scope and Outstanding Questions

The distinction between empirical consequences and normative standards is the first unresolved issue. Demonstrating that a change supports or undermines the continued functioning of a living system establishes an objective causal relationship. Explaining why that relationship constitutes a standard intrinsic to the system requires further argument. The concepts of life, viability, and agency are mutually connected within APS, but conceptual interdependence cannot replace an independent account of why a particular standard is warranted. The possibility of circular explanation must therefore remain visible rather than being hidden by the language of biological mattering.

A second issue concerns the individuation of the normative beneficiary. Viability is system-relative, and the boundaries of the relevant system cannot always be taken for granted. Cancer cells, symbiotic associations, and other multiscale organisations may contain units whose conditions of continued functioning conflict. Identifying which organisation supplies the standard of assessment calls for evidence of material integration, organisational dependence, continuity, and the explanatory question being asked. A choice of explanatory scale cannot, by itself, establish biological individuality.

These questions are not unique to APS. Organisational accounts of autonomy and function, theories of adaptivity, and evolutionary approaches to proper function already offer substantial resources for addressing them. APS must be compared with those accounts in their strongest relevant forms, rather than assuming that its conceptual vocabulary provides an independent solution. Its integration of Agency, Process, and Scale may help clarify the relationship between viability, activity, continuity, and explanatory extent, but additional explanatory gain must be demonstrated in particular cases.

A six-stage critical investigation completed in November 2026, APS–PHIL–01, retained the existing APS conceptual architecture with qualifications. It identified the grounding of intrinsic biological normativity and the individuation of the organisational bearer of that normativity as open questions. This finding is a constraint on future claims, not evidence that the APS interpretation is incoherent or that competing theories have definitively settled the issues.

## From Biological to Human Normativity

Human forms of evaluation are historically and evolutionarily continuous with the capacities of living organisms, but their distinctive features require separate explanation. Language, shared practices, institutions, and reflective justification make moral, epistemic, and social norms different from the physiological conditions under which an organism remains viable. Biological vulnerability may be part of the background against which human practices develop, yet it does not determine the content or authority of those practices.

APS therefore distinguishes the biological significance of sustaining or undermining living organisation from the reasons people give for what they ought to believe or do. The relation between these domains is a subject for historical, psychological, and philosophical investigation, not a straightforward derivation of moral or epistemic norms from biological survival. Recognising this distinction allows biological normativity to be examined without either reducing human normative life to physiology or projecting human deliberation onto organisms that do not exhibit it.

## Normativity in APS

The APS account begins from a material fact: living organisation is maintained under conditions in which its functioning can be supported, disrupted, restored, or irreversibly lost. The framework interprets these viability-relative differences as the basis of biological significance and relates them to the activity of agency and the temporal continuity of organised persistence. It does not require an external designer or conscious judgement to identify the underlying causal asymmetries.

Its philosophical interpretation remains qualified. Empirical evidence can establish that a condition matters to the continued functioning of a specified system, but does not by itself prove that the resulting standard is intrinsic in the full philosophical sense. Nor does conceptual integration demonstrate explanatory superiority over established organisational, adaptivity, or evolutionary accounts. APS contributes a way of coordinating these questions while preserving the need for independent evidence, justified attribution, and strongest-comparator assessment.

## Key Point

Within APS, biological normativity is understood through the viability-relative differences by which living systems maintain, restore, impair, or lose organised continuity. These differences are materially real and empirically investigable. Their interpretation as intrinsic normative standards remains a philosophical proposal to be evaluated alongside established alternatives, particularly where the grounding of normativity and the identification of its organisational bearer are disputed.

### APS Interpretation and Existing Explanations

APS interprets biological normativity through viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary perspectives on the activity, continuity, and explanatory extent of that organisation.

This interpretation overlaps substantially with established accounts of biological autonomy, organisational functions, and adaptivity. APS does not claim that viability orientation alone resolves the philosophical grounding of intrinsic normativity or demonstrates superiority over these alternatives.

### Evidence and Explanatory Limits

Viability-related differences can be investigated through perturbation, regulation, repair, physiological failure, and recovery.

Such evidence establishes material consequences for specified biological organisations, but does not automatically establish intrinsic normativity or identify the appropriate organisational beneficiary. These questions require independent philosophical and empirical justification.

### Contribution and Outstanding Questions

The article clarifies the place of biological normativity within APS and its relationships to agency, organised persistence, function, and explanatory scale.

It preserves two unresolved questions identified by APS–PHIL–01: the grounding of intrinsic biological normativity and the individuation of the system whose viability provides the relevant standard.

Any additional explanatory contribution must be demonstrated against the strongest relevant alternatives rather than inferred from conceptual integration.
