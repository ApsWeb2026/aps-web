## The Problem with “Emergence”

The term *emergence* is widely used in biology to describe phenomena such as multicellularity, development, cognition, and ecosystem organisation. It often signals the appearance of complex, system-level organisation that is not immediately evident at smaller scales.

However, its meaning varies widely. In some contexts, emergence functions as a neutral shorthand for complex pattern formation. In others, it carries stronger philosophical implications, including claims that higher-order properties are irreducible to underlying processes or that novelty appears in ways that resist explanation.

This ambiguity creates a problem for biological theory. When emergence is used descriptively, it adds little explanatory content. When used strongly, it risks introducing discontinuity into systems that are otherwise understood as dynamically organised and historically continuous. The result is a term that often signals complexity without clarifying its basis.

## Conventional Framings of Emergence

In philosophy of biology, emergence is typically understood in two broad ways.

- **Weak emergence** refers to higher-order properties that arise from interactions among components but remain explainable in principle through those interactions. It is often associated with complex systems, self-organisation, and nonlinear dynamics.  

- **Strong emergence** refers to properties that are genuinely novel and irreducible, such that they cannot be explained—even in principle—by underlying processes. This view introduces ontological discontinuity and is rarely adopted in contemporary biological science.

While weak emergence is widely accepted as a descriptive label, both forms leave open a key question: what kind of organisation makes such phenomena possible?

## The APS Reframing

APS does not treat emergence as an explanatory category. Instead, it treats it as a descriptive term that requires further specification in terms of biological organisation.

In APS, living systems are not passive assemblies of parts from which higher-order properties “arise.” They are viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisations in which processes continuously regenerate the constraints that sustain them.

What is often described as emergence corresponds, in APS terms, to the reorganisation of constraint-closed systems through viability-oriented evaluation, semiosis, and coordination across scale.

This shift has several implications:

- There is no need to posit ontological novelty beyond organised process  
- Apparent “new properties” reflect reconfigured constraint relations, not unexplained additions  
- Biological organisation is historically continuous, even when it becomes more differentiated  

APS does not deny that higher-order patterns and system-level properties arise in complex biological systems. Rather, it rejects the use of emergence as an explanatory stopping point. The task of explanation is to account for how such organisation is produced, maintained, and transformed through viability-oriented processes.

## Emergence and Evolution

In evolutionary contexts, emergence is often used to describe the appearance of new traits, functions, or forms of organisation. APS clarifies this by distinguishing between appearance and transformation.

Evolution does not generate biological organisation from arbitrary material processes. It transforms existing, viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation through variation, differential persistence, and inheritance.

Novelty arises through the reorganisation of constraint-closed systems, not through the appearance of properties that lack an organisational basis.

From this perspective:

- Multicellularity, cognition, and ecological organisation are historical transformations of organisation  
- Their properties reflect new configurations of constraint relations, not irreducible novelty  
- Evolutionary explanation remains continuous across time and scale  

This aligns with the APS view that evolutionary processes operate within systems already capable of sustaining organised persistence.

## When (and How) to Use “Emergence”

APS does not prohibit the use of the term emergence. However, it restricts its role.

Emergence may be used as a descriptive shorthand for:

- complex pattern formation  
- higher-order organisation  
- system-level properties not evident at smaller scales  

But it should not be used:

- as a substitute for explanation  
- to imply discontinuity or irreducibility  
- to obscure the organisation that makes the phenomenon possible  

Where precision is required, APS prefers terms such as *formation*, *development*, or *transformation of organisation*.

## Implications for Biological Explanation

By reframing emergence, APS clarifies the structure of biological explanation.

Explanation does not proceed from parts to wholes via unexplained leaps. Instead, it proceeds through the analysis of constraint-closed organisation, viability-oriented process, and scale-coupled constraint relations.

What appears as emergence is the outcome of these dynamics viewed at a particular resolution.

This has methodological consequences:

- Biological explanation should identify how organisation is produced and maintained  
- Explanations should track constraint relations across scale and time  
- Apparent novelty should be analysed as organisational transformation, not treated as primitive  

## Conclusion

Emergence has long served as a placeholder for complexity in biological thought. APS retains its descriptive usefulness but removes its explanatory ambiguity.

Biological organisation does not emerge in a metaphysically problematic sense. It is continuously enacted through viability-oriented, constraint-closed processes.

## Key Point

In APS, what is often called emergence is the ongoing production and transformation of constraint-closed biological organisation—continuous, processual, and grounded in viability-oriented activity.
