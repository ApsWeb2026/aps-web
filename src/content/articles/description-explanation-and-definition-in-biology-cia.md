## Introduction

Biology relies heavily on ordinary language.

Organisms are said to:
- pursue goals  
- possess functions  
- follow norms  
- adapt to survive  
- operate at higher and lower levels  

Such language is often useful and harmless as shorthand description. Problems arise when descriptive language is silently transformed into explanatory or definitional claims.

APS therefore distinguishes between:
- description  
- explanation  
- definition  

This distinction is not linguistic policing. It is a way of stabilising explanatory meaning within biological theory.

## Descriptive Language

Descriptive language reports observable patterns, behaviours, or outcomes.

For example:

- “The organism survives.”
- “This structure has a function.”
- “The system behaves as if it has a goal.”
- “There are different levels of organisation.”

These statements may be perfectly acceptable descriptions.

By themselves, however, they do not explain:
- what makes the system living  
- how the behaviour is organised  
- what grounds the function or goal-like activity  

APS therefore does not reject ordinary descriptive language. It clarifies what must be organisationally true for such descriptions to make explanatory sense.

## Explanatory Language

Explanation identifies the organisational conditions under which biological phenomena occur.

At this point, language carries ontological commitments.

For example:

- “The organism survives because it wants to survive.”
- “Function explains behaviour.”
- “Norms guide the system.”
- “Higher levels control lower ones.”

Here, familiar terms are no longer acting as descriptive shorthand. They are being used to explain biological organisation.

APS intervenes at this level by asking:

What organisation makes these claims meaningful?

The framework consistently re-anchors explanation in viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation rather than in outcomes, abstractions, or metaphorical shorthand.

## Definitional Language

Definitions specify what kind of thing something is.

Examples include:

- “Life is reproduction.”
- “Agency is a behavioural capacity.”
- “Normativity is rule-following.”
- “Function implies purpose.”

APS is explicitly a definitional framework. Precision matters here because definitions determine the boundaries between:

- life and non-life  
- genuine agency and passive behaviour  
- biological normativity and external evaluation  

Definitional clarity therefore requires greater precision than ordinary descriptive use.

## Category Drift

Many conceptual confusions in biology arise through category drift.

Category drift occurs when:
- outcomes are treated as causes  
- abstractions are treated as mechanisms  
- heuristic descriptions are treated as ontology  

Examples include:

- treating goals as causes rather than descriptions of viability-oriented organisation  
- treating functions as intentions rather than contributions to persistence  
- treating norms as external rules rather than viability-relative asymmetries  
- treating levels as ontological strata rather than analytic partitions of scale  

APS attempts to prevent such drift by continuously returning explanation to organisation itself.

## Why APS Does Not Reject Ordinary Language

APS does not attempt to abolish familiar biological language.

Scientists and ordinary speakers will continue to use terms such as:
- goal  
- function  
- adaptation  
- survival  
- regulation  

The framework intervenes only when these terms are used explanatorily or definitionally without clarification of the organisational assumptions they carry.

The issue is therefore not vocabulary but explanatory grounding.

APS preserves ordinary language for communication while requiring conceptual precision where explanatory claims are being made.

## Organisation Rather Than Outcomes

A recurring principle in APS is that explanation must be grounded in organisation rather than outcomes alone.

For example:

- survival does not explain persistence unless the organisation sustaining viability is specified  
- function does not explain behaviour unless the contribution to organised persistence is clarified  
- adaptation does not explain biological change unless ongoing reorganisation is identified  

This shift moves explanation away from retrospective outcome description and toward the organised processes through which living systems sustain themselves.

## Why This Matters

The distinction between description, explanation, and definition matters because biology routinely moves between these modes without making the transitions explicit.

As a result:
- descriptive shorthand may acquire unintended ontological force  
- metaphors may harden into mechanisms  
- explanatory ambiguities may become embedded in theory  

APS attempts to stabilise biological explanation by making organisational commitments explicit.

This is not terminological rigidity. It is an attempt to preserve explanatory coherence across agency, process, and scale.

## Conclusion

Ordinary biological language is often descriptively useful but explanatorily ambiguous.

APS distinguishes descriptive, explanatory, and definitional uses of language in order to clarify what organisational conditions biological explanations presuppose.

The framework therefore does not reject familiar terminology. It clarifies the explanatory commitments carried by that terminology when it is used to explain or define living systems.

Many conceptual confusions arise not from ordinary language itself, but from unnoticed shifts between description, explanation, and definition.

APS seeks to make those shifts visible.

## Key Point

APS distinguishes descriptive, explanatory, and definitional uses of biological language in order to prevent explanatory ambiguity and re-anchor biological explanation in viability-oriented organisation rather than outcomes or metaphorical shorthand.
