## The Famous Question

Few questions have been asked more often than:

**What is the meaning of life?**

The question appears in philosophy, religion, literature, psychology, and everyday conversation. It has inspired ancient debates, modern self-help books, spiritual traditions, scientific speculation, and countless late-night discussions. For many people, it is the ultimate question—the one that lies behind all the others.

The answers have been remarkably diverse. Some have argued that the meaning of life lies in happiness, virtue, love, knowledge, service, or spiritual fulfilment. Others have suggested that life has no inherent meaning and that meaning must be created rather than discovered. Religious traditions often locate meaning in a relationship with the divine, while secular philosophies frequently emphasize personal values, human flourishing, or contribution to others.

Popular culture has also had its say. In Douglas Adams' famous science-fiction satire *The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy*, a supercomputer calculates the answer to the ultimate question of life, the universe, and everything. After millions of years of computation, the answer turns out to be simply: **42**.

The joke works because it exposes a deeper difficulty. Before we can answer a question, we must understand what the question means. Knowing the answer is not much help if we do not know what is being asked.

The same problem arises with the meaning of life. Different people often mean very different things when they ask the question. Some are asking about purpose. Others are asking about value, significance, morality, happiness, or fulfilment. Some are asking whether life has any meaning at all.

For this reason, the question is more complicated than it first appears. The phrase *meaning of life* bundles together several different ideas that are often treated as though they were the same thing.

APS approaches the question from a somewhat different direction. Rather than beginning with human beliefs, personal fulfilment, or cosmic purpose, it begins with life itself. It asks a prior question:

**How does meaning arise in living systems?**

This shift does not dismiss the traditional question. Instead, it seeks to understand the biological foundations that make questions of meaning possible in the first place.

Before asking what the meaning of life is, however, we must first ask a simpler question:

**What do we mean by meaning?**

## Meanings of Meaning

The word *meaning* seems simple enough, but it is used in several different ways. When people ask about the meaning of life, they are often combining multiple questions into a single phrase. Some of these questions overlap, while others are quite distinct.

Clarifying these different senses of meaning is an important first step. Without doing so, discussions about the meaning of life can quickly become confused, with different people answering different questions without realizing it.

One common form of meaning is **linguistic meaning**. This is the sense in which words, symbols, or messages convey information. If someone asks, "What does this sentence mean?" they are asking about interpretation. Linguistic meaning concerns representation, communication, and understanding.

Another form is **existential meaning**. This is the sense most people have in mind when they wonder whether life is meaningful. Questions about purpose, significance, fulfilment, identity, and belonging all fall within this domain. Existential meaning concerns how individuals understand their place in the world and what makes life worth living.

A third form is **moral meaning**. Here the focus shifts from what life means to how we ought to live. Questions about right and wrong, responsibility, justice, and human flourishing belong to the moral domain. While moral meaning often overlaps with existential meaning, the two are not identical. A person may find meaning in activities that are not necessarily moral, and moral obligations may sometimes conflict with personal sources of meaning.

There is also what we may call **human meaning**. Human beings create systems of significance that extend far beyond immediate biological concerns. Art, science, religion, philosophy, culture, law, history, and collective memory all contribute to forms of meaning that are uniquely human in their complexity. Human meaning is deeply connected to language, symbolism, imagination, and social life.

These forms of meaning are familiar because they are part of everyday human experience. Yet there is another kind of meaning that is often overlooked.

This is **biological meaning**.

Biological meaning does not begin with language, philosophy, morality, or culture. It concerns something more fundamental: the way living systems distinguish between conditions that matter and conditions that do not. A shortage of water matters to a plant. A chemical gradient matters to a bacterium. A predator matters to a deer. In each case, certain features of the world become significant because they affect the continued activity of the organism.

At first glance, these various forms of meaning may appear completely separate. In reality, they are related. Linguistic meaning depends on minds capable of interpretation. Moral meaning depends on beings capable of evaluating actions and consequences. Human meaning depends on cognitive, social, and cultural capacities. All of these forms of meaning arise within living systems.

The relationship is therefore not one of identity but of continuity. Biological meaning is not the same as moral meaning. Moral meaning is not the same as existential meaning. Human meaning is not reducible to biological processes alone. Yet each form of meaning builds upon capacities that emerge within the living world.

Understanding this relationship allows us to approach the question of life's meaning from a new direction. Instead of beginning with human purpose or cosmic significance, we can begin by asking how meaning becomes possible at all.

### The Many Meanings of Meaning

Meaning can refer to several different but related things.

**Linguistic meaning** concerns words, symbols, and messages. When we ask what a sentence means, we are asking about interpretation and representation.

**Existential meaning** concerns significance and purpose. When people ask whether life is meaningful or worthwhile, they are usually asking an existential question.

**Moral meaning** concerns values and obligations. Questions about how we ought to live, what is right or wrong, and what responsibilities we have to others belong to this domain.

**Human meaning** refers to the rich forms of significance created through language, culture, memory, imagination, science, religion, art, and social life. These meanings are uniquely elaborated in human societies.

**Biological meaning** concerns a more fundamental question: why some conditions matter to living systems in the first place. A lack of water matters to a plant. A chemical signal matters to a bacterium. A predator matters to a deer. In each case, something becomes significant because it affects the organism's continued activity and persistence.

These forms of meaning are related but not identical. Linguistic meaning concerns interpretation. Existential meaning concerns significance. Moral meaning concerns value. Human meaning concerns the symbolic and cultural worlds that people create. Biological meaning concerns the conditions that matter to living systems. Understanding the relationships among these different forms of meaning provides the foundation for the discussion that follows.

These different meanings should not be confused, but neither should they be treated as completely unrelated. Together they form a continuum extending from the biological conditions that make significance possible to the rich symbolic and cultural worlds created by human beings.

The remainder of this article focuses primarily on **biological meaning**. APS argues that understanding how meaning emerges in living systems provides a foundation for understanding many of the higher forms of meaning that humans experience and create. To explore this idea, however, we must first distinguish meaning from another concept with which it is often confused: **purpose**.

## Why Biology Changes the Question

For most of human history, questions about meaning have been approached through philosophy, religion, ethics, and personal reflection. These traditions have explored what makes life valuable, what purposes human beings should pursue, and how individuals can live meaningful lives. Such questions remain important, but they often begin with human experience already taken for granted.

Biology introduces a different perspective.

Instead of starting with human beliefs or values, biology begins with life itself. It asks what distinguishes living systems from nonliving ones and whether that distinction has implications for understanding meaning.

Consider a rock resting on a hillside. Rain may erode it, sunlight may warm it, and gravity may eventually carry it downslope. These events affect the rock, but they do not matter *to* the rock. The rock has no stake in what happens. It does not regulate its condition, respond to preserve itself, or distinguish between favourable and unfavourable circumstances.

The same is true of rivers, clouds, planets, and stars. They undergo change and participate in complex physical processes, but there is no sense in which certain outcomes are better or worse for them. They simply follow the dynamics of the systems of which they are a part.

Living systems are different.

A bacterium moves toward nutrients and away from harmful chemicals. A plant alters its growth in response to light, water, and competition. An animal seeks food, avoids danger, repairs injury, and reproduces. In each case, some conditions contribute to continued viability while others threaten it. Certain states become favourable, others unfavourable. The organism's activities are organized around maintaining itself within a changing environment.

This observation does not require consciousness, intention, or reflection. A plant does not need to contemplate its future for drought to matter to it biologically. Nor does a bacterium need beliefs or desires for nutrients to have significance. The distinction lies not in awareness but in organization. Living systems are organised in ways that make some outcomes consequential for their continued existence.

This shift is subtle but profound. Once a system actively maintains itself, the world ceases to be merely a collection of external events. Some environmental conditions become relevant. Some differences become important. Some interactions contribute to continued functioning while others undermine it.

From a biological perspective, then, the question is no longer simply:

> What is the meaning of life?

A prior question emerges:

> Why do some things matter to living systems at all?

This question does not replace the traditional search for meaning. Instead, it asks how meaning becomes possible in the first place. Before there can be human values, personal purposes, moral commitments, or cultural ideals, there must first be living systems for which conditions can be favourable or unfavourable, beneficial or harmful, relevant or irrelevant.

APS approaches meaning from this biological starting point. Rather than treating meaning as something added to life from outside, it asks how significance emerges from the organization and activity of living systems themselves.

To understand this idea, we must examine more closely what biological meaning is and how living systems generate it.

## Biological Meaning: When Things Matter

If meaning is not limited to language, culture, or human reflection, what form does it take in the living world?

APS approaches this question through the idea of **biological meaning**.

Biological meaning arises when differences become consequential for the continued activity of a living system. A condition becomes meaningful when it affects whether an organism can maintain itself, function effectively, and persist through time. In this sense, meaning is not primarily about interpretation, symbolism, or conscious understanding. It is about significance within the context of life itself.

This may initially seem like an unusual use of the word *meaning*. We often associate meaning with language, ideas, or subjective experience. Yet the biological world is filled with situations in which certain conditions clearly matter to living systems long before language, symbolic thought, or conscious reflection enter the picture.

Consider a bacterium moving through its environment. Some chemical gradients lead toward nutrients, while others indicate harmful substances. The bacterium's responses are not random. Certain features of the environment influence whether it can acquire resources, maintain its internal organization, and continue its activities. Those environmental differences therefore have biological significance.

The bacterium does not need to understand what nutrients are. It does not need beliefs, intentions, or conscious awareness. The significance arises because some environmental conditions contribute to the continuation of the organism while others undermine it.

The same principle applies across the living world.

A plant growing in the shade alters its growth patterns in response to available light. Water availability influences root development. Temperature affects metabolism. Herbivore damage triggers defensive responses. These environmental differences matter because they influence the plant's ability to maintain itself and continue its life processes.

Again, this does not imply that plants possess human-like awareness or reflective understanding. The point is simpler and more fundamental. Some conditions contribute to continued viability, while others threaten it. Through their organization and activity, plants distinguish between these possibilities in biologically meaningful ways.

Animals provide even more familiar examples. Food, shelter, mates, predators, social partners, and environmental hazards all influence survival and reproduction. Many animals can learn from experience, remember past events, and modify behaviour in response to changing circumstances. These capacities greatly expand the range of meaningful relationships an organism can have with its environment, but they do not create biological meaning from nothing. Rather, they build upon a more basic condition already present throughout life: some things matter.

This observation points toward a deeper feature of living systems.

Unlike nonliving objects, organisms are organized around their own continued existence. They continuously acquire resources, regulate internal conditions, repair damage, respond to environmental change, and maintain functional integrity despite ongoing disturbances. Life is not a static state but an ongoing process of active self-maintenance.

APS describes this continuity through the concept of **organised persistence**.

Organised persistence refers to the capacity of living systems to maintain and renew themselves through time despite constant material turnover and environmental change. The molecules within an organism may change, cells may be replaced, and behaviours may vary, yet the living system continues because its organization remains dynamically sustained.

This perspective helps explain why meaning emerges in the first place.

If a system were entirely indifferent to its own continuation, nothing would be biologically significant. Environmental changes would simply occur without any distinction between favourable and unfavourable outcomes. For living systems, however, some conditions support continued organization while others disrupt it. The difference matters because the organism itself is engaged in maintaining a viable relationship with its environment.

This introduces another important APS concept: **viability**.

Viability refers to the capacity of a living system to remain functionally organized under changing conditions. A viable organism continues to operate within the conditions necessary for its ongoing activity. Threats to viability are therefore biologically meaningful because they affect the organism's ability to persist.

Seen from this perspective, biological meaning is neither mysterious nor imposed from outside life. It does not require a designer assigning significance to events. Nor does it require a conscious observer interpreting them. Meaning emerges because living systems are organized in ways that make some conditions consequential for their continued existence.

A drought becomes meaningful to a plant because water availability affects viability.

A nutrient gradient becomes meaningful to a bacterium because it affects metabolic activity.

A predator becomes meaningful to a deer because it affects the animal's ability to continue living.

In each case, significance emerges from the relationship between the organism and its circumstances.

Importantly, this significance is not merely located in the environment, nor is it located solely within the organism. Biological meaning arises through the interaction between the two. The same environmental condition may be highly significant for one organism and largely irrelevant for another. Water is meaningful to a desert plant in a different way than it is to a fish. A chemical signal that guides one bacterial species may be meaningless to another. Significance depends upon the organization, needs, and activities of the living system involved.

This relational character of biological meaning helps explain why life is so diverse. Different organisms inhabit different worlds of significance because they engage with their environments in different ways. Yet beneath this diversity lies a common pattern: living systems continuously distinguish between conditions that support persistence and conditions that threaten it.

The APS framework therefore proposes that biological meaning is not an accidental by-product of life. It is a natural consequence of the way living systems are organized. Wherever there is ongoing self-maintenance, regulation, adaptation, and viability-oriented activity, there are conditions that matter. Wherever conditions matter, biological meaning emerges.

This does not mean that all forms of meaning are biological. Human meanings associated with art, morality, religion, science, and personal purpose involve additional layers of cognition, language, culture, and reflection. Nevertheless, these more complex forms of meaning do not appear from nowhere. They develop from a deeper biological foundation in which living systems already inhabit a world structured by significance.

The crucial question, then, is how living systems generate and sustain these meaningful relationships with their environments. What allows organisms to distinguish between beneficial and harmful conditions, to regulate themselves in response to change, and to maintain their viability over time?

APS addresses this question through the concept of **biological agency**.

To understand why some things matter to living systems, we must therefore examine the agency through which living systems actively create and maintain meaningful relationships with the world around them.

## Agency and the Origin of Meaning

Biological meaning does not arise simply because living systems exist. It arises because living systems are active participants in their own continued existence.

This is where the APS concept of **biological agency** becomes central.

Throughout this article, we have seen that some conditions matter to living systems while others do not. Water matters to a plant. Nutrients matter to a bacterium. A predator matters to a deer. These conditions are biologically significant because they affect the organism's ability to maintain itself and persist through time.

But significance does not emerge automatically from the environment alone.

A chemical gradient in a pond is not inherently meaningful. Sunlight is not inherently meaningful. A predator is not inherently meaningful. These become meaningful only in relation to living systems whose continued activity is affected by them.

To understand why some things matter, we must therefore understand the nature of the organisms to which they matter.

APS approaches this through the concept of agency.

In its broadest sense, agency refers to the capacity of a living system to initiate, regulate, and coordinate interactions that contribute to its continued viability. Agency is not limited to conscious decision-making, deliberate planning, or reflective thought. Human agency includes these capacities, but biological agency is more fundamental. It refers to the active, self-maintaining character of life itself.

A useful way to see this distinction is to compare living systems with many familiar nonliving systems.

A river flows downhill. A crystal grows. A hurricane maintains a recognizable structure for a period of time. These systems can exhibit impressive forms of organization, yet they do not regulate themselves in ways directed toward their own continued viability. They do not actively maintain boundaries, repair damage, acquire resources, or modify their behaviour in response to changing conditions in order to sustain themselves.

Living systems do.

A bacterium adjusts its movements in response to chemical gradients. A plant alters patterns of growth in response to light and water availability. An animal modifies its behaviour when environmental conditions change. Across the living world, organisms continuously regulate their relationships with their surroundings in ways that support continued functioning.

Agency therefore transforms the relationship between organism and environment.

For a passive object, environmental events simply occur.

For an agent, environmental events acquire significance.

The difference is profound.

A falling temperature is merely a physical change in the environment. For an organism, however, that same change may affect metabolism, resource availability, reproduction, or survival. The environmental event becomes relevant because it influences the organism's ongoing activity.

Agency is what creates this relevance.

Through agency, organisms continuously distinguish between conditions that support viability and conditions that threaten it. They acquire resources, avoid hazards, repair disruptions, regulate internal states, and modify their activities in response to changing circumstances. These processes do not require conscious awareness. They require only that the organism be organized in ways that allow it to maintain itself through active engagement with its environment.

From an APS perspective, this is the origin of biological meaning.

Meaning emerges because living systems are not passive objects undergoing change. They are active systems engaged in maintaining themselves. Their activities establish a distinction between what contributes to continued functioning and what undermines it. In doing so, they generate a world of biological significance.

This idea can be understood through a simple example.

Imagine two identical patches of sunlight striking two different surfaces. One falls on a rock, the other on a plant.

For the rock, the sunlight produces physical effects. The rock may warm slightly, but nothing in the rock's organization depends upon interpreting or responding to the light.

For the plant, the situation is different. Light contributes to photosynthesis, influences growth, affects energy acquisition, and helps regulate developmental processes. The same environmental condition has become biologically meaningful because of the way the plant is organized and because of the activities through which it maintains itself.

The significance does not reside in the sunlight alone.

Nor does it reside solely within the organism.

It emerges through the active relationship between the two.

This principle extends across all scales of life. Organisms continually generate meaningful relationships through their interactions with the environments they inhabit. Different organisms generate different worlds of significance because they possess different forms of organization, different capacities, and different requirements for continued viability.

A cactus and a fish inhabit different worlds of biological meaning. A bee and an oak tree respond to different signals and opportunities. A wolf and a coral reef engage with their surroundings in different ways. Yet all participate in the same fundamental process: they actively distinguish between conditions that support persistence and conditions that threaten it.

Agency therefore provides the missing link between life and meaning.

Biological meaning is not something added to living systems from outside. Nor is it a purely subjective phenomenon waiting for consciousness to appear. Meaning emerges because living systems actively regulate themselves and their relationships with the world. Through agency, environmental differences become biologically relevant. Through agency, significance enters the living world.

This perspective also helps explain why meaning can exist long before language, culture, or reflective thought. The foundations of meaning are already present wherever living systems actively maintain themselves and respond to conditions that affect their viability. Human forms of meaning build upon these foundations, but they do not create them.

Understanding agency therefore reveals why living systems inhabit worlds structured by significance. The next question is how organisms detect, interpret, and respond to these meaningful differences. To answer that question, we must turn to cognition and the processes through which living systems make use of biologically meaningful information.

## Cognition: Interpreting What Matters

If biological meaning explains why some conditions matter to living systems, cognition helps explain how organisms detect, interpret, and respond to those conditions.

The two concepts are closely related, but they are not the same.

Biological meaning concerns significance. It refers to the fact that some conditions contribute to viability while others threaten it. Cognition concerns the processes through which organisms make use of these meaningful differences. Put simply, biological meaning concerns what matters, while cognition concerns how organisms engage with what matters.

This distinction is important because cognition is often associated exclusively with brains, consciousness, or intelligent behaviour. In everyday language, to be cognitive is often taken to mean thinking, reasoning, or solving problems in ways that resemble human mental activity.

APS adopts a broader perspective.

From an APS viewpoint, cognition refers to the processes through which living systems detect relevant conditions, integrate information, modify activity, and respond adaptively to changing circumstances. Cognition is therefore understood as a scale-independent form of adaptive regulation rather than as a capacity restricted to organisms with nervous systems.

This does not mean that all organisms think in the same way. A bacterium, an oak tree, a honeybee, and a human being possess very different structures and capabilities. The claim is not that they share identical mechanisms. Rather, they share a common functional challenge: they must continually engage with biologically meaningful differences in ways that support continued viability.

A simple bacterial example illustrates the principle.

A bacterium moving through a chemical gradient encounters a changing environment. Some chemicals indicate nutrient-rich regions, while others signal harmful conditions. The bacterium does not merely drift passively. It alters its movement in response to these differences. Through sensory and regulatory processes, it effectively distinguishes between conditions that support its continued activity and conditions that do not.

The bacterium is not consciously evaluating alternatives. Nevertheless, it is responding selectively to biologically meaningful information. In APS terms, cognition begins wherever living systems detect and use relevant differences to regulate their interactions with the environment.

Plants provide another instructive example.

Plants continuously monitor and respond to changing conditions. They adjust growth in response to light, alter root development in response to water and nutrient availability, modify physiology in response to temperature, and coordinate defensive responses when damaged or attacked by herbivores. These activities require the integration of information from multiple sources across the organism.

Again, the claim is not that plants possess human-like awareness. Rather, plants demonstrate that cognition need not depend upon brains or nervous systems. They detect meaningful environmental differences, integrate information across time and space, and generate adaptive responses that contribute to continued viability.

In animals, cognition becomes increasingly elaborate.

Many animals learn from experience, remember past events, anticipate future conditions, navigate complex environments, recognize individuals, and modify behaviour in flexible ways. These capacities expand the organism's ability to engage with biologically meaningful information. Instead of responding only to immediate conditions, animals can increasingly draw upon memory, prediction, and learning.

Human cognition extends these capacities still further. Through language, abstraction, imagination, and symbolic thought, humans can reflect on possibilities far removed from immediate experience. We can consider hypothetical futures, construct scientific theories, create legal systems, and contemplate the meaning of life itself.

Despite these enormous differences, APS emphasizes continuity rather than separation.

Across the living world, cognition serves a common role: it helps organisms engage effectively with conditions that matter for their continued activity. The specific mechanisms vary enormously, but the underlying functional challenge remains recognizable. Organisms must detect relevant differences, integrate information, coordinate responses, and maintain viability within changing environments.

This continuity helps explain why APS describes cognition as a functionally equivalent mode of adaptive regulation across multiple forms of life.

Functional equivalence does not mean identical mechanisms. Wings and fins perform different functions, and eyes have evolved many times through different pathways. Likewise, the cognitive processes of bacteria, plants, animals, and humans differ profoundly in their physical implementation. What they share is the capacity to use information in ways that support adaptive engagement with meaningful aspects of their environments.

Seen in this light, cognition is deeply connected to organised persistence.

Living systems persist because they continually regulate themselves in relation to changing circumstances. Such regulation depends upon the ability to detect conditions, evaluate their significance, and modify behaviour or internal activity accordingly. Cognition therefore contributes directly to viability by helping organisms maintain effective relationships with the environments they inhabit.

This relationship can also be understood in temporal terms.

Biological meaning establishes significance in the present. Cognition allows organisms to extend that significance across time. Memory preserves information about past interactions. Anticipatory responses prepare organisms for likely future conditions. Learning allows previous experiences to influence future behaviour. Through these processes, cognition expands the organism's capacity to engage with a meaningful world.

Importantly, cognition does not create biological meaning from nothing.

A nutrient is biologically significant before it is detected. Water affects plant viability whether or not the plant's responses are successful. A predator remains dangerous even if prey fail to recognize it. Cognition operates within a world that is already structured by biological meaning.

At the same time, cognition enriches that world.

The more effectively an organism can detect, integrate, remember, and respond to meaningful differences, the more sophisticated its engagement with the environment can become. Cognition therefore deepens and expands biological meaning without replacing it.

This expansion has important consequences.

As organisms become capable of responding not only to immediate conditions but also to signals, cues, and representations, the living world acquires new forms of significance. A scent can indicate food. A warning call can indicate danger. A visual display can indicate reproductive status. A pattern can stand for something beyond itself.

Meaning is no longer limited to direct interactions with the environment. It begins to flow through signs.

This development marks an important transition in the evolution of meaning. Living systems increasingly engage not only with biologically significant conditions but also with signals that represent those conditions. Understanding this transition requires us to examine semiosis: the processes through which signs, signals, and representations acquire significance within the living world.

## Semiosis: From Signal to Significance

A flower opening in the morning light, a bird responding to a warning call, a plant adjusting its growth in response to changing day length, a bacterium following a chemical gradient—each of these examples involves more than a direct physical interaction with the environment.

They involve information.

More specifically, they involve situations in which one thing comes to stand for, indicate, or signal something else. A scent may indicate food. A shadow may indicate danger. A change in day length may indicate the approach of a new season. A chemical gradient may indicate the location of resources.

This capacity lies at the heart of **semiosis**.

Semiosis refers to the processes through which signs, signals, and representations acquire significance within living systems. It concerns how organisms use information about the world to guide their activities and maintain viable relationships with their environments.

At first glance, semiosis may appear to belong primarily to the human world. Language, writing, mathematics, and symbolic communication are all examples of sign use. Yet semiosis extends far beyond human culture. Wherever organisms respond to something because it indicates something else, sign processes are present in some form.

The key point is that a signal does not become meaningful by itself.

A signal becomes meaningful because it matters to an organism.

A chemical molecule drifting through water is merely a physical event. A scent molecule travelling through the air is merely a physical event. A pattern of light reflected from a distant object is merely a physical event. These become signs only when they participate in the activities of living systems.

This is why semiosis is closely connected to biological meaning.

Biological meaning establishes significance by making some conditions consequential for organised persistence. Semiosis extends this significance by allowing organisms to respond not only to conditions themselves but also to indicators of those conditions.

Consider a grazing animal hearing an alarm call.

The sound itself is not the danger. The sound matters because it indicates the possible presence of a predator. The animal responds not to the predator directly but to a sign that stands for a potentially significant condition.

Similarly, many plants use environmental cues to regulate development. Changes in day length can trigger flowering. Chemical signals released by neighbouring plants can influence defensive responses. The cue itself is not the condition to which the organism ultimately responds. Rather, it provides information about conditions that may affect future viability.

Even bacterial chemotaxis contains a rudimentary form of this principle. Chemical gradients do not merely affect bacterial movement mechanically. They provide information about resource distributions in the surrounding environment. The bacterium's behaviour is organized in relation to what the signal indicates.

In each case, signs allow organisms to engage with the world more effectively than direct reaction alone would permit.

This introduces an important consequence of semiosis.

Semiosis expands the temporal and spatial reach of biological meaning.

Without sign processes, organisms would largely respond only to conditions that are immediately present. Through semiosis, organisms can respond to events that are distant, anticipated, hidden, or only indirectly encountered. A warning call may indicate a predator that has not yet appeared. Seasonal cues may indicate environmental changes that have not yet occurred. A scent trail may indicate food located beyond immediate perception.

Semiosis therefore allows living systems to extend adaptive regulation beyond the here and now.

This expansion becomes increasingly important as cognition becomes more sophisticated.

Simple sign processes allow organisms to respond to immediate cues. More complex forms of semiosis support learning, memory, anticipation, and social communication. Organisms increasingly interact not only with the physical world itself but also with information about the world.

From an APS perspective, semiosis is not an isolated phenomenon layered onto life from above. It emerges naturally from the interaction between biological meaning and cognition.

Biological meaning establishes what matters.

Cognition enables organisms to detect and use meaningful differences.

Semiosis allows meaningful differences to be represented, communicated, and acted upon through signs.

These processes are deeply intertwined. Remove biological meaning and signs lose their significance. Remove cognition and signs cannot be interpreted. Remove semiosis and the ability to engage with indirect information becomes greatly diminished.

Seen this way, semiosis forms a bridge between the biological and human worlds.

In nonhuman organisms, sign processes support adaptive regulation, communication, coordination, and anticipation. In humans, these same processes become elaborated through language, writing, mathematics, art, science, law, religion, and culture. Human symbolic systems are extraordinarily complex, but they build upon capacities that are already present in more fundamental forms throughout the living world.

This continuity is important because it helps explain how human meaning emerges without appearing suddenly or mysteriously.

Language did not create meaning from nothing.

Culture did not create significance from nothing.

Symbolic thought did not create value from nothing.

Rather, each represents a further development of processes through which living systems already engaged with biologically meaningful aspects of their environments.

As semiosis becomes increasingly sophisticated, signs no longer merely indicate food, danger, or opportunities for survival. They come to represent memories, identities, values, beliefs, aspirations, and entire systems of knowledge. Meaning becomes distributed across communities, traditions, institutions, and cultures.

The result is a new domain of significance: the uniquely human world of symbolic meaning.

To understand the meaning of life in its fullest sense, we must therefore move beyond biological meaning, cognition, and semiosis alone. We must examine how human beings transform these biological foundations into the rich worlds of purpose, morality, identity, culture, science, religion, and personal significance that characterize human life.

## Human Meaning: Continuity Without Reduction

By this point, a pattern has begun to emerge.

Biological meaning arises when some conditions become consequential for the viability and organised persistence of living systems. Cognition enables organisms to detect, integrate, remember, and respond to those meaningful differences. Semiosis allows significance to be extended through signs, signals, and representations.

Human meaning builds upon these foundations.

At the same time, it cannot be fully explained by them.

This balance between continuity and difference is central to the APS perspective. Human beings are living organisms and therefore participate in the same fundamental biological processes found throughout the living world. Yet humans also possess distinctive capacities that allow meaning to expand into forms unknown elsewhere in nature.

Understanding human meaning therefore requires avoiding two opposite mistakes.

The first is to treat humans as completely separate from the rest of life, as though meaning suddenly appeared with language, consciousness, or culture. This view risks creating an unnecessary divide between human experience and the biological world from which it emerged.

The second is to reduce all human meaning to survival, reproduction, or other biological processes. This view overlooks the extraordinary complexity of human symbolic life and fails to explain why people devote themselves to activities whose significance often extends far beyond immediate biological concerns.

APS rejects both extremes.

Human meaning emerges from biological meaning, but it is not reducible to it.

The foundation remains biological. Like all organisms, humans inhabit a world in which some conditions matter more than others. We require food, water, shelter, social relationships, and countless forms of environmental support. Our bodies remain living systems engaged in organised persistence. Biological meaning therefore continues to shape human life at every moment.

Yet human cognition and semiosis dramatically expand the scope of significance.

Language is one of the most important developments in this process.

Through language, humans can communicate not only about immediate conditions but also about absent objects, hypothetical possibilities, imagined futures, remembered pasts, and abstract ideas. Words allow experiences to be shared, preserved, compared, criticized, and transformed. Meaning is no longer confined to direct interaction with the environment. It becomes distributed across conversations, stories, traditions, and institutions.

Language also allows meanings to accumulate across generations.

A single organism can learn only so much during its lifetime. Human societies, however, preserve knowledge through speech, writing, education, and cultural transmission. Scientific discoveries, ethical traditions, religious teachings, artistic practices, and historical memories can persist long after the individuals who created them have disappeared.

This introduces another distinctive feature of human meaning: **collective memory**.

Much of what humans find meaningful is inherited rather than individually discovered. Languages, customs, laws, beliefs, and social practices connect individuals to communities extending across both space and time. Human meaning therefore exists not only within individual minds but also within cultural systems that outlive any particular person.

Culture plays a central role in this process.

Culture provides shared frameworks through which people interpret their experiences and understand their place in the world. Different cultures may emphasize different values, traditions, and aspirations, yet all provide systems of significance that help organize human life.

Through culture, meaning becomes social as well as biological.

Humans do not simply respond to environmental conditions. They respond to symbols, norms, expectations, stories, and institutions. Money, governments, universities, religions, legal systems, and nations all depend upon shared symbolic meanings that exist only because people collectively recognize and sustain them.

These symbolic systems vastly expand the range of things that can matter.

A scientific theory matters because of what it explains.

A work of art matters because of what it expresses.

A moral principle matters because of how it guides behaviour.

A religious belief matters because of how it shapes understanding and practice.

None of these forms of significance can be fully captured by reference to immediate biological needs alone.

Morality provides a particularly important example.

Biological meaning concerns conditions that affect viability and organised persistence. Moral meaning concerns questions of right and wrong, justice and responsibility, obligation and care. Although morality emerges within living communities and depends upon cognitive and social capacities that evolved within the biological world, moral questions cannot be reduced simply to questions of survival.

Human beings evaluate actions, establish norms, criticize institutions, and imagine better futures. Moral meaning therefore extends biological significance into domains of reflection, judgement, and collective responsibility.

Art similarly expands the horizons of meaning.

Music, literature, painting, architecture, and performance often serve no immediate survival function. Yet they can profoundly influence how individuals and communities understand themselves. Art allows human beings to explore emotions, identities, possibilities, and experiences that transcend immediate practical concerns.

Science provides another remarkable expression of human meaning.

Scientific inquiry is motivated by curiosity, explanation, and understanding. Through science, humans seek knowledge not merely because it contributes to survival but because understanding itself has become meaningful. Scientific meaning emerges through collective efforts to interpret the world and situate ourselves within it.

Religion represents yet another dimension.

Religious traditions often address questions of ultimate significance, purpose, morality, transcendence, and belonging. APS does not attempt to evaluate the truth of particular religious claims. Instead, it recognizes religion as one of the ways human beings construct and engage with systems of meaning that extend beyond immediate biological concerns.

What unites these diverse forms of human meaning is the capacity for reflection.

Humans do not merely participate in meaningful relationships; they can think about them.

We can ask why something matters.

We can compare different values.

We can revise our goals.

We can reject inherited traditions.

We can imagine alternative futures.

We can create entirely new systems of significance.

This capacity depends upon symbolic thought.

Symbols allow humans to represent possibilities that are not immediately present. Through imagination, people can explore hypothetical worlds, anticipate future outcomes, and construct narratives about who they are and who they wish to become. Meaning therefore becomes increasingly open-ended. It is not simply discovered but also created, negotiated, and transformed.

Personal identity emerges within this symbolic landscape.

Individuals develop life stories, commitments, aspirations, relationships, and projects that contribute to their sense of self. Questions about purpose, fulfilment, vocation, and legacy all arise within this uniquely human domain of meaning.

Yet despite its complexity, human meaning remains connected to its biological foundations.

Without living organisms there would be no cognition.

Without cognition there would be no semiosis.

Without semiosis there would be no language.

Without language there would be no culture.

Without culture there would be no science, religion, morality, art, or personal narratives.

The continuity is real.

But so is the novelty.

Human meaning is therefore best understood as an emergent expansion of biological meaning. It grows from the same fundamental condition that characterizes all life: some things matter. Yet through cognition, symbolic thought, culture, and collective history, human beings transform that condition into extraordinarily rich worlds of significance.

This perspective helps illuminate the relationship between biology and the traditional question of meaning. Human beings are not detached observers asking questions from outside nature. We are living systems whose biological foundations have given rise to language, culture, imagination, and reflection.

The question is therefore no longer simply how humans create meaning.

A deeper question now comes into focus:

If biological meaning is intrinsic to living systems and human meaning emerges from those foundations, does life itself possess meaning?

## Does Life Have Meaning?

Having explored biological meaning, cognition, semiosis, and human meaning, we can now return to a question that has been quietly guiding the discussion from the beginning:

**Does life have meaning?**

At first glance, the answer may seem to depend entirely on philosophy, religion, or personal belief. Some traditions answer with a confident yes, locating meaning in divine purpose, moral duty, human flourishing, or spiritual fulfilment. Others argue that life has no inherent meaning and that individuals must create meaning for themselves.

APS approaches the question from a different starting point.

Rather than asking whether life possesses a predetermined purpose or ultimate destination, APS asks whether meaning is already present within the living world itself.

From this perspective, the answer is yes.

Life has meaning because living systems generate relationships of significance.

Throughout this article, we have seen that organisms are not passive objects drifting through a meaningless environment. They actively maintain themselves, regulate their interactions, respond to changing conditions, and engage with circumstances that affect their continued viability. In doing so, they create a distinction between what matters and what does not matter.

This distinction is the foundation of biological meaning.

For a plant, water matters.

For a bacterium, nutrient availability matters.

For an animal, food, shelter, and danger matter.

These forms of significance are not imposed from outside life. They emerge from the organization and activities of living systems themselves.

In this sense, life is not indifferent to its own continuation.

Living systems continuously generate conditions of relevance. They distinguish between favourable and unfavourable circumstances, beneficial and harmful interactions, opportunities and threats. Through agency, cognition, and semiosis, organisms inhabit worlds structured by significance rather than mere physical change.

This does not mean that life possesses a single universal purpose.

Indeed, one of the central themes of APS is that meaning and purpose should not be confused.

To say that life has meaning is not to claim that every organism exists for the same reason, pursues the same goal, or contributes to a predetermined cosmic plan. Biological meaning does not require a final destination toward which all life is moving toward. Living systems exhibit directionality because they are organized around maintaining viability and organised persistence, not because they are following a script written in advance.

This distinction is important because questions about meaning are often transformed into questions about purpose.

A person may ask whether life has meaning and receive an answer about life's purpose. Yet the two concepts are not identical.

**Meaning** concerns significance: what matters and why.

**Purpose** concerns what something is for or what end it serves.

**Value** concerns what is regarded as good, desirable, or worth pursuing.

**Significance** concerns the importance or consequences of something within a particular context.

These ideas frequently overlap, but they need not coincide. Something may be meaningful without serving a single overarching purpose. A scientific discovery may be meaningful because it transforms understanding. A friendship may be meaningful because it shapes a life. A work of art may be meaningful because of what it expresses. In each case, significance exists without requiring a universal purpose shared by all things.

APS approaches life in a similar way.

The claim is not that life possesses one ultimate goal. The claim is that life generates significance through the activities of living systems themselves. Meaning emerges because organisms continuously establish relationships in which some conditions matter more than others.

This perspective naturalizes meaning without eliminating it.

In many discussions, only two options seem available. Either meaning is imposed from outside nature by some transcendent source, or meaning is regarded as a purely subjective invention existing only in individual minds. APS suggests a different possibility.

Meaning can emerge as a natural feature of living systems.

The emergence of biological meaning does not require an external designer assigning significance to events. Nor does it require organisms to consciously invent significance where none exists. Meaning arises because living systems actively maintain themselves in relation to their environments. Their activities create distinctions between conditions that support viability and conditions that undermine it.

Agency plays a central role in this process.

Organisms are not passive recipients of environmental influences. They regulate themselves, acquire resources, avoid hazards, repair disruptions, and adapt to changing circumstances. Through these activities, they establish relationships of relevance and significance. Agency transforms environmental differences into biologically meaningful conditions.

Organised persistence provides the broader context.

Living systems endure through continuous processes of maintenance and renewal. Because they persist through time, future conditions matter. Resources matter. Damage matters. Opportunities matter. The significance of these conditions arises from their relationship to the organism's ongoing activity.

Cognition extends this significance.

Organisms detect meaningful differences, integrate information, learn from experience, and modify behaviour accordingly. Cognition allows living systems to engage with significance more effectively and across broader spans of space and time.

Semiosis expands the process still further.

Through signs and signals, organisms can respond not only to immediate conditions but also to indicators of those conditions. Meaning becomes distributed through communication, representation, anticipation, and interpretation. In humans, this process reaches extraordinary levels of complexity through language, culture, science, morality, and symbolic thought.

Seen together, agency, organised persistence, cognition, and semiosis form an integrated account of how meaning emerges and develops within the living world.

At this point, an important objection often arises.

If meaning emerges from living systems, does that not make it merely subjective?

The concern is understandable. If meaning is not imposed from outside life, it may appear to depend entirely upon individual perspectives or preferences.

APS argues that this conclusion does not follow.

Biological meaning is not purely objective in the sense of existing independently of living systems. Nutrients matter to a bacterium because of the kind of organism it is. Water matters to a plant because of the way the plant is organized. Significance always involves a relationship between an organism and its circumstances.

Yet biological meaning is not purely subjective either.

A plant cannot simply decide that water no longer matters. A deer cannot choose to make predation irrelevant. The significance of these conditions is constrained by the biological realities of the organisms involved. Meaning emerges through real relationships between living systems and the environments within which they operate.

APS therefore understands meaning as fundamentally **relational**.

Meaning exists neither entirely in the world nor entirely in the organism. It arises through the interaction between them.

This relational perspective helps explain both the diversity and the unity of meaning in the living world.

Different organisms inhabit different worlds of significance because they possess different forms of organization, different capacities, and different requirements for viability. Yet all living systems participate in the same general process through which environmental differences become meaningful through their relationship to ongoing biological activity.

Human meaning represents the most elaborate expression of this process, but not an exception to it.

The values, purposes, identities, beliefs, and aspirations that shape human lives emerge within a broader biological context in which significance is already present. Human beings inherit a world structured by meaning before they begin to reflect upon it. Through culture, language, and symbolic thought, they transform and expand that inheritance into increasingly complex forms.

This perspective allows APS to answer the question "Does life have meaning?" in a distinctive way.

Life has meaning not because meaning is imposed upon it from outside, nor because meaning exists only in human minds. Life has meaning because living systems continuously generate relationships of significance through their activities, interactions, and efforts to maintain themselves within changing environments.

Meaning is therefore neither an illusion nor an external gift.

It is a natural consequence of life itself.

This conclusion brings us back to the question with which the article began. If meaning emerges wherever living systems create and sustain relationships of significance, and if human meaning represents the most reflective and symbolically elaborated expression of this process, then we are finally in a position to ask:

**What is the meaning of life?**

## What Is the Meaning of Life?

We can now return to the question that began this article:

**What is the meaning of life?**

For centuries, people have sought a single answer. Philosophers have proposed competing theories. Religious traditions have offered different visions of purpose and significance. Scientists, psychologists, artists, and writers have all contributed their own perspectives. Yet despite these efforts, no universally accepted answer has emerged.

Part of the difficulty lies in the question itself.

As we have seen, the phrase *meaning of life* can refer to several different things. It may refer to purpose, value, significance, morality, fulfilment, identity, or belonging. Different answers often address different aspects of the question.

APS approaches the problem from a different direction.

Rather than asking what purpose life serves, APS asks how meaning becomes possible in the first place.

This shift changes the discussion.

Instead of searching for a hidden message, predetermined destination, or universal objective, APS examines the processes through which significance emerges within the living world. The answer it offers is therefore not a single slogan, doctrine, or commandment. It is an explanation of how living systems come to inhabit worlds in which things matter.

The story begins with biological meaning.

Living systems are not passive objects carried along by physical events. They actively maintain themselves, regulate their interactions, acquire resources, respond to challenges, and sustain their organization through time. Because organisms are engaged in organised persistence, some conditions become consequential for their continued activity. Water matters to a plant. Nutrients matter to a bacterium. Predators matter to a deer.

Meaning emerges because living systems create distinctions between conditions that support viability and conditions that threaten it.

Agency is central to this process.

Through biological agency, organisms actively participate in their own continuation. They do not simply undergo change; they regulate and coordinate interactions that help sustain functional integrity under changing conditions. Agency transforms environmental differences into relationships of significance. It creates a world in which some outcomes matter more than others.

Cognition extends these meaningful relationships.

Living systems detect, integrate, remember, and respond to biologically significant differences. They learn from experience, anticipate future conditions, and modify behaviour in adaptive ways. Cognition therefore expands the scope of biological meaning and allows organisms to engage with increasingly complex environments.

Semiosis expands it further.

Through signs, signals, and representations, organisms can respond not only to immediate conditions but also to indicators of those conditions. Meaning becomes distributed through communication, anticipation, and interpretation. In humans, semiosis develops into language, symbolic thought, and culture.

This gives rise to human meaning.

Human beings inherit the biological foundations of significance while extending them through reflection, imagination, memory, morality, science, religion, art, and social life. Human meaning is therefore both continuous with and distinct from biological meaning. It emerges from the same living foundations while developing forms of significance that cannot be reduced to immediate biological concerns.

From an APS perspective, the meaning of life is not a fixed purpose imposed from outside nature. Nor is it merely a subjective invention existing only in individual minds.

Meaning is a natural consequence of life's organization.

Wherever living systems actively maintain themselves, distinctions emerge between what supports continued activity and what undermines it. These distinctions create relationships of significance. Through cognition and semiosis, those relationships become increasingly rich and flexible. Through language and culture, they become the basis for the symbolic worlds inhabited by human beings.

The meaning of life is therefore not something that appears only when humans ask philosophical questions.

Nor is it something waiting to be discovered as a final answer hidden within the universe.

Meaning is an ongoing feature of living systems themselves.

Life continuously creates, interprets, and responds to significance.

This perspective also helps reconcile two intuitions that often appear to conflict.

On the one hand, meaning feels deeply personal. Individuals pursue different goals, values, relationships, and aspirations. No single human purpose adequately captures the diversity of meaningful lives.

On the other hand, meaning does not seem entirely arbitrary. Some experiences genuinely matter. Some losses are genuinely significant. Some achievements, relationships, and commitments carry enduring importance.

APS explains this tension by locating human meaning within a broader biological framework. Meaning is personal because individuals inhabit unique histories, relationships, and cultural contexts. Yet meaning is not arbitrary because it emerges from real relationships between living systems and the conditions that shape their continued activity and flourishing.

The search for meaning therefore does not begin outside life.

It begins within life.

Every organism inhabits a world in which some things matter more than others. Human beings inherit this condition and transform it through language, culture, imagination, and reflection. We create stories, institutions, moral systems, scientific theories, artistic traditions, and personal identities that give shape to our lives. These are not departures from meaning but extensions of a process already present within the living world.

The question "What is the meaning of life?" therefore has no single answer that applies equally to every individual, culture, or circumstance.

Yet APS suggests that there is a common foundation beneath the diversity of human meanings.

Life is meaningful because living systems generate and sustain relationships of significance. Through agency, cognition, and semiosis, organisms create worlds in which conditions matter. Human beings extend these biological foundations into increasingly complex forms of understanding, value, and purpose.

In this sense, the meaning of life is not a destination to be reached or a message to be decoded.

It is the ongoing process through which living systems make the world matter.

Life does not merely possess meaning.

Life is the source from which meaning emerges.
