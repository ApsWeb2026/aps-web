## Introduction — Persistence Through Change

Living systems persist while continually changing.

Their materials turn over, physiological conditions fluctuate, developmental processes alter their form, and their relations with their surroundings change over time. Biological persistence therefore cannot generally mean preservation of an identical state.

Some forms of regulation concern conditions that must remain within viable ranges despite disturbance. Others alter regulatory activity as circumstances or anticipated demands change. Still others concern the maintenance, coordination, or recovery of an unfolding biological course.

**Homeorhesis** addresses this third explanatory problem.

Historically associated especially with Waddington's account of development, homeorhesis directs attention from the maintenance of a state towards the robustness of an organised trajectory. Later physiological uses extended the concept to coordinated changes through which an organism enters or maintains changing physiological conditions.

APS retains this trajectory-centred insight while restricting its scope. Homeorhesis is not simply change, development, adaptation, or persistence through time. It applies where an unfolding biological trajectory is itself regulated: where its organisation is maintained, coordinated, or recovered despite perturbation.

Homeorhesis is therefore a specific form of **trajectory regulation**. It can contribute to organised persistence, but it is not identical with organised persistence and is not required for every case of biological persistence.

**Where This Article Fits**

*Within APS, homeorhesis belongs primarily to the domain of **developmental organisation**, while also having legitimate physiological and broader organisational applications.*

*Its explanatory setting is wider than homeorhesis itself.*

**Viability** *provides the biological reference relative to which regulatory outcomes matter. **Organised persistence** concerns the continuity of living organisation through change. Some cases of organised persistence depend upon processes whose organisation extends across time in such a way that an unfolding course, rather than only a present condition, becomes explanatorily important. Where that course is maintained, coordinated, or recovered through regulation, homeorhetic description may be warranted.*

*Homeorhesis therefore occupies a more specific explanatory position than organised persistence.*

*It must also be distinguished from neighbouring APS concepts. **Biological agency** concerns viability-oriented activity; homeorhesis can describe one temporal-regulatory pattern of such activity but is not equivalent to agency. **Development** frequently involves temporally organised trajectories, but development as such is not necessarily homeorhetic. **Adaptation** has several biological uses and, when it refers to evolutionary adaptation, must not be treated as a consequence or necessary expression of homeorhesis.*

*Homeostasis and allostasis are especially important neighbouring concepts. They overlap with homeorhesis in biological regulation, but they make different regulatory features salient. None should be treated as a more advanced organisational stage than another.*

## From Homeostasis to Homeorhesis

Homeostasis is traditionally associated with the maintenance of biologically important conditions despite perturbation. It is sometimes described too simply as the preservation of fixed internal values.

That interpretation is misleading.

Homeostatic variables fluctuate, regulatory targets can be ranges rather than single values, and the organisation maintaining them can itself be dynamic. Contemporary analysis of homeostatic control reinforces this point: feedback regulation need not hold a controlled variable literally constant.

The distinction between homeostasis and homeorhesis therefore cannot be reduced to a contrast between a static organism and a changing one. Both concern dynamic biological regulation.

Waddington introduced homeorhetic thinking in the context of development. His epigenetic landscape represented development as an unfolding course whose outcome could remain robust despite perturbation. The relevant contrast was not simply **state versus change**, but regulation directed towards the maintenance of a course through change.

This gives homeorhesis its distinctive explanatory focus.

Later physiological applications broadened that focus. Bauman and Currie's analysis of pregnancy and lactation, for example, described coordinated metabolic changes across tissues as homeorhetic regulation associated with a changing physiological course. This use extends beyond embryological development while retaining the central idea that what is organised is not merely a condition at one moment but a temporally extended pattern of change.

APS adopts this broader possibility cautiously. It does not define every dynamic regulatory process as homeorhetic. Temporal change is ubiquitous in biology; homeorhesis becomes explanatorily useful only where there is evidence that an unfolding course is itself maintained, coordinated, or recovered.

## Homeostasis, Allostasis, and Homeorhesis

Homeostasis, allostasis, and homeorhesis belong to overlapping histories of biological regulation. Their boundaries are not universally agreed, and they should not be forced into an artificial hierarchy.

A useful distinction can nevertheless be made by asking what each concept principally brings into explanatory focus.

| Concept | Principal explanatory question | Regulatory emphasis | Temporal emphasis | APS treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Homeostasis | How are viable conditions maintained despite perturbation? | Regulation of relevant variables, relations, or conditions within viable ranges | Continuing regulation of present conditions through ongoing dynamics | A regulatory description centred on maintenance of viable conditions |
| Allostasis | How is regulation adjusted as circumstances or demands change? | Flexible or anticipatory alteration of regulatory activity | Changing present or anticipated demands | A related and partly overlapping description centred on adjustment of regulation |
| Homeorhesis | How is an unfolding biological course maintained, coordinated, or recovered? | Regulation of an organised trajectory | Developmental, physiological, or other temporally extended course | A specific description of trajectory regulation where such regulation is warranted |

*Table 1. Homeostasis, allostasis, and homeorhesis distinguished by their principal explanatory emphases. These are not mutually exclusive mechanisms, successive stages of biological organisation, or an exhaustive taxonomy of biological regulation.*

The distinction is therefore one of **explanatory emphasis**, not a partition of living systems into three exclusive regulatory kinds.

Homeostasis need not imply literal constancy. Allostasis need not be reduced to a changed homeostatic set point. Homeorhesis does not mean dynamic regulation in general.

A biological process may legitimately be described in more than one of these ways if different regulatory dependencies are being examined. A physiological transition, for example, might involve homeostatic control of particular variables, allostatic alteration of regulatory deployment, and homeorhetic coordination of an extended physiological trajectory.

The concepts need not compete for ownership of the same phenomenon.

This is especially important for APS. The framework does not require a universal taxonomy in which every regulatory event must first be classified as homeostatic, allostatic, or homeorhetic. The explanatory question is instead which dependencies must be identified to account for the biological phenomenon under investigation.

## Explanatory Architecture

APS places homeorhesis within a sequence of increasing explanatory specification:

**Viability → Organised Persistence → Temporally Extended Organisation → Trajectory Regulation → Homeorhesis**

Each term restricts the explanatory target more precisely.

### Viability

Living organisation persists only within conditions compatible with its continued organisation. Viability therefore supplies the normative reference for APS accounts of regulation: some changes support continued organisation while others undermine it.

Homeorhesis does not establish this normativity. It presupposes it.

### Organised Persistence

APS treats biological persistence as continuity of organisation through material and structural change, not as the indefinite preservation of an identical physical state.

Organised persistence is consequently broader than homeorhesis. Living organisation may persist through many forms of regulation and reconstruction that do not warrant homeorhetic description.

### Temporally Extended Organisation

Some biological phenomena cannot be adequately characterised by examining conditions at a single moment. Their organisation partly consists in an unfolding course: development, coordinated physiological transition, regeneration, maturation, or other temporally extended processes.

But temporal extension alone does not establish homeorhesis. A sequence can unfold through time without its trajectory being regulated.

### Trajectory Regulation

The decisive additional requirement is evidence that the unfolding course is itself subject to regulation.

This may be expressed through robustness against perturbation, coordinated alteration among component processes, recovery of a disrupted course, or other dependencies showing that continuation of the trajectory is not merely a succession of events.

Trajectory regulation is therefore narrower than temporally extended organisation.

### Homeorhesis

Homeorhesis is the description APS applies where such trajectory-regulating organisation is the phenomenon requiring explanation.

The dependency is therefore asymmetric:

**Homeorhesis presupposes temporally organised persistence, but organised persistence does not presuppose homeorhesis.**

This prevents homeorhesis from becoming another name for the continuity of life through change.

## What Makes a Trajectory Homeorhetic?

Not every biological trajectory is homeorhetic.

A developmental sequence, physiological transition, or behavioural progression can be described retrospectively as a trajectory simply because one condition followed another. That description alone establishes no regulatory organisation.

A stronger claim requires evidence.

A trajectory may warrant homeorhetic description where perturbation reveals robustness of the course, where deviations are compensated or recovered, or where changing component processes are coordinated in ways that sustain the unfolding organisation. Different biological cases may exhibit these properties differently.

The central issue is therefore not whether a process has a beginning and an end, nor whether it changes predictably. It is whether the organisation of the process contributes causally to maintaining, coordinating, or recovering the course.

This also distinguishes **trajectory regulation** from mere temporal regularity.

A repeated sequence can be regular without being regulated. Likewise, a trajectory can reach a similar endpoint after perturbation for reasons that do not establish a regulatory dependency. Claims of homeorhesis therefore require biological evidence appropriate to the system concerned.

**Robustness** should be used with the same care. Persistence despite perturbation can provide evidence of regulation, but robustness is not automatically homeorhesis. What matters is whether the relevant robustness concerns the organisation of an unfolding course.

Similarly, **recovery** should not be assumed whenever a trajectory continues after disturbance. Recovery requires evidence that the disrupted course was restored or reorganised in a way that supports continuation of the relevant biological trajectory.

Homeorhesis is consequently an explanatory claim about organised temporal regulation, not a synonym for temporal continuity.

## Homeorhesis and Biological Agency

APS defines biological agency as viability-oriented activity through which living organisation contributes to sustaining the conditions of its persistence.

Homeorhetic regulation can be one temporal pattern of that activity.

Where an organism regulates an unfolding developmental or physiological trajectory relative to viability, the relevant regulatory processes are expressions of biological agency. The system is not merely undergoing change; its organised activity contributes to maintaining or recovering a viable course.

This relationship must not be reversed.

Biological agency does not depend upon homeorhesis. Viability-oriented activity includes forms of regulation that need not involve the maintenance of an extended trajectory. Homeostatic regulation, repair, environmental response, and other activities may contribute to persistence without warranting homeorhetic description.

Nor is homeorhesis a separate causal power added to agency.

Within APS, Agency, Process, and Scale are analytic projections of one biological organisation. Describing a process as homeorhetic identifies a temporal-regulatory pattern within that organisation. Describing it in terms of agency identifies the viability-oriented activity expressed through the organisation.

The two descriptions answer different but related explanatory questions.

Homeorhesis can therefore describe **how some viability-oriented activity is organised across time**, without becoming either the definition or a necessary condition of biological agency.

## Homeorhesis, Development, and Plasticity

Development remains the historically strongest domain for homeorhesis.

Waddington's use of the concept was connected with the capacity of development to follow robust trajectories despite perturbation. The epigenetic landscape provided a way of representing developmental courses whose organisation could resist or compensate for disturbance.

That historical connection remains important because it prevents homeorhesis from being detached from the explanatory problem that originally gave it force.

Nevertheless:

**development is not synonymous with homeorhesis.**

Development necessarily unfolds through time, but temporal unfolding alone does not demonstrate trajectory regulation. A homeorhetic interpretation requires evidence that developmental organisation maintains, coordinates, or recovers the relevant course.

The same restriction applies to developmental plasticity.

Plasticity concerns the capacity for development or phenotype to vary in relation to conditions. Such variation can itself be organised and regulated, but plasticity should not automatically be described as homeorhetic. A plastic response may alter the developmental trajectory rather than restore a previously specified one, and whether the resulting course is homeorhetically regulated is an empirical question.

This point is particularly important because homeorhesis should not imply rigid return to a predetermined developmental path.

Trajectory regulation can involve modification as well as recovery. What persists may be an organised relation among changing processes rather than an invariant sequence of states.

The relevant question is therefore not:

**Did the organism return to exactly the same path?**

It is:

**Does the evidence show organised regulation of the unfolding course relative to continued viable organisation?**

That formulation preserves Waddington's insight while avoiding an overly fixed conception of developmental trajectories.

## Homeorhesis and Adaptation

The relationship between homeorhesis and adaptation requires particular care because **adaptation** is used for different biological phenomena.

At the organismic scale, regulatory adjustment can alter physiology or behaviour in response to changing conditions. Developmental plasticity can generate different phenotypic outcomes under different environmental circumstances. These processes may contribute to continued viability, and some may involve trajectory regulation.

Neither should automatically be identified with evolutionary adaptation.

In evolutionary biology, adaptation concerns historically produced characteristics of populations or lineages associated with differential persistence and reproduction across generations. That explanatory problem involves inheritance, variation, fitness, and natural selection. It cannot be derived from the homeorhetic regulation of an individual biological trajectory.

APS therefore does not place homeorhesis in a dependency such as:

**Homeorhesis → Adaptation → Persistence**

nor does it claim that homeorhesis provides the temporal mode within which evolutionary adaptation occurs.

A homeorhetic process may itself be an evolved feature. Developmental or physiological trajectory regulation may influence the phenotypic variation exposed to selection. Plastic responses can also participate in evolutionary dynamics. But these are specific relations requiring case-by-case investigation.

Homeorhesis does not explain evolutionary adaptation merely because both involve change through time.

This distinction also prevents a second error: treating homeorhesis as a mechanism that protects adaptive change from organisational disruption. Whether a biological change contributes to viability, whether it is developmentally regulated, and whether it constitutes an evolutionary adaptation are separate explanatory questions.

Their relations must be demonstrated rather than inferred from terminology.

## Homeorhesis and Organised Persistence

Homeorhesis becomes most useful within APS when its relation to organised persistence is stated precisely.

Living systems persist through change. But that general fact does not imply that every instance of organised persistence is trajectory-regulated.

Some persistence depends primarily upon maintaining relevant conditions within viable ranges. Some involves repair or replacement. Some involves reorganisation in response to environmental conditions. Some depends upon developmental or physiological processes whose extended course is itself regulated.

It is in this last class of cases that homeorhesis becomes especially informative.

Homeorhetic regulation can therefore be **one way in which organised persistence is realised where persistence depends upon maintaining, coordinating, or recovering an organised trajectory through change**.

This formulation preserves the explanatory contribution of homeorhesis without allowing it to absorb the more general APS concept.

Organised persistence answers the broader question:

**How does living organisation continue through change?**

Homeorhesis asks a more specific question:

**Where that continuity depends upon an unfolding biological course, how is the course itself maintained, coordinated, or recovered?**

The distinction matters because APS seeks explanatory dependencies rather than redescriptions.

Calling every case of biological persistence homeorhetic would add no explanatory discrimination. The concept becomes useful only when it identifies a specific regulatory organisation that can be distinguished from other ways in which living systems persist.

## Why Homeorhesis Matters

Homeorhesis matters because biological explanation can be distorted when regulation is considered only in terms of conditions at individual moments.

Some biological organisations persist precisely by changing in coordinated ways.

Development provides the clearest example. The relevant biological achievement is not necessarily preservation of the organism in its present condition. Successful development requires departure from that condition while retaining sufficient organisation for a viable course to continue.

Physiological transitions can have a similar structure. Pregnancy, lactation, maturation, recovery, and other temporally extended processes may require coordinated changes rather than preservation of previous regulatory conditions.

Homeorhesis provides a vocabulary for investigating such cases.

Its value, however, depends upon restraint. If homeorhesis is expanded to mean all dynamic regulation, all development, all adaptation, or all persistence through time, it ceases to discriminate among explanatory problems.

APS therefore uses the concept only where three conditions are satisfied:

1. there is a biologically relevant **temporally extended course**;
2. evidence supports **regulation, coordination, robustness, or recovery of that course**; and
3. the trajectory-regulating organisation contributes to **viability-oriented organised persistence**.

These conditions do not establish a new universal principle of life. They identify the circumstances under which homeorhetic description earns an explanatory role.

## Conclusion

Homeorhesis draws attention to an important feature of biological organisation: living continuity can sometimes depend upon regulating change rather than preserving a present condition.

Its historical origin in developmental biology remains central. Waddington used homeorhetic thinking to characterise the robustness of developmental trajectories. Later physiological applications showed that the concept can also illuminate coordinated biological transitions beyond development.

Homeostasis, allostasis, and homeorhesis consequently offer related but partly overlapping perspectives on regulation. Homeostasis foregrounds the maintenance of viable conditions; allostasis foregrounds adjustment of regulation as demands change; homeorhesis foregrounds regulation of an unfolding course. These distinctions are explanatory emphases, not mutually exclusive mechanisms or stages of biological organisation.

APS places homeorhesis within a more general explanatory architecture:

**Viability → Organised Persistence → Temporally Extended Organisation → Trajectory Regulation → Homeorhesis**

This sequence does not make homeorhesis the culmination of biological organisation. It progressively specifies the circumstances under which the concept is warranted.

Homeorhesis can therefore contribute to explaining how some living systems persist through organised change. It is not organised persistence itself, not the universal temporal mode of life, and not a necessary dependency of agency, development, adaptation, or evolutionary change.

Its explanatory value lies precisely in that narrower role.
