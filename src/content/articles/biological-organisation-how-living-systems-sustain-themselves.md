> **Where this article fits:** This article explains biological organisation as the organisation through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence. It develops the relationship among viability, constraint closure, regulation, organised persistence, and biological agency, while treating Agency, Process, and Scale as complementary analytic projections of the same biologically real organisation. Its central concern is biological organisation as a target of explanation rather than as a replacement for established biological explanations.

## 1. The Problem of Biological Continuity

Living systems persist while undergoing continual change. Their molecular constituents turn over, metabolic activities fluctuate, structures are repaired and replaced, and their conditions of existence vary from moment to moment. Growth and physiological adjustment alter the system itself, sometimes substantially. Yet through these changes an organism can remain a continuing living system. What persists, therefore, cannot simply be identified with the continued presence of the same material constituents or with the preservation of an unchanged physical configuration.

This problem becomes clearer when living systems are viewed as processes rather than as static objects. Process-oriented approaches in biology have emphasised that organisms depend upon continuing activity for their existence: many structures that appear stable at one timescale are maintained only through processes of production, transformation, exchange, and replacement operating at others (Nicholson & Dupré 2018). This does not make biological structure unimportant. Cells have membranes, organisms have anatomies, and biological functions depend upon highly specific material arrangements. Rather, the persistence of those structures itself often requires continuing activity. A structural description can tell us what is present at a particular time without, by itself, explaining how the organisation represented by that structure continues through change.

The explanatory question therefore extends beyond what a living system is made of or which mechanisms produce a particular outcome. We can also ask what makes the continuing organisation of those components and mechanisms possible. Contemporary work on biological organisation treats this as a legitimate problem in its own right: organisation can be something to be explained as well as something invoked in explaining biological phenomena (Mossio 2023). The central problem of this article is one of biological continuity: how does a living system maintain the organisation through which it remains capable of continuing as a living system when neither its material composition nor many of its particular states remain unchanged?

This question does not compete with explanations of molecular interactions, biochemical pathways, physiological mechanisms, or physical structures. Such explanations identify indispensable features of living systems. The further question concerns how those features are related and coordinated in the continuing organisation of the system to which they belong. Biological persistence is therefore not adequately characterised by duration alone. What requires explanation is the organisation through which continuity is achieved despite change.

## 2. Biological Organisation as a Target of Explanation

Organisation is sometimes used loosely to mean arrangement: components occupy particular positions, form a structure, or stand in describable relations to one another. Biological organisation involves more than this. A living system depends upon activities whose effects contribute to maintaining the conditions under which those activities can continue. Metabolism supplies and transforms materials; membranes regulate exchanges; regulatory processes alter activity as conditions change; repair and replacement counter deterioration. These processes differ enormously among organisms, but they illustrate a common explanatory point. The organisation of a living system concerns not only which parts and processes are present, but how their activities depend upon and contribute to the continuing operation of the system.

[[box:organisation-is-not-structure]]

The organisational approach to biological autonomy gives this idea a more precise form through the concept of constraints. A constraint limits or channels a process while remaining sufficiently stable relative to that process to influence how it proceeds. In living systems, such constraints are not necessarily permanent structures. They may themselves be produced, maintained, repaired, or replaced by other processes within the system. What matters is the organisation of dependencies through which constraints contribute to maintaining the conditions of their own continued operation (Moreno & Mossio 2015). Organisation in this sense is more than spatial arrangement: it identifies relations through which biological activities become mutually dependent.

Constraint closure captures a particularly important feature of this organisation. In an organisationally closed system, constraints depend upon other constraints and processes within the system for the conditions of their continued existence, while themselves contributing to the maintenance of that system. Closure does not mean physical isolation. Living systems remain thermodynamically open, continually exchanging matter and energy with their surroundings, nor need every process occurring within an organism be produced entirely from within. Closure identifies an organisation of dependence through which the system participates in maintaining the conditions that make its own continued activity possible (Moreno & Mossio 2015).

This perspective also clarifies the relation between organisational and mechanistic explanation. Biological mechanisms remain materially indispensable, while organisational analysis asks how mechanisms and constraints are coordinated within the continuing system. Appealing to organisation cannot substitute for specifying the material activities through which it is realised. Work connecting mechanistic and organisational approaches shows how control and regulation can link these explanatory concerns (Bich & Bechtel 2022). Organisation has explanatory value when it identifies such relations, not when it merely redescribes a system as an integrated whole.

Constraint closure nevertheless does not exhaust what must be explained about living organisation. It identifies organisational dependencies without fully specifying why some changes remain compatible with continuation whereas others undermine it. Living organisation operates under conditions in which its own continuation is at stake. Understanding that difference requires relating organisation to viability.

## 3. Viability, Regulation, and the Organisation of Persistence

A biological system persists under conditions in which changes can affect its capacity to continue functioning. Some can be accommodated, compensated for, or repaired; others progressively undermine the organisation on which continued activity depends. Living organisation must therefore be understood not only through relations of organisational dependence but also through the conditions under which that organisation remains viable.

This viability-sensitive character of biological organisation has precedents in work on autonomy and adaptivity. Di Paolo distinguishes mere self-production from the capacity of a system to regulate its activity relative to the consequences of changing conditions for its continued existence (Di Paolo 2005). Organisational approaches likewise connect biological function and normativity to contributions made within the current organisation of a self-maintaining system (Moreno & Mossio 2015; Mossio, Saborido & Moreno 2009). These approaches differ in important respects, but they converge on a point that matters here: biological organisation is not indifferent to every possible state through which a system might pass.

That asymmetry provides the limited sense of biological normativity required here. Conditions that sustain or restore organised functioning are not equivalent, for the system, to conditions that progressively compromise it. This requires neither conscious judgement nor intention, preference, foresight, or an explicit representation of survival as a goal. The distinction arises from the organisation itself: some changes remain compatible with continued viable functioning while others erode the conditions on which that functioning depends. APS captures this as viability-relative asymmetry. The formulation is APS-specific, while the broader attempt to ground biological normativity in present organisational relations is established in the organisational literature (Mossio, Saborido & Moreno 2009).

The practical significance of this asymmetry becomes visible in regulation. Living systems do not merely undergo perturbations; their activities can change in ways that alter how perturbations affect the organisation as a whole. Biological regulation can modify constitutive processes from within the system, adjusting activity in response to changing internal or external conditions (Bich et al. 2016). Such regulation can involve changes in rates, interactions, constraints, resource allocation, or other materially realised relations. The details vary, but the general point is that maintaining organisation is itself organised activity.

That activity is necessarily temporal. Metabolic replacement, physiological compensation, repair after damage, re-establishment of disturbed relations, and longer-term reconstruction require changes whose effects unfold over time. What matters is not whether each state resembles the one before it, but whether sequences of change continue to sustain or restore the organisational conditions required for further functioning. Regulation links present activity to temporally extended continuity, allowing organisation to be maintained not through the elimination of change but through changes that themselves participate in preserving the possibility of continuation.

Biological persistence should therefore not be equated with mere duration. Many non-living structures can endure without actively maintaining the conditions of their continuation. Living systems persist through ongoing organisation that compensates for disruption, replaces deteriorating components, reorganises relations, and responds to altered conditions. Continuity is continually achieved rather than passively retained. In APS, organised persistence names this temporally extended continuity of living organisation through change.

Organised persistence does not imply preservation of the same structure, state, or pattern, nor does it require every perturbation to be successfully corrected. Living systems can fail, deteriorate, or cease to remain viable. Where biological continuity is maintained, however, it depends upon organisation capable of sustaining and, when necessary, re-establishing the conditions of its own operation. Constraint closure identifies relevant organisational dependencies; viability distinguishes changes that support continuation from those that undermine it; regulation and reconstruction materially enact that distinction over time.

Biological organisation can accordingly be characterised as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation whose continuity is actively sustained through regulation and reconstruction. Its activity, temporal continuity, and distribution across spatial and temporal extents can then be distinguished analytically without fragmenting that organisation into separate realities.

## 4. Agency, Process, and Scale as Analytic Projections

The Agency–Process–Scale framework makes these complementary perspectives explicit (Spencer 2026). They distinguish aspects of the same continuing biological organisation rather than identifying separate components or kinds of biological reality.

Agency concerns what living systems do. A living system does not merely possess an organisation; its organisation is enacted in activity. Metabolism, regulation, repair, growth, movement, and other biological activities contribute in different ways to maintaining or re-establishing the conditions under which the system can continue. Biological agency is therefore viability-oriented organisational activity. It need not involve cognition, intention, deliberation, or conscious choice. Nor is agency an additional capacity attached to an otherwise complete living system. It is the present-tense activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained (Spencer 2026).

Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change. Components are produced and degraded, disturbances compensated for, constraints maintained or reconstructed, and relations among processes altered as circumstances change. Process directs attention to this temporal organisation of persistence. It does not identify another entity alongside the organism or require processes to be metaphysically prior to organisation. It asks how organisation expressed in present activity remains continuous through sequences of change.

Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. Molecular interactions, cellular regulation, tissue organisation, physiological coordination, whole-organism activity, and relations with surrounding conditions can each become relevant to a biological explanandum. Multiscale research demonstrates that biological behaviour may depend upon interactions and constraints whose explanatory significance changes with the spatial or temporal scale at which a phenomenon is investigated (Green & Batterman 2017). Scale matters without implying that living organisation is divided into a hierarchy of independently acting levels.

This becomes especially important when influences extending across scales are described. Some literature uses terms such as “downward causation” for cases in which larger-scale conditions affect processes described at smaller scales. Green (2018), for example, examines how such claims can be made concrete through scale-dependent parameters and boundary conditions. The relevant explanatory work lies in this material specification rather than in the directional metaphor. Tissue configuration can alter conditions for cellular processes, physiological states can change molecular interactions, and spatial organisation can constrain subsequent activity. Such effects occur through materially realised constraints, boundary conditions, signals, interactions, resource distributions, histories, or other physical relations. A wider spatial or temporal description does not acquire an additional causal power simply by being wider.

Integrated organisation likewise need not imply a central controller standing above the processes it coordinates. Control can be distributed through materially implemented relations among mechanisms and constraints (Bich & Bechtel 2022). Which spatial or temporal extent becomes explanatorily important depends on the dependencies relevant to the phenomenon being explained.

Agency, Process, and Scale are therefore neither stages nor independent causes, components, levels, or interacting ontological domains. They are analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Agency asks what living systems do; Process asks how continuity is maintained despite change; Scale asks where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. The distinctions are analytic; the organisation and activities they disclose are biologically real.

Their complementarity becomes particularly clear when the living system itself changes substantially. Development provides a demanding case because biological organisation can remain continuous through major transformations of structure, activity, and relations.

## 5. Persistence Through Developmental Transformation

A developing organism does not persist by preserving the structure with which it began. Its dimensions, material composition, patterns of activity, functional capacities, and relations among parts may all change substantially. Structures arise, differentiate, are remodelled, and sometimes disappear. Yet these transformations ordinarily belong to the continuing history of one living system rather than constituting a succession of unrelated systems. Development therefore makes particularly clear why biological continuity cannot depend upon preserving an unchanged state.

A processual view of organisms already shows why material or structural sameness cannot provide the general basis of such continuity. Living systems depend upon continuing processes of production, maintenance, and transformation, so persistence need not require preservation of particular constituents or an unchanging configuration (Nicholson & Dupré 2018). Development intensifies the point because change is not merely something against which an existing organisation must be defended. Transformation can itself belong to the continuing organisation of the living system. The question becomes how successive organisational configurations remain connected through activities and dependencies that sustain biological continuity.

An organisational account of development makes this relationship explicit. Bich and Skillings (2024) argue against treating development as a sequence in which one completed organisational state is simply replaced by another. Organisation and development are reciprocally related: organisation at a given time participates in generating conditions for subsequent transformation, while developmental change alters the organisation through which further activity becomes possible. Continuity therefore need not require an invariant organisational configuration throughout the developmental trajectory.

For present purposes, development demonstrates particularly clearly what organised persistence entails. Persistence does not require an organism to preserve the organisation of an earlier state. What must continue is an organised relation among activities and conditions through which viable functioning remains possible as the system changes. Developmental transformation can alter particular constraints, structures, activities, and relations while remaining continuous with the organisation from which that transformation arose.

Organised persistence should therefore not be confused with resistance to change. Sometimes persistence requires restoration: damage is repaired or disturbance compensated for so that disrupted organisation can be re-established. Development demonstrates another possibility. Continuity can require transformation because maintaining a previous configuration may itself be incompatible with the continuing organisation of a developing system. What persists is not a fixed arrangement but organised continuity through which changing configurations remain connected within the history of the living system.

Development does not independently establish this account, nor do Agency, Process, and Scale supply three separate explanations of it. They disclose the activity, temporal continuity, and spatial and temporal organisation through which developmental persistence occurs. That organisation, however, is not materially self-sufficient. Development, like other forms of biological persistence, depends upon exchanges, signals, conditions, and constraints extending beyond an organism considered in isolation.

## 6. Organisation in an Environment and Across Scale

The activities through which an organism maintains and transforms itself depend upon exchanges of matter and energy, physical and chemical conditions, and interactions with its surroundings. Environmental relations are therefore not merely a background against which biological organisation operates. They form part of the material conditions under which it can continue. Recognising this dependence does not require treating the organism and everything with which it interacts as one undifferentiated system.

Dependence does not by itself establish organisational identity. Organisms can depend profoundly upon environmental conditions while retaining forms of organisation that are not thereby attributable to the environment as a whole. Work on organism–environment systems emphasises that what counts as an environment cannot always be specified independently of the organism and the relations relevant to the phenomenon under investigation (Corris 2020). The biological significance of temperature, nutrients, chemical gradients, physical structures, other organisms, or other environmental features depends upon the relations through which such conditions affect the living system. Recognising these relations makes biological organisation less isolated, not less specific.

The boundary between organism and environment should therefore not be confused with independence from the environment. A living system can be organisationally distinct while materially open and environmentally dependent. Its continued organisation may require external resources, removal of products, particular physical conditions, and interactions that alter its own activities. None of this implies that the surrounding environment possesses the same organisation as the organism. Coupling alone does not establish constraint closure, and dependence alone does not establish biological agency or normativity. Claims about where living organisation is present must depend upon the organisation demonstrated, rather than upon interaction alone.

Environmental dependence also illustrates why biological explanation may need to follow relations across different spatial and temporal extents. A local process can depend upon conditions established through wider patterns of organisation, while events at one time can alter conditions under which later processes occur. Multiscale approaches show that explanations can require variables, constraints, and interactions characterised at different scales, with their relevance determined by the phenomenon being explained (Green & Batterman 2017). A wider scale does not automatically supply a better explanation; biologically relevant dependencies simply need not be confined to the extent at which a particular mechanism is first described.

Where such dependencies cross scales, their explanatory significance depends upon material specification. Boundary conditions, signals, interactions, resource distributions, spatial arrangements, temporal histories, and other realised relations can alter what occurs locally (Green 2018). Wider organisational conditions matter through such relations, not because a higher level possesses an independent causal authority. Conversely, local activity can alter wider conditions through equally material pathways. The task is to identify the dependencies through which conditions change.

Biological organisation is consequently neither confined to isolated internal machinery nor dispersed indiscriminately across everything with which an organism interacts. It is realised through materially specific relations whose relevant spatial and temporal extent depends upon the organisation being explained. Environmental conditions can be indispensable without themselves constituting a living system; relations extending across scales can constrain local activity without invoking a hierarchy of independently acting levels.

Biological continuity can now be understood as materially realised, temporally sustained, environmentally dependent, and organised across the spatial and temporal extents relevant to persistence. Molecular interactions, mechanisms, regulatory processes, structures, environmental relations, and multiscale dependencies can all contribute to explaining it. Treating biological organisation as an explanatory target asks how those contributions are related in sustaining the living system.

## 7. Biological Organisation as a Target of Explanation

Treating organisation as an explanatory target does not diminish the importance of molecular, mechanistic, physiological, developmental, ecological, or other approaches. Different biological questions require different explanatory resources. A molecular explanation may identify interactions indispensable to a process; a mechanistic explanation may show how organised components produce an outcome; physiological explanation may disclose regulatory relations; developmental explanation may clarify transformation through time; ecological explanation may identify dependencies between organism and environment. Their explanatory value depends partly upon the question being asked and the explanatory aim against which an account is assessed. Explanatory pluralism in this sense does not imply that every explanation is equally adequate, but that biological phenomena can support different legitimate explanatory questions whose answers need not take the same form (Brigandt 2013).

Organisation becomes especially important when the explanandum concerns how such contributions are integrated into the continuing activity of a living system. Mechanistic and organisational explanations need not be competitors. Mechanisms specify materially realised components, activities, and interactions; organisational analysis asks how mechanisms and constraints are related within the system whose continued operation they help sustain. The organisational question is therefore not what exists in addition to mechanisms, but how the relevant dependencies contribute to continuing biological organisation.

The same principle applies to scale. Different spatial and temporal descriptions can disclose dependencies relevant to different questions, and multiscale analysis may be necessary when a phenomenon cannot be adequately characterised at a single scale (Green & Batterman 2017). No scale is automatically privileged because it is wider or narrower than another, and explanatory plurality does not require every scale to appear in every explanation. The relevant extent is determined by the dependencies that matter to the explanandum.

Biological organisation therefore provides a target around which different explanatory contributions can be related without reducing them to a single vocabulary. Contemporary organisational biology already treats organisation as both something requiring explanation and something capable of contributing to explanation (Mossio 2023). APS develops this orientation by asking how materially realised dependencies bear upon the continuing viability and organised persistence of the living system. Organisational explanation is warranted where identifying those relations makes a difference to understanding the biological phenomenon at issue, not because organisation universally supersedes other explanatory targets.

To treat biological organisation as a central explanatory target is consequently not to claim that every biological question is fundamentally organisational. Molecular detail, mechanisms, physical principles, developmental histories, and environmental relations do not become explanatory only when translated into organisational language. Their contribution depends upon the explanandum. The organisational perspective becomes distinctive when the question concerns how such dependencies participate in maintaining, re-establishing, or transforming the conditions under which the living system can continue.

This returns us to the problem with which the article began. Living systems do not sustain themselves by preserving the same material constituents, remaining in an unchanged state, or maintaining one fixed organisational configuration. Their continuity depends upon activities that regulate, repair, reconstruct, and sometimes transform the organisation through which continued functioning remains possible. Those activities are materially realised, environmentally dependent, temporally extended, and organised across the spatial and temporal extents relevant to persistence. Agency, Process, and Scale disclose different aspects of this one continuing organisation without dividing it into separate biological realities.

What persists, then, is neither matter alone nor an abstract pattern detached from its material realisation. It is living organisation continually enacted through the dependencies that sustain it. Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. This organisation consists in the ongoing modulation of constraints through which a system actively maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence. Living systems sustain themselves not by remaining unchanged, but through organised activity in which change itself can contribute to persistence.

Biological organisation thereby provides a common explanatory target to which different biological approaches may contribute without being reduced to a single explanatory vocabulary.
