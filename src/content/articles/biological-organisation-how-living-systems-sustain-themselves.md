> **Where this article fits**
>
> This article examines biological organisation as a central explanatory concern within APS. It places the framework's account of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation alongside established research on biological autonomy, organisational closure, regulation, and mechanistic explanation. Its particular purpose is to clarify how Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary analytic perspectives on the continuity of living organisation through change. It does not claim that APS originated organisational approaches to biology, that organised persistence is the universal target of biological explanation, or that the framework has demonstrated explanatory superiority over established alternatives.

## The Problem of Biological Continuity

Living organisms exhibit a remarkable combination of continuity and change. Their molecular constituents are continually synthesised, degraded, exchanged, and replaced. Cells maintain their functions through metabolic activity, membrane transport, regulatory adjustment, and the repair or renewal of structures. Multicellular organisms undergo development, growth, physiological adaptation, and ageing, often experiencing substantial changes in their material composition and organisation while remaining identifiable as continuing living individuals.

The persistence of an organism therefore cannot always be understood as the persistence of an unchanged collection of material parts. What continues is a living system whose constituent activities and structures remain sufficiently coordinated to sustain its functioning, even as particular components and relationships change.

This observation raises a significant explanatory question: how can a biological system maintain continuity when the material conditions and processes through which it exists are themselves continually changing?

The question has a substantial history in biology and philosophy of biology. Organisational approaches have emphasised that the activities of living systems contribute to maintaining the conditions on which those activities depend. Contemporary accounts of biological autonomy and constraint closure have developed this insight into more precise theoretical descriptions of the relationships among biological processes, constraints, and self-maintenance (Montévil and Mossio, 2015; Mossio, Montévil and Longo, 2016).

Within APS, the continuity of living organisation through change is described as **organised persistence**. This expression identifies a phenomenon requiring explanation, not an additional mechanism or causal force responsible for its occurrence.

Organised persistence must therefore be distinguished from the processes that contribute to it. Metabolic regulation, molecular repair, physiological coordination, developmental reorganisation, and environmental interaction may all contribute to continuity in particular biological circumstances. Their contributions must be established through the appropriate biological evidence rather than inferred from the general fact that an organism persists.

The organisational question complements established biological investigation. Molecular biology identifies biochemical interactions; physiology examines the regulation and coordination of functions; developmental biology investigates the production and transformation of organised form; ecology examines relationships among organisms and their environments; and evolutionary biology investigates historical transformations in populations and lineages. Each provides explanatory resources that may illuminate biological continuity where relevant to the particular question being investigated.

APS does not require these different explanations to be reduced to a single organisational account. Its interest is in making explicit the relationships among activities, structures, constraints, and conditions where those relationships are relevant to understanding the persistence of living organisation.

## Biological Organisation in Contemporary Theory

Biological organisation is more than the spatial arrangement of biological components. The distinction is important because an arrangement of parts does not, by itself, explain how those parts contribute to the continued functioning of a living system.

A cell contains membranes, enzymes, nucleic acids, molecular assemblies, and numerous other structures. Their biological significance depends not merely on their presence but on the activities and relationships through which they participate in cellular functioning. Enzymes contribute to metabolic transformations; membranes participate in selective exchange and compartmentalisation; regulatory systems alter activities in response to changing conditions. The continuing operation of the cell depends upon particular relationships among these activities and structures.

Mechanistic explanations investigate such relationships by identifying the entities, activities, and organisation responsible for particular phenomena. They can provide detailed accounts of how biological processes occur and why particular interventions produce particular outcomes.

Organisational approaches ask a related but distinguishable question: how do the activities and constraints of a biological system contribute to maintaining the system within which they operate?

The contemporary autonomy tradition has given this question considerable theoretical precision. In particular, Montévil and Mossio (2015) characterise biological organisation through the closure of constraints. Their account distinguishes processes occurring under thermodynamically open conditions from constraints that influence those processes while exhibiting relative stability over relevant timescales.

Constraints can influence the course of biological processes without being understood as external designers or independent directing forces. Their continued existence may itself depend upon other activities within the biological system. Organisational closure occurs where such dependencies form a network in which constraints contribute, directly or indirectly, to maintaining the conditions of their own continued operation.

This conception is not equivalent to material isolation. Living organisms remain dependent upon exchanges of matter and energy with their environments. Organisational closure concerns relationships of mutual maintenance within an open material system, not independence from environmental conditions.

Mossio, Montévil and Longo (2016) further develop organisation as a theoretical principle for understanding biological stability and variation. Their account emphasises that biological organisation is not simply the conservation of a fixed structure. It also provides a context in which changes may be accommodated, propagated, or incorporated into continuing biological activity.

These developments establish that the organisational interpretation of living systems is a substantial research tradition rather than an innovation introduced by APS.

An additional contribution comes from Bich and Bechtel (2022), who examine the relationship between mechanistic accounts and the autonomy framework. They argue that control and regulation can help connect these approaches, showing how biological mechanisms may be coordinated in ways that contribute to maintaining organismal viability.

Their analysis is particularly important because it demonstrates that mechanistic explanation and organisational self-maintenance need not be treated as competing alternatives. The integration of mechanisms, control, regulation, and viability is already an established subject of theoretical investigation.

APS should therefore not claim novelty merely from bringing these concepts together.

Instead, APS adopts viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation as its account of living organisation and examines that organisation through three complementary analytic projections: Agency, Process, and Scale.

The contribution being articulated is a particular way of organising explanatory attention. Whether that organisation of concepts produces additional scientific understanding or explanatory gain must be established through appropriately matched comparisons, not assumed from the architecture itself.

## Viability, Regulation, and the Organisation of Persistence

The maintenance of biological organisation depends upon conditions that permit continued functioning. These conditions include material resources, energy availability, suitable physicochemical environments, and the continuing operation of activities and constraints required by the system concerned.

APS uses **viability** to identify the conditions under which living organisation can continue to function.

Viability should not be understood as a single universal numerical quantity or as a fixed set of conditions applying identically to every organism. What matters depends upon the organisation under investigation, its environmental circumstances, and the temporal extent relevant to the explanatory question.

For a cell, changes in membrane integrity, metabolic activity, or the availability of essential resources may alter its prospects for continued functioning. For a multicellular organism, viability may depend upon coordinated physiological activities involving many tissues and organs. In developmental contexts, the relevant conditions may themselves change as the organism undergoes transformation.

These differences are biologically consequential because not all changes have equivalent effects on the continued functioning of the organisation concerned.

Within APS, this asymmetry provides the limited sense of **biological normativity** required by the organisational account. Some conditions support continued functioning, while others impair or destroy the organisation on which that functioning depends.

This does not imply that the organism consciously judges its circumstances, represents its interests, or deliberately chooses among alternatives. Viability-relative significance is a property of the relationship between a living organisation and the conditions of its continuation, not an attribution of mental experience.

The connection between biological organisation, autonomy, and normativity has already been extensively explored in organisational approaches to biology. APS draws upon this intellectual context while retaining its own formulation of viability-oriented activity.

Regulation becomes important because living systems encounter changes that can alter the conditions of their continued functioning. Regulatory processes may modify metabolic rates, alter resource allocation, repair damage, adjust physiological activity, or change interactions with the environment.

Such processes can contribute to maintaining or re-establishing viable organisation. Their effectiveness depends upon the biological circumstances and cannot be established merely by describing an activity as regulatory.

The same qualification applies to repair and reorganisation. Not every response to disturbance restores viability, and not every organisational change preserves continuity. Regulation can fail; damage can exceed the capacities of repair; and changes in environmental conditions can make continued functioning impossible.

These adverse outcomes are important to biological explanation because they help identify which relationships and activities are actually necessary for the persistence of the system under investigation.

APS characterises living organisation as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**.

Within this formulation, viability identifies the conditions relevant to continued functioning, constraint closure identifies a central organisational relationship among mutually dependent constraints, and viability-oriented activity concerns the enactment and maintenance of the organisation through which the living system persists.

Organised persistence identifies the resulting continuity through change. It is not an additional cause operating alongside metabolism, regulation, repair, development, or other biological processes.

The scientific task is therefore to determine which materially realised activities and dependencies account for continuity in the particular case.

## Agency, Process, and Scale as Analytic Projections

APS examines living organisation through three complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections: Agency, Process, and Scale.

These projections do not divide a living system into three independent constituents. Nor do they identify successive stages through which biological organisation must pass. They are different ways of analysing one materially realised organisation, selected according to the explanatory question.

**Agency concerns what living systems do.**

Within APS, biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity. It includes activities through which living systems regulate, maintain, repair, reorganise, acquire resources, and modify their relationships with their environments where these activities contribute to the conditions of continued functioning.

Agency is not an additional force directing otherwise passive biological mechanisms. It identifies the viability-oriented character of the organisation enacted through those mechanisms and activities.

Nor does biological agency require consciousness, intention, deliberation, symbolic representation, or cognition. These capacities raise additional questions that cannot be settled merely by establishing the existence of viability-oriented activity.

**Process concerns how continuity is maintained despite change.**

Living organisation is enacted through processes occurring over time. Metabolic transformations, physiological regulation, cellular renewal, development, and environmental interaction may contribute to maintaining or reconstructing the relationships necessary for continued functioning.

The Process projection directs attention to these dynamics rather than treating biological persistence as the conservation of an unchanged structure.

This does not imply that every biological process contributes positively to viability. Some processes are destructive, pathological, or incompatible with continued functioning. Their explanatory significance depends upon their actual consequences for the organisation concerned.

**Scale concerns where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.**

Biological explanations may involve molecular interactions, cellular processes, organismal regulation, ecological relationships, or changes extending across generations. Their relevance depends upon the phenomenon being investigated.

APS does not treat these descriptions as a hierarchy of independently existing causal levels. Differences in spatial or temporal extent do not themselves establish relations of control, dependence, or causal influence.

Where activities at different extents are explanatorily related, the relevant connections must be specified through materially realised interactions, conditions, constraints, or dependencies.

A biological example can illustrate the complementarity of these projections.

Consider a cell responding to a change in its external environment. Membrane transport, metabolic regulation, and other physiological activities may contribute to maintaining conditions compatible with continued functioning.

The Agency projection asks how these activities contribute to viability. The Process projection examines how the relevant adjustments occur and how they alter the course of cellular activity through time. The Scale projection identifies the spatial and temporal extents necessary to explain the response, including the relationships among molecular activities, cellular organisation, and environmental conditions.

These are not three competing causal explanations. They are complementary perspectives on the same biological case.

The explanatory adequacy of the resulting account still depends upon the mechanisms, evidence, and relationships actually established. The use of APS terminology does not, by itself, add explanatory capacity.

## Persistence Through Developmental Transformation

Development provides an especially clear illustration of the distinction between material or structural invariance and biological continuity.

A developing organism may undergo substantial changes in size, morphology, physiology, and the relationships among its constituent structures. Cells differentiate, tissues acquire specialised functions, and new relationships among organs and physiological systems emerge.

These transformations need not interrupt the continuity of the developing organism.

Indeed, continued development often depends upon changes that would be incompatible with the maintenance of an earlier structural arrangement. Biological continuity can therefore involve the regulated transformation of organisation rather than the preservation of an unchanged form.

This does not mean that development can be explained simply by invoking organised persistence. Developmental biology already provides detailed accounts of gene regulation, cellular signalling, morphogenesis, differentiation, mechanical interactions, and the environmental conditions influencing developmental outcomes.

The organisational question concerns how such activities and dependencies contribute to the continued functioning and transformation of the developing system.

For example, the differentiation of cells within a developing tissue involves changes in cellular activities and relationships. These changes may contribute to the emergence of new tissue functions while depending upon the continued operation of metabolic, regulatory, and developmental processes.

An organisational analysis asks how these relationships are coordinated and maintained through the transformation. It does not replace the experimentally established mechanisms responsible for differentiation.

Within APS, Agency concerns the viability-oriented activities through which developmental organisation is enacted. Process concerns the dynamics of transformation and continuity. Scale concerns the spatial and temporal extents required to explain the developmental phenomenon.

The three projections therefore direct attention to different aspects of one developing organisation.

Development also illustrates the limits of organisational continuity. Developmental regulation can be disrupted, differentiation can proceed abnormally, and the conditions necessary for continued development may fail.

Such outcomes help distinguish the processes contributing to viable developmental organisation from those that merely accompany developmental change.

The explanatory value of APS in this context would depend upon whether its analysis makes relevant relationships more explicit, assessable, or useful than the strongest available developmental account of the same phenomenon.

Development illustrates the organisational question; it does not independently establish the superiority of the APS framework.

## Organisation in an Environment and Across Scale

Living organisation is materially realised and environmentally dependent.

Organisms require exchanges of matter and energy, and their continued functioning depends upon environmental conditions compatible with their organisation. At the same time, living systems can alter their surroundings through metabolism, behaviour, resource use, and other activities.

Biological organisation therefore cannot be understood as a materially isolated system.

This dependence is compatible with the concept of organisational closure. Closure of constraints concerns relationships among constraints and processes within an organisation; it does not imply that the organism is closed to matter, energy, or environmental influence.

Nevertheless, environmental dependence does not automatically erase the distinction between the living system and its surroundings.

A system may depend upon external resources without those resources thereby becoming components of its constraint-closed organisation. Likewise, an organism may alter its environment without every resulting environmental change becoming an expression of biological agency.

The relevant boundaries depend upon the organisation and explanatory target being investigated.

This distinction is important when examining biological systems across spatial and temporal extents.

A physiological response may involve molecular interactions within cells, coordination among tissues, and changes in organism–environment relations. These activities can be causally connected, but their connections must be demonstrated through the relevant material interactions and dependencies.

It is insufficient to say that a higher level controls a lower level, or that a lower level produces a higher level, without specifying what these expressions mean in the particular biological case.

The APS Scale projection therefore does not introduce an independent hierarchy of causal levels. It identifies the spatial and temporal extents relevant to the organisation under investigation.

This position is compatible with mechanistic approaches that identify organised interactions among components, as well as with organisational approaches that examine how mechanisms contribute to the maintenance of the wider living system.

Bich and Bechtel (2022) provide an important account of this relationship through their analysis of control and regulation. Their treatment demonstrates that coordinated biological control need not be represented as the action of a centralised directing agency.

APS does not claim to replace such analyses. Its concern is to preserve the relationship among viability-oriented activity, the processes through which organisation continues, and the extents across which relevant dependencies are realised.

The resulting account remains subject to empirical investigation.

## What Organisational Explanation Contributes

Biological organisation is a significant explanatory concern, but it is not the universal target of biological explanation.

Some biological questions can be adequately answered through the identification of particular molecular mechanisms. Others require physiological models, developmental histories, ecological relationships, evolutionary explanations, or combinations of explanatory resources.

An organisational perspective is relevant where the relationships among activities, structures, constraints, and conditions contribute to understanding the phenomenon under investigation.

It does not follow that every successful biological explanation must explicitly invoke organisational closure, viability, or organised persistence.

Nor does it follow that describing a mechanism as part of a wider organisation necessarily improves the explanation.

The distinction between conceptual integration and explanatory gain is especially important.

A framework may make relationships among established concepts more explicit. It may help readers recognise dependencies that are otherwise dispersed across different descriptions. It may provide a consistent vocabulary for comparing biological cases or identifying questions requiring further investigation.

These are potentially useful intellectual contributions.

They are not automatically evidence that the framework explains more than the strongest established account of the same phenomenon.

A claim of distinctive explanatory gain requires a properly matched comparison that specifies the explanatory target, reconstructs the strongest relevant alternative, identifies the additional contribution being claimed, and establishes that contribution through appropriate evidence.

The comparison may legitimately show additional gain, complementarity, equivalent redescription, an unresolved result, or no useful additional contribution.

APS accepts these outcomes as part of responsible scientific assessment.

The organisational approach also has a substantial intellectual history. Contemporary theories of autonomy, constraint closure, regulation, and integrated biological control already address many of the relationships that APS seeks to make explicit.

The distinctive articulation of APS lies in its use of Agency, Process, and Scale as complementary analytic projections of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. This articulation should be assessed for its precision, empirical relevance, integrative usefulness, and explanatory contribution rather than presumed to constitute a new biological principle.

Within APS, Life is characterised as:

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

This is the canonical APS definition of life. It does not replace the empirical and theoretical work required to determine how particular living systems are organised or how particular biological phenomena are explained.

Organised persistence identifies the continuity of that living organisation through change.

The central scientific question is therefore not whether the terminology of organisation can be applied to living systems, but whether specifying particular organisational relationships helps us understand biological phenomena in ways that are clear, evidence-based, and appropriately related to established explanations.

In this sense, APS offers an integrative perspective on biological organisation while leaving its scientific contribution open to disciplined comparative assessment.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

**How do living systems maintain biological continuity despite continual material, functional, developmental, and environmental change, and what kind of organisation makes that persistence possible?**

### Architectural Role

This Core article establishes **biological organisation as a central explanatory target within APS**.

It situates the APS account of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation within established research on biological autonomy, organisational closure, regulation, and mechanistic explanation.

The article explains how Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of one materially realised living organisation.

Its purpose is not to replace molecular, mechanistic, physiological, developmental, ecological, evolutionary, or other biological explanations. It identifies organisational relationships to which these approaches may contribute where relevant to the explanandum.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

These concepts establish the APS commitments presupposed by this article:

- **Life** — viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.
- **Biological Agency** — viability-oriented organisational activity.
- **Viability** — conditions relevant to the continued functioning of living organisation.
- **Constraint Closure** — the organisational relationship through which mutually dependent constraints contribute to maintaining the organisation within which they operate.
- **Process** — the analytic projection concerned with how continuity is maintained despite change.
- **Scale** — the analytic projection concerned with where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.
- **Organised Persistence** — continuity of living organisation through change.

These dependencies do not constitute a hierarchy, chronology, sequence of biological levels, or set of independent causes.

Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of one living organisation rather than independent causes, components, or hierarchical levels.

The article contributes to the integrative architecture of APS while acknowledging the substantial existing scholarship on biological organisation and leaving the explanatory adequacy of particular biological accounts to be established through appropriate evidence and comparison.
