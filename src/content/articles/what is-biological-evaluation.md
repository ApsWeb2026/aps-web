## Introduction

Living systems do not merely exist within their environments. They continuously distinguish conditions that support their continued functioning from those that threaten it. A bacterium moves toward nutrients and away from toxins. A plant alters its growth in response to light, water, and competition. Animals assess opportunities, risks, and social relationships. Across all forms of life, some conditions matter more than others.

APS refers to this process as biological evaluation.

Biological evaluation is the process by which living systems distinguish internal and external conditions according to their significance for viability, functioning, and adaptive persistence. Through evaluation, organisms identify what supports or undermines their continued existence and adjust their activity accordingly.

Why Evaluation Matters

Biology is often described in terms of information, regulation, adaptation, or behaviour. While each of these concepts is important, they leave an important question unanswered:

Why does some information matter to an organism while other information does not?

A thermometer detects temperature, but it does not care whether the temperature rises or falls. A living organism also detects temperature, but for the organism temperature may affect metabolism, growth, survival, or reproduction. The difference is that living systems do not merely process information. They evaluate its biological significance.

Evaluation therefore introduces a crucial dimension absent from purely mechanical systems: relevance. Living systems continuously distinguish between conditions that promote persistence and those that threaten it.

Evaluation and Agency

In APS, biological agency refers to the capacity of living systems to initiate, regulate, and coordinate goal-directed interactions that sustain functional integrity and adaptive continuity.

Agency necessarily involves evaluation.

An organism incapable of distinguishing favourable from unfavourable conditions could not regulate itself adaptively. It could not identify food, avoid danger, repair damage, coordinate development, or respond effectively to environmental change. Agency therefore depends upon the capacity to evaluate.

For this reason, biological evaluation is not an optional feature of living systems. It is one of the processes that makes agency possible.

Evaluation Is Not Information Processing

Biological evaluation is closely related to information processing, but the two are not identical.

Information processing concerns the detection, transmission, storage, and transformation of information. Evaluation concerns the significance of that information for the organism.

A receptor may detect a signal, but evaluation determines whether the signal is beneficial, harmful, irrelevant, or requires a response. Information processing describes how information moves through a system. Evaluation concerns what that information means for the continued functioning of the system.

This distinction helps explain why living systems are not simply information-processing machines. They are systems that process information in relation to their own persistence.

Evaluation Is Not Cognition

Evaluation is also distinct from cognition.

In APS, cognition refers to context-sensitive information processing that supports adaptive regulation. Cognitive systems often involve perception, memory, learning, anticipation, and flexible decision-making.

Evaluation appears to be more fundamental.

Many organisms evaluate conditions without possessing the forms of cognition associated with animals or nervous systems. Bacteria distinguish nutrients from toxins. Immune systems discriminate between threats and non-threats. Plants respond differently to changing environmental conditions. These processes involve biological evaluation even when sophisticated cognition is absent.

Cognition therefore builds upon evaluation rather than replacing it. Evaluation provides the biological significance that cognition can subsequently process, integrate, and elaborate.

Evaluation and Behaviour

Behaviour can be understood as evaluation expressed in action.

Organisms move toward resources, avoid threats, cooperate with partners, compete with rivals, and modify their environments because they evaluate conditions differently. Behaviour is therefore not simply movement or activity. It is directed activity shaped by ongoing evaluations of biological significance.

This applies across a wide range of organisms and scales. Whether the response involves a growing root, a migrating animal, or a social group coordinating collective action, behaviour reflects distinctions about what matters under current conditions.

Evaluation and Evolution

Evaluation also contributes to evolutionary processes.

Evolutionary change ultimately depends on variation, heredity, and differential persistence. However, organisms are not passive objects within these processes. Through behaviour, development, habitat selection, niche construction, and social interaction, organisms influence the conditions under which evolution occurs.

These influences are often guided by biological evaluation. Organisms preferentially exploit some opportunities, avoid some threats, construct some environments, and form some relationships rather than others. In doing so, they contribute to the evolutionary dynamics that shape future populations and lineages.

Biological evaluation does not replace natural selection, but it helps explain how organismal activity participates in evolutionary change.

Evaluation, Value, and Meaning

Biological evaluation should not be confused with human values, morality, or meaning.

A plant evaluating light availability is not making a moral judgement. A bacterium moving toward nutrients is not pursuing a philosophy of life. Nevertheless, these examples reveal something important: living systems already distinguish conditions according to their significance.

Human values, moral systems, and experiences of meaning emerge from far more complex forms of evaluation involving cognition, language, culture, social interaction, and reflective reasoning. They cannot be reduced to simple biological regulation. Yet neither do they emerge from nowhere.

From an APS perspective, biological evaluation provides the deepest biological foundation from which increasingly sophisticated forms of significance can develop.

A simplified pathway is:

Agency → Biological Evaluation → Cognition → Behaviour → Values → Morality → Meaning

This pathway does not imply that morality or meaning are reducible to biology. Rather, it highlights the continuity between the evaluative capacities found throughout life and the richer forms of significance found in human societies.

Biological Evaluation in APS

Biological evaluation is not a fourth APS principle alongside agency, process, and scale.

Instead, it is a bridge concept.

It helps explain how agency operates, how cognition acquires significance, how behaviour becomes directed, how values emerge, and how meaning becomes possible. Evaluation therefore connects many domains that are often treated separately within biology, philosophy, and the human sciences.

By making this process explicit, APS provides a clearer account of how living systems distinguish what matters.

Conclusion

Living systems are not passive collections of parts responding mechanically to external forces. They continuously distinguish conditions according to their consequences for continued functioning and persistence. This process of biological evaluation underlies adaptive regulation across all forms of life.

Biological evaluation is therefore a fundamental feature of living systems. It supports agency, guides behaviour, informs cognition, and provides the biological roots of significance. Through increasingly complex forms of social interaction, culture, and reflection, it also contributes to the emergence of values, morality, and meaning.

To understand how living systems act, learn, adapt, and ultimately create worlds of significance, we must first understand how they evaluate.
