## Introduction

How do living systems remain alive despite continually changing conditions?

Organisms persist while undergoing material turnover, energetic exchange, developmental transformation, environmental interaction, perturbation, injury, repair, and decay. Their material constituents and relations change continually, yet sufficient organisational continuity is maintained for them to continue functioning as living systems.

This continuity cannot be explained by static structure alone. Living systems continually sustain, regulate, repair, reorganise, and re-establish conditions required for their continued functioning. Nor are these conditions maintained in isolation. Organism–world relations provide resources, constraints, perturbations, and opportunities that materially alter what continued functioning requires and what forms of activity remain possible.

APS uses **viability** to identify the system-relative conditions under which continued functioning can be maintained and re-established.

Viability is therefore not simply the fact that a system remains alive. It concerns the conditions relative to which ongoing biological activity can support, undermine, or restore continued functioning. A living system may remain extant temporarily while those conditions deteriorate, just as maintaining viability may require substantial reorganisation rather than preservation of a fixed state.

Within APS, viability must be understood as an aspect of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed living organisation. It is analytically distinguishable from biological agency and organised persistence, but it is not an intermediate component linking them. Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity, while organised persistence concerns the continuity of living organisation through change. Viability identifies the conditions relative to which that activity and continuity acquire their persistence-related significance.

This relationship gives viability an important organising role within APS. It helps clarify how function, normativity, development, resilience, organism–world relations, and cognition can be investigated in relation to conditions that support or undermine continued biological functioning without implying that viability by itself explains these phenomena or supersedes their established biological explanations.

## Viability Beyond Survival

Viability is sometimes interpreted narrowly as survival, but the two should be distinguished. Survival describes an outcome—the continued existence of a system—whereas viability concerns the organisational conditions under which continued functioning remains possible.

The distinction matters because continued presence does not guarantee continued viability. A system can remain extant temporarily while progressively losing regulatory, physiological, developmental, or relational capacities required for its continued functioning. Conversely, maintaining viability can require instability, repair, adaptation, reorganisation, or transformation when existing organisation is no longer adequate under changed conditions.

Viability therefore concerns more than occupancy of a momentary state. It directs attention to whether the organisation required for continued functioning is being maintained or can be re-established through ongoing activity.

[[box:viability-is-not-mere-survival]]

This temporal dimension is particularly important because living systems are dynamically maintained organisations. Their continued functioning depends not upon remaining materially unchanged but upon preserving and reconstructing relevant organisational relations despite continual turnover and perturbation.

The distinction between survival and viability also prevents a misleading contrast between living and non-living persistence. A non-living structure may endure for long periods without participating in the maintenance of conditions required for its own continued functioning. Living systems, by contrast, are organised through activity that is viability-oriented: what occurs within and through the system can make a difference to whether viable conditions are maintained, undermined, or restored.

## Viability and Organised Persistence

Living systems maintain viability through ongoing regulation, repair, reorganisation, material exchange, and interaction with their environments. Depending upon the system and explanatory target, this can involve metabolic organisation, physiological regulation, repair of damage, responses to perturbation, developmental reorganisation, reconstruction of degraded organisation, and changes in organism–world relations.

These activities should not be understood as steps in a sequence leading from Agency through Viability to Organised Persistence. They are different aspects of the ongoing organisation through which living systems continue functioning.

Biological agency identifies the viability-oriented organisational activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained. Viability identifies the system-relative conditions in relation to which that activity supports or undermines continued functioning. Organised persistence identifies the explanatory problem of how sufficient continuity of living organisation is maintained and re-established despite continual change.

The relationship is therefore analytic rather than sequential. Viability does not convert agency into persistence, and organised persistence is not a later state produced after viability has first been secured. Living organisation persists through activity whose consequences are significant relative to viability conditions.

This distinction also clarifies why viability is central without functioning as a bridge or independent layer within APS. Its importance lies in making explicit the persistence-relative asymmetry between conditions compatible with continued functioning and conditions that undermine it.

Within APS, organised persistence consequently asks how one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation maintains and re-establishes continuity through change.

## Viability and Temporal Organisation

Viability is inherently temporal because the conditions required for continued functioning cannot be understood at an isolated instant. Living systems undergo continual material replacement, physiological adjustment, developmental transformation, environmental interaction, and internal reorganisation. What matters is not material identity across time but the maintenance and re-establishment of organisation sufficient for continued functioning.

This processual character connects viability with **Process**, the APS analytic perspective concerned with how continuity is maintained despite change. Process does not constitute an additional component of living organisation. It directs explanatory attention toward temporal organisation: how activities and relations unfolding at different rates contribute to, undermine, or restore viable conditions.

Living systems must often coordinate processes with different temporal characteristics, respond to changing internal and external conditions, preserve continuity despite material turnover, reorganise under perturbation, and integrate developmental transformation with ongoing regulation. These requirements make temporal organisation central to understanding how viable functioning continues through change.

Viability should therefore not be conceived as a static state that a living system simply possesses. Viable conditions are continually affected by biological activity and organism–world relations, and they may be maintained, degraded, or re-established over time.

Temporal organisation thus helps explain why persistence does not require invariance. Living systems can remain continuous precisely because their organisation is capable of changing in ways that maintain or re-establish viability-relevant conditions.

## Viability and Constraint Closure

Viability and constraint closure are analytically distinguishable aspects of the APS account of living organisation, but they should not be represented as independently constituted components that are subsequently combined.

Constraint closure concerns materially realised relations among constraints through which ongoing biological activity is selectively channelled and mutually sustained. Viability orientation concerns the system-relative asymmetry between conditions compatible with continued functioning and conditions that undermine it.

Within the canonical APS formulation—

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

—neither term is a temporal or explanatory precursor of the other. Constraint closure does not first generate a neutral organisation that viability subsequently renders biological, and viability is not an additional ingredient added to otherwise independently constituted closure.

This distinction matters because closure considered abstractly does not by itself establish Life, agency, normativity, or organised persistence. At the same time, viability orientation should not be treated as an independent discriminator whose presence alone settles biological status. The APS claim concerns one materially realised organisation whose constraints and activities are organised relative to conditions of continued functioning.

Under perturbation or changing circumstances, those conditions may be maintained, undermined, or re-established through changes in organisation. Viability therefore directs attention to the significance of organisational change relative to continued functioning without extending “beyond” closure as though the two belonged to successive explanatory layers.

## Viability and Normativity

Viability is central to the APS account of biological normativity because living systems exist under conditions in which different states, activities, and relations have unequal consequences for continued functioning.

Some changes support or restore viable conditions; others undermine them. This is a **viability-relative asymmetry**. It does not require conscious judgement, intention, moral evaluation, or an external observer assigning value to biological states. The asymmetry arises from the organisation of the living system itself and the consequences different conditions have for its continued functioning.

Within APS, this provides the organisational basis for biological normativity. A regulatory response can succeed or fail, a process can contribute to or undermine function, and a perturbation can be tolerated or damaging because these differences bear upon viability-relevant conditions.

Viability therefore also bears upon biological evaluation and significance. Biological activity acquires significance insofar as differences in conditions or relations matter to the organisation's continued functioning. APS describes Biological Evaluation as the process through which agency generates significance, while viability provides the persistence-relative conditions in relation to which those differences matter.

Function is related but should not be collapsed into viability. Within APS, a biological function is a present-tense viability-relative organisational contribution. A structure, process, or activity is functional insofar as its contribution bears upon maintaining or re-establishing viable organisation in the relevant context.

These relationships do not form a linear sequence in which Agency produces Viability, which produces Function, which then produces Normativity. Agency, viability, function, normativity, evaluation, and significance are analytically distinguishable aspects of living organisation whose precise explanatory relationships depend upon the target under investigation.

## Viability and Agency

Agency and viability are closely related within APS, but their relationship is not reciprocal interaction between two independently constituted entities.

**Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity.**

Agency therefore identifies what living systems do as living organisations: they regulate, modulate constraints, repair, reorganise, coordinate, and alter organism–world relations in ways whose consequences bear upon continued functioning. Such activity need not involve cognition, deliberation, conscious intention, or behavioural choice.

Viability identifies the system-relative conditions that give this activity its persistence-related orientation. Activity can support, undermine, or restore those conditions, and this asymmetry makes it possible to distinguish successful regulation from failure without attributing mental evaluation to the system.

It is therefore misleading to say either that Agency first maintains an independently existing Viability or that Viability subsequently gives Agency its orientation. Within APS, viability orientation is already intrinsic to the definition of biological agency. The distinction between the two is analytic: Agency concerns the activity of living organisation, while viability concerns the conditions relative to which that activity is significant for continued functioning.

This relationship also clarifies why agency should not be reduced to overt behaviour. Regulation, constraint modulation, repair, physiological adjustment, developmental reorganisation, and changes in organism–environment relations can all instantiate biological agency when they constitute viability-oriented organisational activity.

Agency and viability consequently illuminate different explanatory questions concerning the same living organisation rather than forming successive links in a causal chain.

## Viability Across Biological Domains

Viability-relevant conditions can be investigated across many biological domains, but this does not mean that viability constitutes the same explanatory object in every domain or that all biological organisation should itself be described as viable.

In physiology, viability concerns conditions under which metabolic, regulatory, and other organismal activities can continue functioning. Physiological processes can maintain or restore these conditions, while physiological failure can progressively undermine them.

Development presents a different problem. Organisms undergo substantial transformation while remaining living systems. Viability therefore bears upon how changing developmental organisation remains compatible with continued functioning throughout those transformations.

Evolution concerns historical transformation across generations. APS does not treat viability as an alternative to evolutionary explanation. Evolutionary theory explains changes in populations and lineages through established evolutionary processes, while viability can be used to ask how the continued functioning of organisms bears upon the historical processes in which they participate.

Ecology makes organism–world dependence especially explicit. Living systems acquire resources, encounter perturbations, modify surroundings, and participate in relations that alter viability-relevant conditions. These environmental relations are materially constitutive of the circumstances under which organismal viability is maintained or undermined, but this does not require attributing biological agency or organismal viability indiscriminately to ecological systems themselves.

Cognition requires a further distinction. APS does not identify viability-oriented regulation with cognition. Cognition involves a more specific organisation of biological significance across time, while viability provides part of the biological context within which significance arises. Life does not require cognition; cognition presupposes Life.

These examples illustrate why **Scale** should not be understood as a hierarchy of biological levels. Viability-relevant relations may extend across different spatial and temporal extents, and explanations should track where the materially relevant constraints, activities, dependencies, and environmental conditions are organised for the target under investigation.

Scale therefore asks where persistence is organised rather than locating viability at a single privileged level or distributing a single form of viability uniformly across cells, organisms, ecosystems, and lineages.

## Viability and Resilience

Viability does not require perfect stability. Living systems continually encounter perturbation, resource variation, injury, developmental change, and degradation. If viable organisation depended upon preservation of a fixed state, persistence under ordinary biological conditions would be impossible.

Resilience is relevant because living systems can sometimes absorb disturbance, reorganise activity, repair damage, and re-establish conditions compatible with continued functioning. In this sense, resilience can contribute to viability without being identical to it.

This distinction matters because resilience should not be equated with resistance to change. Physiological flexibility, developmental plasticity, behavioural adjustment, and other forms of reorganisation can preserve continuity precisely by altering existing organisation.

Viability therefore concerns neither rigidity nor return to an invariant state. What matters is whether relevant organisation can continue or be re-established under changed conditions.

This reinforces the processual character of living organisation. Persistence is maintained through transformation, and resilience identifies one family of capacities through which disruption can be accommodated without loss of the organisation required for continued functioning.

## Viability in APS

Within APS, viability belongs to the canonical account of Life rather than occupying an intermediate position in a chain linking Agency to organised persistence.

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

From this starting point, different APS concepts identify different explanatory questions concerning the same living organisation.

**Agency** concerns what living systems do: viability-oriented organisational activity.

**Viability** concerns the system-relative conditions under which continued functioning can be maintained and re-established.

**Organised persistence** concerns the maintenance and re-establishment of living organisation through change.

**Process** asks how continuity is maintained despite change.

**Scale** asks where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.

These formulations do not divide living systems into components or establish a hierarchy of explanatory levels. Agency, Process, and Scale are complementary analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Viability and organised persistence identify related aspects and explanatory targets within that organisation.

This architecture allows APS to compare biological explanations without assuming that every phenomenon is explained by viability or that all biological concepts reduce to a single principle. Development, physiology, ecology, evolution, cognition, function, normativity, adaptation, and resilience retain their distinctive explananda and established explanatory resources.

The methodological question is instead whether making viability-relevant organisation explicit clarifies a particular explanandum, discriminates among alternatives, constrains inference, or otherwise adds explanatory work relative to the strongest relevant comparator. Such gain must be demonstrated rather than inferred from the integrative reach of the vocabulary itself.

Viability is therefore important within APS because it identifies a common persistence-relative concern without functioning as a universal explanatory answer.

## Why Viability Matters

Clarifying viability helps APS distinguish several questions that can otherwise become conflated.

First, it clarifies the difference between continued existence and continued functioning. A system can remain present while losing the organisation required for its future continuation, making survival alone an inadequate representation of viability.

Second, viability makes explicit the asymmetry underlying the APS account of biological normativity. Conditions and activities can support, undermine, or restore continued functioning, providing a naturalised basis for success and failure without requiring conscious evaluation.

Third, viability helps relate biological agency to persistence without inserting a causal bridge between them. Agency is already viability-oriented organisational activity, while organised persistence concerns how the living organisation enacted through such activity maintains continuity through change.

Fourth, viability directs attention to organism–world relations. The conditions relevant to continued functioning are not wholly internal to an organism; resources, constraints, perturbations, and opportunities can be materially organised through relations extending beyond its boundary.

Finally, viability provides a useful comparative question across biological domains: what conditions are relevant to continued functioning in this system, over this temporal extent, and for this explanandum?

These contributions make viability an important organising concept within APS. They do not establish that viability is a uniquely APS discovery or that invoking viability automatically improves an explanation. Organisational, autonomy, physiological, ecological, evolutionary, and other biological approaches already provide substantial explanatory resources concerning self-maintenance, regulation, persistence, and environmental dependence.

The scientific value of the APS treatment therefore depends upon what additional explanatory work it performs in particular comparisons.

## Conclusion

Living systems persist under conditions of continual material, developmental, physiological, and environmental change. Their continuity depends not upon remaining static but upon maintaining and re-establishing organisation compatible with continued functioning.

APS uses **viability** to identify the system-relative conditions relevant to that continued functioning.

Viability is not equivalent to survival, equilibrium, stability, fitness, or simple persistence. Nor is it an independent component inserted between biological agency and organised persistence. It belongs within the canonical APS account of Life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

This perspective clarifies several relationships within the framework. Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity. Organised persistence concerns the continuity of living organisation through change. Process asks how that continuity is maintained, while Scale asks where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents. Function, normativity, resilience, evaluation, and cognition raise further explanatory questions whose relations to viability must be specified rather than assumed.

Viability consequently occupies an important integrative position within APS without serving as a universal explanatory bridge. Its principal role is to make explicit the system-relative conditions in relation to which biological activity can support, undermine, or restore continued functioning.

That role also places viability under the same comparative discipline as other APS concepts. Its scientific contribution must be assessed relative to particular explanatory targets, relevant evidence, and the strongest established comparator.

Viability therefore helps APS formulate a central biological question with greater precision: **under changing conditions, what organisation and relations must be maintained or re-established for this living system to continue functioning as the organisation it is?**
