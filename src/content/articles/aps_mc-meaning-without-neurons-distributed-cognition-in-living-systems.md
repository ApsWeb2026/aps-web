**Where This Article Fits**

*This article addresses a specific downstream question within the APS account of cognition.*

*The APS cognition architecture first asks whether cognition is warranted at all. That question is governed by the APS cognition criterion and requires cognitive attribution to be distinguished from Agency, Evaluation, Significance, Integration, regulation, signalling, and other forms of biological organisation that may remain non-cognitive.*

*The present article begins only after that attribution has been independently warranted. Its concern is **implementation and localisation**: which material processes and relations realise the relevant cognitive organisation, across what spatial and temporal extents they operate, and whether describing that implementation as distributed makes an additional explanatory difference.*

*It therefore complements rather than replaces the APS articles establishing the cognition boundary, relating cognition to organised persistence, and examining cognition in particular biological domains.*

## The Localisation Problem

Cognition is often associated with specialised nervous systems and, especially, with brains. Neural organisation clearly provides powerful means of coordinating perception, memory, learning, evaluation, and action. But its importance does not settle a more general biological question: **where is the organisation that makes a cognitive capacity possible?**

This is a question of material implementation and explanatory localisation. Within APS, cognition is not identified by the presence or absence of a particular structure. It is attributed when integrated biological significance modulates activity across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities in ways not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation. That criterion specifies what must be established before cognitive attribution is warranted; it does not determine in advance which material structures must implement the relevant organisation.

The localisation problem therefore begins only after cognition has been independently warranted. It asks which biological processes, structures, constraints, and relations realise the cognitive organisation under investigation, across what spatial and temporal extents they operate, and whether tracing their distribution makes a scientifically consequential difference to the explanation.

## Non-Neural Does Not Mean Cognitive

Nothing in the APS cognition criterion requires cognitive organisation to be realised by neurons. A nervous system may provide mechanisms through which cognition is implemented without nervous tissue thereby becoming part of the definition of cognition. APS therefore does not treat neurality as a necessary condition of cognition.

The converse inference must be resisted just as carefully. Absence of neurons does not establish cognition. Non-neural living systems can exhibit sensitivity to environmental differences, viability-oriented regulation, signalling, physiological and developmental plasticity, retained effects of previous conditions, and complex coordination among biological processes. These phenomena may be scientifically important without being cognitive.

Non-neurality therefore has no privileged evidential status. It neither excludes cognition nor establishes it. What matters is the organisation actually present and the evidence supporting its attribution.

## Cognition Must Be Established First

This distinction follows from the broader APS cognition architecture. Living systems exhibit Agency: viability-oriented activity through which the conditions of organised persistence are sustained. Biological Evaluation establishes viability-relative asymmetries among conditions; Significance concerns the viability-relative relevance of those evaluated differences to continued organisation; and Integration brings multiple significant relations into functional coordination.

None of these, individually or jointly, is sufficient for cognition.

The operative APS criterion remains:

> **Biological agency becomes cognitive when integrated biological significance modulates activity across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities in ways not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation.**

This boundary becomes particularly important when cognition is described as distributed. Distribution cannot convert a non-cognitive process into a cognitive one. A regulatory system does not become cognitive merely because its regulation extends across many cells, tissues, processes, or environmental relations, and extensive signalling does not establish cognition merely because information passes among spatially separated parts of a living system.

The explanatory order must therefore be preserved:

**cognitive attribution → material implementation → possible distributed implementation**

rather than:

**distributed biological organisation → cognition**.

## From Cognitive Attribution to Material Implementation

Once cognition is independently warranted, the next question is how the relevant cognitive organisation is materially realised. Cognition cannot perform explanatory work as an abstract capacity detached from biological organisation: whatever makes a cognitive capacity possible must be implemented through material processes and relations within the living system.

Different cognitive phenomena need not depend upon the same mechanisms. Relevant organisation may involve neural circuitry, biochemical networks, physiological states, morphological structures, signalling relations, retained effects of previous activity, organism–environment coupling, or combinations of such processes. APS neither infers cognition from any of these nor requires every cognitive system to instantiate a common cognitive mechanism.

The requirement is instead that proposed cognitive organisation be open to investigation in terms of its material realisation. This separates two questions that are easily conflated: **is cognition warranted in this case, and what materially implements the cognitive organisation that has been warranted?** The first is a cognition-boundary question. The second is an implementation question. The present article concerns the second.

## What Would “Distributed” Mean?

Biological organisation is commonly realised across more than one spatial or temporal extent. Processes within cells participate in wider cellular and tissue organisation; physiological activity can depend upon relations among spatially separated structures; present activity may depend upon retained consequences of earlier conditions; and organismal activity may depend upon continuing relations with environmental circumstances.

Yet calling cognitive implementation *distributed* should require more than observing that many things are involved. A distributed-implementation hypothesis becomes relevant where adequately explaining the warranted cognitive organisation requires materially realised relations among processes that cannot be satisfactorily localised to a single privileged component or narrowly bounded subsystem. The relevant distribution may be spatial, temporal, or both.

This does not mean that scales themselves interact or cause cognitive activity. The causal and organisational work is performed by material processes, structures, constraints, and relations. Scale, in the APS sense, identifies the spatial and temporal extents across which those relations must be followed for the explanatory problem at issue.

Nor does distributed implementation imply that every participating component is itself cognitive. A cognitive capacity attributed to an organism need not imply cognition in each cell, tissue, signalling pathway, environmental relation, or other component contributing to its implementation. **Attribution and implementation must remain distinct.**

## Distributed Regulation Is Not Yet Distributed Cognition

The distinction matters because distributed biological regulation is widespread. Living organisation commonly depends upon coordinated relations among multiple processes rather than upon a single controller. Signalling, physiological coordination, developmental regulation, morphological change, retained effects of prior conditions, and environmentally responsive activity can all extend through biological organisation.

Such phenomena may contribute to cognitive organisation where cognition has independently been established, but they do not establish it. A system may coordinate many signals while remaining adequately explained as a regulatory system. It may alter later activity because of earlier conditions without organising present activity across a cognitively relevant field of possibilities. Complex collective behaviour likewise does not establish that the collective itself satisfies the criterion for cognitive attribution.

For this reason, phenomena such as cellular gradient response, plant signalling, or microbial collective regulation cannot be classified as cognition merely because they display distributed sensitivity and coordination. The evidence must establish the organisation required for cognition and then identify how the distributed processes contribute to its implementation.

## When Does Distributed Implementation Matter Explanatorily?

Even where cognition and distributed implementation are both warranted, a further question remains: **does describing the cognition as distributed improve the explanation?**

Distribution is not automatically explanatory gain. It becomes explanatorily consequential where tracing distributed implementation identifies relevant dependencies omitted by a more localised account, changes warranted inference, distinguishes cases otherwise conflated, reveals constraints on cognitive organisation, exposes an empirical gap, or generates a discriminating research question.

These are possible consequences to be demonstrated rather than benefits that follow merely from adopting distributed terminology. A wider description is not necessarily a better explanation. If all scientifically consequential explanatory work can be recovered without relevant loss by a more localised account, expanding the explanatory boundary has not demonstrated additional explanatory gain.

The relevant extent must therefore be determined by the explanandum rather than by a prior preference for either localised or distributed explanation.

## The Comparator Test

Claims about distributed cognition consequently require comparison, but the appropriate comparator depends upon the question being asked.

Where **cognitive attribution itself is at issue**, the relevant comparison is with the strongest adequate non-cognitive explanation. Distributed signalling, physiological coordination, plasticity, retained effects of previous conditions, or flexible regulation may all be explicable without cognition. If an adequate non-cognitive account recovers the phenomenon without relevant explanatory loss, cognitive terminology has not yet demonstrated an additional contribution.

Where **cognition has already been independently warranted**, the question changes. The relevant comparison may now concern alternative accounts of its material implementation. Does a proposed distributed account identify scientifically consequential relations omitted by a more localised explanation? If not, distribution may remain a useful description of implementation without constituting an additional explanatory contribution.

The resulting diagnostic sequence is:

**Is cognition present?**  
Compare the cognitive interpretation with the strongest adequate non-cognitive explanation.

**How is warranted cognition implemented?**  
Identify and compare the material organisation relevant to the cognitive capacity.

**Does distributed implementation matter explanatorily?**  
Determine whether tracing the wider organisation changes explanation, inference, discrimination, constraint, gap detection, or research practice.

The comparator is therefore target-dependent. These questions should not be collapsed into a single test, and “distributed cognition” should not function as a conclusion built into its terminology.

## Representation and Consciousness Are Separate Questions

Questions about distributed implementation must also remain separate from questions about representation, sentience, and consciousness.

Some cognitive phenomena may involve representational organisation. Others may be adequately explained through embodied dispositions, retained physiological organisation, distributed control, or ongoing organism–environment relations. Representation therefore requires its own explanatory warrant: it is neither established nor excluded by showing that cognitive implementation is distributed.

The same separation applies to sentience and consciousness. Cognitive organisation does not by itself establish subjective experience, and distributed cognitive implementation would provide no independent evidence that subjective experience is present. Conversely, absence of evidence for consciousness does not settle whether cognition is present.

The explanatory questions must therefore remain distinct: whether cognition is warranted; how it is materially implemented; whether that implementation is distributed; whether representation contributes to the phenomenon; and whether subjective experience is present. Answering one does not automatically answer the others.

## What APS Can — and Cannot — Claim

APS permits cognition to be investigated without making nervous systems constitutive of the concept. It therefore leaves open the possibility that some cognitive organisation is implemented through non-neural processes and that, in particular cases, its material realisation may extend across multiple spatial and temporal extents.

But possibility is not attribution. APS cannot infer cognition from non-neurality, signalling, regulation, information processing, biological significance, plasticity, coordination, complexity, or distributed organisation alone. Nor can cognition attributed at one organisational extent automatically be transferred to every component or larger system involved in its implementation.

A claim of distributed cognition therefore carries a sequence of evidential burdens:

**cognitive warrant → material implementation → demonstrated distribution → comparative explanatory consequence**

Cognition must first be independently warranted. The material organisation implementing it must then be identified. Its proposed distribution must be demonstrated rather than presumed. If distributed implementation is claimed to improve explanation, that contribution must finally be tested against the strongest adequate alternative.

Distributed cognition consequently has a narrower and more demanding role within APS than the phrase can sometimes suggest. It is not a declaration that cognition occurs wherever biological organisation is distributed. It identifies an empirical and explanatory problem: **where is warranted cognitive organisation materially realised, which relations are necessary to its implementation, and what difference does tracing those relations make to biological explanation?**

### Position within APS

This article is an extension within the Cognition and Mind cluster. It does not independently redefine cognition or determine whether cognition is present. That question remains governed by the APS cognition criterion:

> **Biological agency becomes cognitive when integrated biological significance modulates activity across a temporal field of viability-relevant possibilities in ways not exhausted by immediate or fixed regulation.**

The present article begins downstream of that criterion. Its explanatory sequence is:

**Cognitive warrant → Material implementation → Organisational distribution → Explanatory consequence → Comparator test**

This sequence is not a chronology, developmental succession, evolutionary progression, hierarchy, or sequence of levels. Nor may it be reversed. Distributed organisation does not establish cognition, and distributed regulation does not become distributed cognition merely because it is complex, biologically consequential, non-neural, or extended across multiple spatial and temporal extents.

Scale has a specific role in this architecture. It identifies the spatial and temporal extents across which materially realised cognitive dependencies must be followed for the explanandum at issue. Scale is not itself a mechanism or causal agent, and cognition attributed at one organisational extent cannot automatically be transferred to the components or larger systems involved in its implementation.

A claim of distributed cognition is warranted only to the extent that the relevant dependencies in this explanatory sequence are independently supported.

### Corpus Significance

This article is the principal APS treatment of the **implementation and localisation of independently warranted cognition**. Its corpus function is not to establish cognition as a general property of living systems, nor to identify distributed biological organisation with distributed cognition. It addresses the subsequent question of where warranted cognitive organisation is materially realised and when its distribution matters to explanation.

The article therefore connects the APS Cognition and Mind corpus with the framework's treatment of Scale and comparative explanatory methodology. It establishes a controlled route from cognitive attribution to material implementation and from material implementation to possible distributed organisation, while requiring any claimed explanatory contribution to survive comparison with the strongest adequate alternative.

Its governing architecture is therefore:

**Cognitive warrant → Material implementation → Organisational distribution → Explanatory consequence → Comparator test**
