**Where This Article Fits**

*This Clarification asks when saying what a biological activity is for helps to explain it. [*Teleonomy — Historical Solution and APS Reinterpretation*](/articles/teleonomy/) traces how biologists have used *teleonomy* to discuss goal-directed activity. [*Function and Normativity — Why Biological Organisation Matters*](/articles/function-and-normativity-why-biological-organisation-matters/) *explains what warrants attributing a function and how that function can remain identifiable when its performance is impaired. This article applies the APS accounts of agency, function, and normativity to physiological regulation, then asks what further evidence is needed to explain the direction of development.*

## Introduction

When soil dries, a plant may close its stomata. Saying that the stomata close *to conserve water* sounds explanatory, but the statement can do several things. It can identify an effect of closure, suggest why that effect matters to the plant under water stress, or imply that past selection favoured the capacity to close because it reduced water loss. The same words can therefore carry different evidential commitments. Closure can also restrict carbon dioxide uptake, so even a measurable reduction in water loss leaves open how much the response contributes to the plant’s viability under particular conditions (Joshi et al., 2022).

Teleology, in its broad biological sense, concerns explanation in terms of ends, purposes, or functions. The question is what warrants that form of explanation. In APS, the starting point is the activity through which a living system contributes to sustaining its own conditions of existence. Applying that idea requires showing how a particular activity has that effect and identifying the system and timescale for which the effect matters.

## From Ends to Explanatory Questions

In *Parts of Animals*, Aristotle asked how the structures and activities of an animal could be understood in relation to what they do in its life (Aristotle, 1937, Book I). For Aristotle, such ends belonged to the organisation and activities of living beings themselves. Kant later described organisms as natural purposes, drawing attention to the way their parts contribute to the whole and depend on the whole for their production and maintenance. Contemporary organisational accounts take up this reciprocal relation and ask how it is realised in living systems (Cuciniello, 2026).

The twentieth-century vocabulary of teleonomy served several purposes within naturalistic biology. Pittendrigh (1958) introduced the term in discussing adaptation and behaviour. Mayr (1974) used it to analyse processes directed by a programme, while Monod (1971) connected teleonomy to the preservation and transmission of living organisation. These uses gave the term different explanatory roles. Dresow and Love (2023) draw on this history to question its value as a general replacement for *teleology*. In a particular case, the evidential requirements depend on whether the question concerns how an activity occurs, what it contributes, or how the capacity arose and was retained.

## Stomatal Closure as a Worked Example

A mechanistic investigation asks how water stress alters stomatal aperture. It can trace how changes in the leaf and its surroundings affect guard-cell activity and how changes in guard-cell turgor alter aperture and gas exchange. This account identifies the causal route by which closure occurs.

An organisational investigation asks whether reduced water loss helps the plant maintain water transport and tissue function under specified conditions, and how this contribution relates to the costs of restricting carbon dioxide uptake. Using data from 18 species, Joshi et al. (2022) tested a model that predicts simultaneous declines in stomatal conductance, carbon assimilation, and photosynthetic capacity during progressive soil drought. To assess a contribution to viability in a particular case, an investigation would compare the consequences of different stomatal responses under comparable conditions and follow them long enough to capture the relevant effects on water balance and carbon assimilation. A change in gas exchange can be detected at the leaf, while its significance for the whole plant may depend on how long the stress lasts and how the plant recovers.

An evolutionary investigation asks whether selection favoured the capacity to close because of an effect such as water conservation. Historical accounts of function give explanatory weight to an effect’s role in the origin or retention of the trait or capacity that produces it (Wright, 1973; Millikan, 1984). A selected-effects claim about stomatal closure would require historical evidence linking the effect of heritable differences in the response to differential survival or reproduction. Present physiological performance can inform this inquiry, and the two claims remain independently assessable.

The phrase *to conserve water* can thus identify a contribution that makes the regulated response intelligible in the life of the plant. The explanation becomes stronger when it shows how the response changes water loss and why that change matters to the plant under the conditions at issue.

## Purposiveness and Normativity in APS

[[box:aps-box-what-aps-adds-to-contemporary-biology]]

APS characterises life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Viability concerns the conditions under which a particular system can continue as a living organisation. Constraint closure concerns the reciprocal dependence of constraints that shape processes and are maintained through processes shaped by other constraints in the organisation. These dependencies coexist with ongoing exchanges of matter and energy with the surroundings. Biological agency is the viability-oriented activity of living organisation, and organised persistence is its continuity through change. This formulation develops the published APS account of agency as the defining activity of life (Spencer, 2026).

A plant’s capacity to regulate stomatal aperture depends on an organisation that keeps its tissues functional and responsive. Under some conditions, the resulting change in gas exchange helps sustain that organisation. Each direction of dependence can be investigated through present causal relations. The end invoked in a purposive explanation is an outcome that the activity tends to bring about under specified conditions.

In APS, purpose concerns how living activity is organised relative to viability, while function concerns a particular contribution within that organisation. In the plant, the adjustment of gas exchange as conditions change illustrates purposive regulation. Limiting water loss is a specific functional contribution of stomatal closure under water stress.

APS uses *evaluation* for the organism’s active differentiation of conditions through responses that bear on its viability. Stomatal closure may be adequate, inadequate, or costly in relation to the plant’s water balance and carbon requirements. Observing closure establishes that a response occurred. Assessing its adequacy requires evidence of its consequences for the plant over the relevant interval. The water-regulating function of stomatal closure can remain attributable when a particular response performs that role inadequately. Reduced carbon uptake may also be a cost of effective water regulation, so assessing the response requires tracing both effects under the conditions at issue. Biological normativity concerns these system-relative differences between activities and conditions that sustain, undermine, or restore viability.

## Organisational Teleology and Regulatory Control

The APS account shares substantial ground with biological autonomy theory (Moreno & Mossio, 2015). Mossio and Bich (2017) argue that biological organisation is teleological because a living system’s activity contributes to producing and maintaining the conditions that make that activity possible. Bich (2024) gives regulatory control a more prominent role by examining how specialised constraints modulate ongoing processes in response to internal and external conditions. The stomatal example illustrates the distinction. Identifying the reciprocal dependencies that sustain the plant and explaining how it modulates gas exchange during drought are related causal tasks.

APS makes the relation among agency, process, and scale explicit by asking whose viability is at issue, which processes affect it, and over what interval the effect holds. A change in aperture at the leaf surface can have consequences for the whole plant, and those consequences can differ between brief water stress and prolonged drought. A claim that the APS formulation advances the organisational explanation would require identifying a causal relation or consequence that existing accounts leave unexplained.

## From Physiological Regulation to Development

A developing organism must sustain viable organisation through change. A seedling, for example, must remain viable as its dependence on stored reserves changes and its photosynthetic capacity develops. The conditions that sustain it during this transition help explain which trajectories are viable. Explaining the direction of a particular trajectory also requires tracing the processes and constraints that generate tissue differentiation, establish photosynthetic capacity, and shape the range of outcomes under different conditions.

Rama (2026) argues that accounts of physiological autonomy leave open how developmental trajectories acquire their direction. Cuciniello (2026) examines both the compatibility of organisational teleology with mechanistic explanation and the difficulty of extending it to development. In APS terms, the developmental problem concerns both continuity of viability and transformation of the organisation that sustains it. Assessing these claims requires attention to the developmental stage and the timescale of the transition.

## Conclusion

A biological statement about what an activity is *for* gains explanatory force when it identifies a causal relation and specifies the conditions under which that relation holds. An organisational account connects an activity to the viable persistence of the system that performs it. A selected-effects account traces an effect’s role in the evolutionary history of the capacity, and a developmental account traces the processes that generate a trajectory. Where these questions concern the same activity, their answers can show how it operates, why it matters now, and how the capacity came to be there.

### Central Question

When does saying what a biological activity is for help explain it, and what evidence supports that explanation?

### Architectural Role

This Clarification uses physiological regulation to distinguish the mechanism of an activity, its contribution to viability, and the evolutionary history of the capacity that produces it.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

The APS argument depends on viability, constraint closure, biological agency, and organised persistence. Aristotle and Kant supply historical antecedents for thinking about natural ends. Contemporary organisational theories provide the closest conceptual comparison.

### Related Explanatory Developments

### Related Explanatory Questions

What evidence distinguishes a current organisational function from a selected effect? When does a response at one scale support viability at another? What determines the direction of a viable developmental transition?

### Position Within APS

Agency concerns viability-oriented organisational activity. Process concerns the maintenance and transformation of that organisation. Scale identifies the system, level of organisation, and interval relevant to a purposive claim.
