**Where This Article Fits**

*Cultural Organisation* *is a Core article within the Social Organisation topic of APS. Its central question is what constitutes cultural organisation and how socially distributed practices, relationships, meanings, and expectations become sufficiently coordinated to support continuing forms of collective life.*

*The article builds upon the APS accounts of communication, coordination, normativity, symbolic activity, and organised persistence. It examines how these processes contribute to cultural arrangements that extend beyond particular individuals or interactions.*

*Its relationship with **Cultural Inheritance** is especially important. Cultural organisation concerns the constitution and maintenance of socially distributed organisation, whereas cultural inheritance concerns the processes through which cultural practices and resources are transmitted, reconstructed, and transformed across individuals and generations. These questions are related but not interchangeable.*

*The present article also connects with **Institutions** and **Technology**, which examine particular arrangements through which cultural organisation becomes stabilised, materially extended, and transformed.*

*APS offers an organisational interpretation of culture rather than a replacement for established anthropological, sociological, or cultural evolutionary explanations. Its explanatory value must be assessed in relation to the phenomena and competing accounts under investigation.*

## Introduction

Culture is among the most influential and widely discussed concepts in the human sciences. It is commonly understood through beliefs, values, customs, traditions, symbols, practices, and forms of knowledge shared within communities. Such descriptions identify important features of cultural life, but they do not always specify how these features become organised into continuing patterns of social activity. Culture is not merely a collection of things that individuals possess. It is also expressed through what individuals do together, how they coordinate their activities, how they acquire and interpret shared expectations, and how established practices shape subsequent participation.

A language, for example, is more than a collection of words or grammatical rules. It is sustained through speakers whose communicative activities reproduce and modify shared conventions. A scientific tradition is more than a body of propositions: it involves practices of investigation, training, criticism, publication, and collective evaluation. A craft tradition similarly involves not only technical knowledge but also materials, tools, skills, and relationships through which competent performance becomes possible. These examples suggest an organisational dimension of culture that cannot be adequately represented by an inventory of cultural contents alone.

The organisational dimension of culture has substantial antecedents in established scholarship. Dewey (1922) examined conduct through habits and their social formation, while Geertz (1973) developed an interpretive understanding of culture centred upon publicly meaningful symbolic activity. These approaches differ in their explanatory commitments, but both resist treating culture simply as an inventory of independently existing items. Cultural evolutionary research, including Boyd and Richerson (1985), has additionally examined the processes through which socially acquired variation is transmitted and transformed. APS therefore enters an already developed field of explanation rather than identifying a previously unrecognised phenomenon.

APS approaches culture through its broader concern with organised persistence: how materially realised systems sustain recognisable patterns of activity through change. Within the social domain, this question concerns relationships among organisms whose coordinated activities generate forms of continuity extending beyond particular individuals and encounters. APS interprets culture as a socially distributed form of organised persistence, directing attention to the relationships through which practices become established, maintained, modified, and sometimes lost.

This interpretation does not imply that all culture serves biological viability, that every cultural arrangement is adaptive, or that cultural evolution necessarily produces increasingly complex or successful organisation. Nor does it imply that cultural organisation is itself a living organism. Its purpose is to identify an explanatory question concerning the constitution and maintenance of cultural arrangements.

A central distinction follows between cultural organisation and cultural inheritance. Cultural organisation concerns the arrangement of activities and relationships through which cultural life is sustained. Cultural inheritance concerns the processes through which cultural practices and resources become available to successive participants and generations. These questions are closely connected, but they are not identical. The present article concentrates upon the first.

## Cultural Organisation and the Problems of Social Life

Social life presents organisational problems that cannot always be resolved through isolated individual activity. Organisms frequently depend upon interactions with others for obtaining resources, avoiding danger, reproducing, raising offspring, acquiring skills, and responding to changing environments. Coordination can make such activities possible, but it is neither automatic nor necessarily stable. Individuals differ in their behaviour, capacities, experience, and interests; social relationships change, environmental conditions fluctuate, and participants enter and leave groups. Practices effective in one context may become inappropriate in another. Continuing social activity therefore depends upon processes through which relationships and expectations can be established, sustained, and revised despite these differences.

Culture can contribute to such organisation by making socially acquired practices, expectations, and capacities available within continuing relationships. Individuals need not independently discover every appropriate response or negotiate every convention anew. They can participate in activities shaped by earlier experience, learning established procedures while also interpreting and modifying them. A community may possess methods of obtaining food, caring for dependants, distributing responsibilities, resolving disputes, or communicating relevant information. Such arrangements can support coordination because participants acquire sufficiently compatible expectations and practical capacities.

This interpretation has affinities with Dewey's (1922) account of habit as socially situated conduct. Habits are not adequately understood as isolated behavioural repetitions detached from the circumstances in which they operate. They connect individual capacities with established ways of acting, making possible forms of conduct that depend upon prior social experience. APS places particular emphasis upon the organisational relationships through which such practices become collectively sustained, without claiming that this emphasis replaces Dewey's philosophical account.

Culture should not, however, be explained solely through its benefits. Cultural practices may be inefficient, exclusionary, costly, or maladaptive. They may persist because of authority, conformity, historical contingency, or the difficulty of replacing established arrangements. Established coordination may advantage some participants while imposing substantial costs upon others. The existence of a cultural practice therefore does not establish that it improves biological viability or collective welfare.

APS treats culture as an organisational phenomenon requiring explanation, not as an automatic solution to every problem of social life. The relevant questions are how particular practices and relationships become organised, what sustains them, whose activities they enable or constrain, and what consequences follow from their continuation or transformation.

## From Symbolic Coordination to Culture

Communication and coordination provide important conditions for cultural organisation. Organisms influence one another through signals, movements, interactions, and responses that affect their subsequent activities. Repeated interactions may establish relatively stable patterns of coordination, while social learning can allow individuals to acquire practices through observing and participating with others. In some social systems, such learning produces behavioural traditions that remain recognisable across multiple participants or generations.

Human cultural organisation often extends these possibilities through language, symbolic representation, explicit teaching, normative expectations, and institutional arrangements. Symbolic systems allow participants to coordinate activities in relation to meanings that are not confined to immediate circumstances. They can refer to absent objects, remembered events, anticipated consequences, imagined possibilities, and shared classifications. Language, writing, numerical systems, and other symbolic resources can consequently extend the scope, precision, and historical continuity of cultural organisation.

Geertz's (1973) interpretive anthropology is an important comparator because it treats culture in relation to publicly meaningful symbolic forms. From this perspective, cultural practices cannot be adequately explained without attending to what actions and symbols mean to participants. An organisational interpretation must not erase this interpretive dimension by redescribing meanings solely as mechanisms of coordination. The significance of a ritual, for example, cannot be inferred simply from its capacity to reproduce a stable pattern of collective activity. Its interpretation requires attention to historically situated understandings, distinctions, and relationships.

APS can examine how meanings participate in the organisation of activity, but it does not thereby replace interpretive analysis. Organisational and interpretive questions may be complementary: one concerns how relationships and practices are sustained, while the other concerns the meanings through which participants understand and enact them. Whether their integration yields additional explanation depends upon the particular case.

Symbolic capacity should not, moreover, be treated as a universal prerequisite for culture. Research on socially learned traditions in non-human animals indicates that culturally relevant behavioural continuity can occur without the elaborate symbolic capacities characteristic of human societies. Communication, social learning, symbolic activity, and culture therefore do not form a single necessary developmental sequence. Different organisms and social systems may exhibit different combinations of these capacities.

APS accommodates this diversity by examining the materially realised relationships through which particular forms of socially learned organisation are established and maintained. Symbolic coordination remains especially important for understanding human culture, but its explanatory significance must be specified rather than assumed to define culture universally.

## Culture as Organised Persistence

The central APS interpretation is that culture can be understood as socially distributed organised persistence. This does not mean that culture is an independent organism or that a cultural system necessarily possesses the autonomy of a living individual. Rather, culture involves patterns of activity and relationships maintained through the participation of living organisms and the material arrangements supporting their interactions.

Such organisation may include learned practices, communication systems, shared expectations, technical skills, normative relationships, symbolic conventions, and institutions. These elements are not simply assembled into a static structure; they acquire cultural significance through their participation in continuing activities. A legal convention exists through practices of interpretation, recognition, enforcement, contestation, and revision. A scientific method persists through its use in investigation, training, and criticism. A social norm is maintained through expectations and responses that influence participation.

The relevant organisation is therefore processual. It depends upon activities through which relationships are repeatedly enacted, sustained, and modified. This perspective is compatible with the wider APS account of Process as organised continuity through change. The components of a cultural arrangement may change while some of its relationships remain sufficiently stable for the arrangement to retain a recognisable identity.

Processual interpretations are not distinctive to APS. The philosophical approaches assembled by Nicholson and Dupré (2018), for example, examine biological entities and their persistence in relation to continuing processes rather than treating stable entities as ontologically primary. APS draws upon a related concern with continuity through change, while applying an organisational question to cultural arrangements. This is an extension of explanatory attention, not evidence that cultural organisation has been derived from biological process ontology.

The distinction is important because the conditions governing the persistence of a cultural tradition are not necessarily those governing the continued life of an organism. A scientific institution may persist through the replacement of its members, whereas an organism's continued existence depends upon a particular kind of viability-oriented organisation. Similarities in the language of persistence must not conceal differences in the organisation and causal relations involved.

Cultural continuity is not guaranteed. Organisation may fragment when relationships cease to support coordinated activity. Practices may lose their significance when the circumstances sustaining them disappear, and institutions may persist formally while their actual functions change. APS therefore distinguishes the persistence of cultural organisation from the mere survival of a name, symbol, artefact, or institutional form.

The explanatory question is which relationships remain operative, how they are sustained, and what changes when those relationships are transformed. This interpretation directs attention to culture as activity and organisation rather than simply as an inventory of cultural contents. It does not establish that organisational explanations are always superior to anthropological, sociological, or evolutionary accounts. Their value depends upon the explanatory problem, the available evidence, and the strength of the alternatives.

## Cultural Organisation and Social Learning

Social learning contributes to cultural organisation by allowing individuals to acquire practices and capacities through interaction with others. Through observation, participation, instruction, imitation, and other learning processes, individuals become competent participants in established forms of social activity. Its organisational importance lies in the possibility of producing sufficiently coordinated capacities among individuals whose histories, experiences, and contributions differ.

A community of practitioners may share methods, standards, and expectations even though no two members have learned or performed them in exactly the same way. Such coordination does not require perfect behavioural uniformity. Participants may differ in their skills, interpretations, and responsibilities while remaining capable of contributing to a continuing cultural arrangement.

The importance of socially supported learning is developed in Sterelny's (2012) account of human apprenticeship and the evolution of learning environments. Henrich (2016) similarly emphasises the significance of social learning and cumulative cultural processes in human evolutionary success. These approaches identify mechanisms and historical consequences that an APS account of cultural organisation cannot simply assume or replace. They also demonstrate why the organisation of learning environments, the acquisition of individual capacities, and the transmission of cultural information need to be distinguished analytically, even when they are closely interconnected in practice.

Learning can transform organisation as well as reproduce it. New participants may modify practices, challenge expectations, introduce alternative methods, or combine previously separate traditions. The result may be greater coordination, new forms of activity, fragmentation, or the disappearance of established arrangements. Cultural continuity consequently involves variable combinations of reproduction and change, not the exact repetition of inherited behaviour.

Boyd and Richerson (1985) provide an especially important comparator through their analysis of cultural transmission and evolutionary dynamics. Their approach examines how culturally acquired variation changes within populations through identifiable processes. The APS distinction between organisation and inheritance should not be understood as a rejection of this work. Rather, it separates a question about the constitution and operation of a cultural arrangement from questions about how cultural variants are transmitted, modified, and distributed.

This distinction can be useful when a cultural arrangement persists despite substantial changes in its particular contents. It directs investigation towards the relationships that sustain coordinated participation, while leaving the detailed mechanisms of transmission and evolutionary change to the appropriate explanatory accounts.

Those mechanisms belong more directly to **Cultural Inheritance**. The present concern is how socially acquired capacities contribute to the constitution and maintenance of cultural organisation.

## Culture Across Scales

Cultural organisation extends across different spatial, temporal, and social scales. At relatively local scales, cultural practices may be maintained through recurring interactions among families, peers, workplaces, or small communities. At wider scales, languages, institutions, economic arrangements, religious traditions, educational systems, and technological infrastructures may coordinate activities among people who never interact directly. Some cultural arrangements remain localised, while others extend across populations and persist through long periods of historical change.

These differences matter because the relationships sustaining cultural organisation are not identical at every scale. Face-to-face coordination may depend heavily upon direct observation, familiarity, and immediate feedback. Larger arrangements may require formal rules, records, communication technologies, specialised institutions, and distributed responsibilities. The organisation of a scientific community, for example, involves local practices of research and training alongside wider systems of publication, criticism, professional recognition, and institutional support.

Neither the local nor the wider arrangement can automatically be treated as the privileged level of explanation. APS treats Scale as a question concerning the spatial and temporal extent of the relationships relevant to the phenomenon under investigation. A study of how a particular skill is learned may concentrate upon local interaction, whereas an explanation of how a scientific discipline persists may require attention to institutions, communication networks, and historical continuity.

These are differences in explanatory requirements, not necessarily differences between independently existing biological levels. The material organisation of a practice must be investigated rather than inferred from the scale at which it is described.

Cultural organisation is therefore multiscale in a practical explanatory sense. Its investigation may require movement between interactions, institutions, populations, and historical processes, but it does not follow that every cultural phenomenon belongs within a fixed hierarchy of levels or that wider-scale descriptions automatically possess greater explanatory power.

## Cultural Organisation and Collective Memory

Collective memory is one of the ways cultural organisation connects present activity with earlier experience. Communities preserve access to the past through narratives, rituals, records, classifications, commemorations, practical traditions, and educational activities. Such resources shape how participants understand their circumstances and coordinate their actions.

Collective memory is more than the passive retention of information. Remembered material acquires significance through the activities in which it is interpreted, communicated, contested, and used. A historical record may survive for centuries, but its cultural significance depends upon practices through which it is read and understood. A tradition may remain recognisable while its meaning changes substantially.

Collective memory is consequently an organised activity rather than a fixed repository. Its continuity depends upon relationships among participants, practices, artefacts, and institutions, including the changing circumstances in which inherited representations are made relevant to present concerns.

Memory may also be selective or contested. Communities can preserve some interpretations while neglecting others. Shared memories may contribute to cooperation, but they can also sustain divisions, exclusion, or inaccurate understandings of the past. APS therefore does not equate collective memory with reliable knowledge or beneficial continuity.

Its organisational concern is how particular forms of historically informed activity are sustained and transformed. The transmission and reconstruction processes through which collective memory passes between participants and generations are examined more directly in **Cultural Inheritance**.

## Cultural Organisation and Institutions

Institutions are important forms of cultural organisation because they establish relatively durable arrangements of roles, expectations, procedures, and responsibilities. Educational, legal, scientific, economic, and political institutions coordinate activities that cannot ordinarily be sustained through isolated individual decisions. They frequently depend upon shared understandings concerning appropriate conduct, authority, obligation, evidence, competence, and participation. These expectations are culturally established and maintained through continuing practices.

An educational institution, for example, is not constituted solely by its buildings or formal rules. It depends upon teaching, learning, assessment, administration, and the recognition of roles and responsibilities. A scientific institution similarly depends upon practices of investigation, criticism, communication, and collective evaluation. Institutions may therefore be understood as relatively stabilised arrangements of cultural organisation, maintained through relationships that extend beyond particular participants.

Institutional continuity does not necessarily indicate organisational success. Institutions can preserve ineffective procedures, reproduce inequalities, resist necessary change, or continue formally after their original functions have altered. Their persistence requires explanation, but persistence alone is not a measure of value.

APS asks how institutional relationships are materially and socially sustained, what activities they enable or constrain, and how they change under different circumstances. It does not follow that institutional organisation is equivalent to the viability-oriented organisation of a living organism, or that institutional persistence establishes a form of autonomous collective biological agency.

The more detailed treatment of institutional authority, stabilisation, and historical transformation belongs to **Institutions**.

## Cultural Organisation and Technology

Technology both depends upon and transforms cultural organisation. Tools, artefacts, infrastructures, and technical systems can make coordinated activities possible by extending human capacities and stabilising practical relationships. Writing allows symbolic material to remain available beyond immediate communication; printing expands the distribution of texts; measurement systems support reproducible procedures; and digital networks alter the speed, scale, and structure of communication. Such technologies can become integral components of cultural organisation.

They do not merely transmit independently existing cultural contents. They can change how activities are performed, which relationships are possible, and how participants coordinate their actions. A scientific discipline organised around laboratory instruments, databases, and publication systems differs materially from one dependent upon direct observation and local correspondence. Technological arrangements can therefore transform the organisation of knowledge itself.

The relationship is reciprocal. Technologies arise within existing social practices and institutional arrangements, while their adoption may alter the distribution of skills, authority, information, and responsibility. Their significance cannot be established simply by describing their technical capacities independently of the activities in which they operate.

Technological change does not necessarily produce improvement. New systems may disrupt established skills, increase dependence upon fragile infrastructures, concentrate control, or alter the conditions under which cultural practices remain sustainable. Technologies can preserve some forms of cultural activity while contributing to the disappearance of others.

APS consequently examines technology as part of the material organisation of cultural life rather than as an external addition to culture. The processes through which technical knowledge and practices are transmitted across generations are treated in **Cultural Inheritance**. The wider organisation and consequences of technological systems are examined in **Technology**.

## Why Culture Matters

Culture matters because it enables forms of coordinated activity that extend beyond the capacities and immediate experiences of particular individuals. Through socially acquired practices, expectations, meanings, and material arrangements, individuals participate in organised activities established through earlier historical experience. Culture can support cooperation, collective learning, specialised work, institutional continuity, and the accumulation of knowledge. It can also sustain conflict, exclusion, ineffective practices, and arrangements that impose substantial costs upon individuals or populations.

An adequate account must therefore explain both the capacities made possible by cultural organisation and the circumstances under which those capacities become constrained or transformed. APS approaches these questions through organised persistence, concentrating upon the relationships through which cultural practices become organised into continuing forms of social activity.

This emphasis helps distinguish cultural organisation from individual beliefs, isolated acts of communication, or the mere possession of cultural artefacts. It also distinguishes the organisation of culture from the processes of cultural inheritance. The first concerns what is organised and how that organisation operates; the second concerns how cultural resources are transmitted, reconstructed, and transformed across participants and generations.

The distinction may provide conceptual clarification when the explanatory problem concerns the persistence of a practice or institution despite changes in its participants and particular contents. It can direct attention to relationships that might otherwise remain implicit. Yet this is a possible contribution, not a demonstration of superiority over existing accounts.

Interpretive anthropology already examines the meanings through which cultural practices are constituted and understood. Cultural evolutionary theory examines mechanisms of transmission and population-level change. Accounts of habit, social practice, and situated learning examine relationships between individual capacities and established forms of activity. These traditions offer substantial explanatory resources, and their differences cannot be resolved simply by placing them within APS terminology.

The value of an APS organisational interpretation must therefore be established through particular comparisons. It would need to show that distinguishing organisation from inheritance, or identifying materially sustained relationships across relevant scales, clarifies a genuine explanatory difficulty, improves the integration of evidence, or supports an inference not adequately supplied by the strongest available alternative.

Until such comparisons are demonstrated, the defensible contribution of this article is conceptual and architectural. It provides a coherent account of cultural organisation within APS while recognising the independent achievements and continuing importance of established cultural scholarship.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

What constitutes cultural organisation, and how do socially distributed practices, relationships, meanings, expectations, and material arrangements support continuing forms of coordinated activity?

### Architectural Role

This article is a Core article within the Social Organisation topic.

It develops the APS interpretation of culture as socially distributed organised persistence, examining how cultural practices and relationships are constituted, maintained, modified, and sometimes lost.

Its principal responsibility is to clarify the organisation of cultural life, rather than to provide a comprehensive theory of cultural transmission or cultural evolution.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

The article builds upon the APS accounts of biological agency, organised persistence, communication, coordination, normativity, symbolic activity, Process, and Scale.

Established anthropological, philosophical, sociological, and cultural evolutionary approaches provide independent conceptual and explanatory resources, particularly concerning meaning, social practice, learning, cultural transmission, and historical continuity.

These relationships must not be interpreted as a necessary progression towards APS. Conceptual affinity, historical influence, and explanatory dependence are distinct relationships requiring separate justification.

These articles address complementary explanatory responsibilities. Cultural Organisation identifies the relationships constituting cultural arrangements without replacing the more specialised investigations of their transmission, institutional stabilisation, or technological transformation.

### Explanatory Boundaries

Cultural organisation does not necessarily imply biological autonomy, collective agency, beneficial adaptation, or increasing complexity.

Socially distributed cultural organisation must not be equated without qualification with the viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation of a living organism.

Cultural practices may persist, fragment, transform, or disappear, and their historical continuity does not itself establish their biological or social value.

The organisational interpretation does not replace the interpretive analysis of cultural meaning or the established investigation of cultural transmission and evolutionary dynamics.

Any claim of explanatory advance requires comparison with the strongest relevant alternative.

### Position Within APS

Within APS, Cultural Organisation provides the principal account of culture as a socially distributed form of organised persistence.

It connects communication, coordination, normativity, symbolic activity, collective memory, institutions, and technology without reducing culture to any one of these phenomena.

Its central architectural contribution is to distinguish the constitution and maintenance of cultural organisation from the processes through which cultural resources are inherited and transformed.

This distinction provides a possible basis for conceptual clarification, but it does not by itself demonstrate explanatory superiority over established approaches. Such a contribution must be established through explicit comparison with alternative explanations and the evidence relevant to particular cultural phenomena.
