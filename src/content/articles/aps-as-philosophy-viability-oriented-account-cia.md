## Biology and the Return of Philosophy

Modern biology achieved extraordinary explanatory success by rejecting the explicit teleology that had long dominated the understanding of life. Evolutionary theory, molecular biology, genetics, physiology, and systems biology transformed living systems into objects of rigorous scientific investigation without invoking external design, intrinsic purpose, or vital forces beyond physical reality.

Yet the success of modern biology also generated a persistent conceptual tension.

Biological explanation continued to rely upon concepts such as function, regulation, adaptation, organisation, signalling, information, cognition, and purpose even while lacking a fully coherent account of how such concepts fit within a rigorously naturalistic understanding of life. Organisms regulate themselves, maintain organisation, adapt to changing conditions, distinguish functional from dysfunctional states, respond selectively to environmental differences, and exhibit forms of coordinated activity that appear intrinsically directed toward continued existence. The language of biology therefore continued to carry normative and purposive implications even as its explanatory foundations remained largely mechanistic.

As biology became increasingly successful experimentally, its conceptual foundations simultaneously became more fragmented. Mechanistic explanation, information-theoretic language, computational models, systems approaches, evolutionary theory, autonomy theory, enactivism, and biosemiotics each captured important dimensions of living systems while often resting upon incompatible explanatory assumptions. Biology increasingly possessed powerful explanatory tools without a unified account of what made biological explanation distinctively biological in the first place.

This situation marked not the failure of biology, but the re-emergence of a philosophical problem internal to biology itself.

The question was no longer whether living systems obey physical law. Few serious biological theories deny this. The deeper question became how physical processes become organised into systems capable of sustaining themselves, regulating activity relative to viability, generating intrinsic normativity, and exhibiting meaningful forms of organisation across time.

In this sense, philosophy returns not as an external critique of biology, but as the clarification of biology’s own explanatory conditions.

It is within this increasingly fragmented explanatory landscape that APS emerges.

The framework does not attempt to replace empirical biology with speculative metaphysics, nor does it reject mechanistic explanation where mechanistic explanation is appropriate. Instead, APS asks a prior and more fundamental question: what kind of organisation makes biological explanation possible at all?

This shift is crucial. APS does not merely introduce additional concepts into biology. It reconstructs the explanatory relations between concepts already central to biological practice. Function, normativity, purpose, semiosis, cognition, adaptation, and evolution are no longer treated as partially disconnected explanatory domains requiring separate philosophical treatment. They become integrated consequences of viability-oriented organisation itself.

In this respect, APS belongs within a long historical effort to understand the distinctive character of living systems while avoiding both reductive mechanism and mysterious vitalism. Like earlier traditions in theoretical biology and philosophy of biology, it recognises that living systems cannot be adequately understood as passive aggregates of components alone. APS differs, however, in seeking a more unified organisational grammar capable of integrating persistence, agency, normativity, semiosis, cognition, and evolutionary transformation within a single explanatory architecture.

The return of philosophy therefore reflects a maturation of biology rather than a retreat from science.

As biological explanation increasingly confronts questions concerning organisation, meaning, normativity, agency, and cognition, philosophical clarification becomes unavoidable. APS represents one attempt to provide such clarification by grounding biological intelligibility in viability-oriented organised persistence itself.

## 2. From Biological Framework to Ontological Proposal

APS is initially presented as a framework for understanding living systems. It emphasises that organisms are not static entities but ongoing processes, that their persistence depends upon continuous activity, and that this activity is organised across interacting spatial and temporal scales. These claims resonate strongly with developments in systems biology, developmental biology, ecology, and theoretical biology, where organisation and interaction increasingly replace static structural description as the primary focus of explanation.

However, once these claims are integrated within a single explanatory architecture, they begin to function as more than descriptive generalisations. They specify the organisational conditions under which something can count as living in the first place.

To say that life is processual is to reject a substance-based ontology of biological entities. To say that it is viability-oriented is to introduce intrinsic normativity into biological organisation. To say that it is constraint-closed is to claim that living systems continuously regenerate the conditions of their own persistence. And to say that living organisation is scale-integrated is to reject the idea that biological explanation can be confined to any single privileged level or explanatory domain.

At this point APS no longer functions merely as a descriptive or integrative framework. It begins to articulate the organisational conditions under which biological explanation itself becomes possible.

The transition is subtle but decisive. What begins as a way of organising biological knowledge gradually becomes a specification of what biological reality must be like in order for that knowledge to cohere.

APS therefore occupies an intermediate ontological position. It is neither a purely speculative metaphysics nor a simple empirical generalisation. Rather, it functions as a biologically grounded account of reality whose commitments emerge from the explanatory structure of biological practice itself.

In this sense, APS does not merely redescribe biological findings. It attempts to articulate the organisational form of reality presupposed by those findings.

Taken together, these commitments amount to more than a conceptual vocabulary for biology. They constitute an account of living systems as viability-oriented, constraint-closed, scale-integrated processes whose continued existence depends upon the ongoing regeneration and modulation of organised persistence.

## 3. Explanatory Grammar and the Conditions of Biological Understanding

A defining feature of APS is its emphasis on explanatory grammar. Rather than beginning with a catalogue of entities, APS begins with the organisational conditions under which biological explanation becomes possible. This represents a significant shift in explanatory orientation. Instead of asking what exists independently of biological understanding, APS asks what must be true of living organisation for biological systems to become intelligible as living systems in the first place.

The focus therefore shifts from the inventory of the world to the organisational conditions that make that inventory biologically meaningful.

This does not deny the reality of biological entities. Cells, organisms, tissues, ecosystems, and evolutionary lineages remain indispensable objects of scientific inquiry. What changes is how they are approached. They are no longer treated as fundamentally independent explanatory units whose properties are analysed in isolation, but as organised systems whose intelligibility depends upon reciprocal relations not reducible to their components alone.

Biological explanation therefore ceases to be merely a process of decomposition. It also becomes a process of situating components within the organised systems that give them biological significance.

This reflects a broader distinction between analysing systems into parts and understanding how those parts become integrated within viability-oriented organisation.

[[box:analysis-vs-synthesis-two-directions-of-explanation]]

In this respect, APS enters into dialogue with a long philosophical tradition concerned with the conditions of intelligibility. In one sense it echoes the Kantian question concerning what must be true for understanding to become possible at all. Yet APS relocates these conditions from cognition to biological organisation itself. The explanatory constraints are not imposed by the structure of the mind, but emerge from the organisational character of living systems. What is at issue is therefore not the possibility of experience in general, but the organisational conditions under which biological explanation becomes coherent.

This shift has important consequences.

Biological explanation is no longer understood as the neutral description of independently structured objects. It becomes an organised explanatory activity constrained by the character of living systems themselves. To explain a biological phenomenon is not merely to identify efficient causes, but to situate that phenomenon within the organised relations that make persistence, variation, regulation, adaptation, and transformation intelligible.

Explanation therefore becomes inseparable from organisation.

Within APS, agency, process, and scale are not independent ontological categories but analytically distinguishable and ontologically co-constitutive dimensions of organised persistence.

Biological agency refers to the viability-oriented modulation of activity.

Process refers to the temporal continuity through which organised persistence is enacted.

Scale refers to the distributed spatial and temporal organisation of that activity across interacting domains.

These dimensions do not divide reality into separate explanatory regions. They articulate different but inseparable aspects of the same organised phenomenon: the continuous maintenance and reorganisation of viability-oriented persistence.

However, the explanatory grammar of APS extends beyond agency, process, and scale alone. As the framework has developed, it has increasingly come to organise biological explanation through a coordinated set of mutually constraining concepts.

Viability specifies the conditions under which organised persistence can succeed or fail.

Persistence describes the ongoing regeneration of organised activity across time.

Constraint closure explains how living systems continuously sustain the conditions of their own existence.

Coupling and scale-coupling explain how organisation is distributed and integrated across interacting spatial and temporal domains.

Agency describes the active modulation of this organisation relative to viability.

Semiosis and evaluation explain how differences become biologically meaningful through their role in viability-oriented regulation.

Resolution clarifies the explanatory granularity through which organisation is analysed or described.

Together these concepts increasingly function not as isolated theoretical terms but as an organisational grammar for biology itself.

[[box:the-organisational-grammar-of-aps]]

This organisational grammar also constrains what counts as an adequate biological explanation.

Any satisfactory explanation must be capable of articulating:
- how activity is regulated relative to viability,
- how organisation is sustained across time,
- how persistence is distributed across scale,
- and how meaningful differences modulate biological activity.

Explanations omitting one or more of these dimensions may remain locally useful while nevertheless failing to capture the organisation of living systems as such.

In this sense, explanatory grammar is not merely descriptive. It is methodologically normative. It specifies the organisational conditions under which biological explanation becomes coherent.

This perspective aligns APS with contemporary philosophy of science approaches that treat explanation not simply as representation, but as an organised practice through which phenomena become intelligible. APS extends this insight further by grounding explanatory constraints directly in the structure of living organisation itself. Explanation is not reduced to language, convention, or modelling strategy alone. It is constrained by the organisational reality of living systems.

APS therefore increasingly functions as a grammar of biological intelligibility.

It does not replace empirical investigation, nor impose an external philosophical structure upon biology. Rather, it makes explicit the organisational constraints already implicit within biological explanation and, in doing so, provides a more coherent account of what life must be like in order to become scientifically intelligible as life.

## 4. Constraint Closure and the Structure of Organised Persistence

Constraint closure occupies a foundational position within APS because it explains how living systems continuously sustain the conditions of their own persistence.

Living systems do not merely operate within constraints. They regenerate, maintain, and reorganise the very constraints that enable their activity. Metabolic processes sustain membranes; membranes regulate metabolic exchanges; regulatory networks coordinate the conditions under which metabolism remains viable. Persistence therefore depends not upon static stability, but upon reciprocal networks of continuously renewed organisational relations.

Constraint closure thus marks a decisive shift in how biological organisation is understood.

In many non-living systems, constraints shape behaviour without being maintained by the systems they constrain. A whirlpool, flame, or convection current may exhibit transient stability, but the conditions enabling that stability remain externally supplied. If those external conditions disappear, the organisation collapses without compensatory reorganisation.

Living systems differ fundamentally in this respect.

They actively participate in maintaining the conditions of their own continued existence. They regulate exchanges with their environment, repair disruptions, compensate for perturbation, reorganise activity under changing conditions, and sustain viability through ongoing modification of organisational relations. Persistence is therefore not the endurance of a structure, but the continuous regeneration of the conditions under which organised persistence remains possible.

Constraint closure explains how such organised persistence can occur without external design or imposed instruction.

However, APS also extends contemporary autonomy theory by arguing that closure alone is insufficient to explain biological organisation fully.

A system may exhibit forms of organisational closure while lacking the capacity to actively regulate and reorganise itself relative to changing conditions. Biological systems do not merely preserve organisational continuity. They actively modulate, reinforce, relax, redirect, and reorganise constraints relative to viability.

This introduces biological agency.

Agency therefore emerges not as an optional property added onto otherwise mechanistic organisation, but as an intrinsic feature of viability-oriented persistence itself. Living systems are not merely self-maintaining; they are actively self-modulating systems organised in relation to conditions that support or undermine their continued existence.

Constraint closure must therefore be understood within the broader organisational grammar of APS.

Closure explains how systems sustain the conditions enabling organised activity.

Viability specifies the conditions under which such persistence can succeed or fail.

Agency explains how systems actively regulate and reorganise activity relative to those conditions.

Scale-coupling explains how this organisation is distributed across interacting spatial and temporal domains.

Normativity arises because persistence depends upon maintaining viability under changing conditions.

These concepts do not function independently. Together they describe the organisational structure through which living systems continuously sustain themselves as living systems.

[[box:the-organisational-grammar-of-aps]]

The explanatory implications of this shift are substantial.

Mechanistic accounts remain indispensable in biology. They explain how organised processes operate through interacting structures, pathways, and causal relations. APS does not reject mechanistic explanation. Rather, it situates mechanism within a broader account of organised persistence.

A mechanism may be fully specified in terms of its components and interactions while still presupposing organisational conditions not explained by the mechanism alone. Mechanisms must themselves be maintained, repaired, integrated, coordinated, and reorganised across time. Constraint closure explains how these enabling conditions remain continuously regenerated within living systems.

In this sense, constraint closure functions as the organisational counterpart to explanatory grammar.

Explanatory grammar specifies the conceptual conditions under which biological systems become intelligible as living systems.

Constraint closure specifies the organisational conditions under which such systems can persist materially through time.

The two therefore converge upon the same fundamental insight: biological intelligibility depends upon organised persistence.

A system unable to regenerate the constraints enabling its own activity cannot coherently be described as a biological individual within APS.

This organisational perspective also transforms the understanding of biological individuality.

A biological individual is not fundamentally defined by material composition, spatial boundary, or component identity. Molecules are continuously replaced, processes fluctuate, and environmental conditions vary. What persists is the organised continuity of reciprocal processes sustaining viability across time.

Identity therefore becomes organised renewal rather than material sameness.

A concrete illustration can be found in bacterial metabolic self-maintenance. Enzymatic networks generate metabolites that stabilise the enzymatic structures responsible for their production. Membrane transport systems maintain ionic gradients enabling the metabolic reactions that sustain membrane integrity. Regulatory processes coordinate exchanges with the environment while compensating for perturbation and fluctuation.

These processes are not independent components assembled into a system from outside. They form a reciprocally organised network in which each process contributes to maintaining conditions required by the others.

What matters biologically is therefore not the presence of particular material components alone, but the viability-oriented organisation linking them.

Constraint closure also grounds biological normativity.

Because living systems must continuously regenerate the conditions enabling persistence, some processes support viability while others undermine it. Functional organisation therefore cannot be understood as value-neutral contribution alone. Functions become intelligible through their role in maintaining viable organisation.

Adaptation likewise becomes the reorganisation of constraint-closed activity under changing conditions such that viability remains sustainable across time.

Semiosis and evaluation emerge from the same organisational structure. Differences become biologically meaningful insofar as they modulate activity relative to viability. Constraint closure therefore contributes not only to the explanation of persistence, but also to the naturalisation of function, normativity, meaning, and biological agency.

Constraint closure thus transforms the structure of biological explanation itself.

The focus shifts:
- from isolated mechanisms to integrated organisation,
- from static structures to ongoing processes,
- from externally imposed order to internally sustained persistence,
- and from passive stability to active viability-oriented organisation.

Living systems become intelligible not as collections of parts, but as dynamically organised, scale-integrated processes continuously regenerating and modulating the conditions of their own existence.

## 5. Conceptual Closure and the Problem of Circularity

One of the defining strengths of APS is the degree of coherence linking its central concepts. Viability, persistence, agency, normativity, function, semiosis, adaptation, and evolution are not introduced as isolated explanatory terms. Each acquires meaning through its relation to the others within a coordinated account of living organisation.

This interdependence gives the framework much of its explanatory power. Yet it also raises an important philosophical question.

Does APS genuinely explain these concepts, or does it merely stabilise them within a self-reinforcing conceptual network?

At issue is not simply whether circularity exists, but what kind of circularity is involved.

A distinction must therefore be made between vicious and virtuous circularity.

Vicious circularity occurs when a concept is defined in terms of itself in a way that produces no explanatory advance. Explanation simply moves in a closed loop without introducing additional constraints or understanding. The phenomenon remains as obscure as before.

Virtuous circularity, by contrast, emerges when interdependence reflects the genuine organisation of the phenomena being described. In such cases, concepts cannot be understood independently because the systems themselves are organisationally interdependent. Circularity then becomes not a logical defect but an indication that explanation must capture reciprocal relations rather than linear causal chains alone.

APS increasingly operates in this second sense.

Its conceptual organisation mirrors the organisational structure of living systems themselves.

Living systems sustain themselves through networks of reciprocally dependent processes. Constraint closure exists because processes continuously regenerate the constraints enabling those same processes. Organised persistence therefore depends upon reciprocal organisational relations rather than unidirectional causal sequences.

APS exhibits a comparable form of conceptual organisation.

Agency is intelligible only in relation to viability-oriented regulation.

Viability is intelligible only in relation to organised persistence.

Persistence depends upon processual continuity.

Process becomes biologically meaningful through its contribution to organised persistence.

Normativity emerges because viability can succeed or fail.

Function becomes intelligible through normatively structured contribution to persistence.

Semiosis emerges through the organisation of meaningful differences relative to viability.

Cognition emerges through the temporally extended integration of evaluative semiosis.

These concepts do not form a simple definitional loop. They constitute a network of mutually constraining explanatory relations articulating different dimensions of the same organised phenomenon: viability-oriented persistence.

The critical question is therefore whether this interdependence is genuinely explanatory or merely terminological.

If APS concepts simply redescribe one another without introducing additional organisational constraints, then the framework risks collapsing into a purely self-referential vocabulary. In that case its coherence would be purchased at the expense of explanatory depth.

However, APS increasingly avoids this problem because its concepts are not interchangeable.

Each introduces a distinct explanatory dimension.

Viability introduces normative conditions governing persistence.

Persistence introduces temporal continuity.

Constraint closure introduces reciprocal organisational dependence.

Agency introduces active modulation relative to viability.

Scale-coupling introduces distributed organisation across interacting domains.

Evaluation and semiosis introduce the organisation of meaningful difference.

Evolution introduces the historical transformation of organised persistence across generations.

Their interdependence therefore reflects the multidimensional structure of living systems themselves rather than arbitrary definitional overlap.

In this respect, the analogy between conceptual closure and biological constraint closure becomes more than illustrative.

Living systems are not organised through isolated mechanisms operating independently of one another. Their persistence depends upon reciprocally sustaining organisational relations distributed across continuously interacting processes.

Similarly, APS does not function through isolated explanatory principles assembled externally into a framework. Its central concepts constrain, stabilise, and specify one another within an integrated explanatory architecture.

Conceptual closure therefore does not isolate APS from criticism.

Rather, it organises the conditions under which criticism becomes meaningful.

Each concept limits the explanatory scope of the others. Viability constrains agency. Agency constrains adaptation. Constraint closure constrains individuality. Semiosis constrains cognition. Resolution constrains explanatory claims concerning scale. The framework remains internally structured precisely because its concepts are mutually constraining rather than freely expandable.

This distinction is crucial.

APS is closed organisationally, not dogmatically.

Its coherence does not depend upon excluding alternatives or insulating itself from revision. On the contrary, the framework must remain open to empirical and conceptual challenge if its explanatory claims are to remain legitimate.

APS therefore requires conditions under which its commitments could, in principle, be revised or rejected.

If systems exhibited long-term organised persistence without regenerating the constraints enabling that persistence, the explanatory centrality of constraint closure would weaken.

If viability-oriented regulation proved fully explicable without agency-like organisation, the explanatory necessity of biological agency would require reconsideration.

If cognition could be fully explained independently of evaluation, semiosis, and viability-oriented organisation, the APS account of cognition would need substantial revision.

Such possibilities are not weaknesses within the framework. They are conditions of its explanatory seriousness.

A framework incapable of specifying what could count against it risks degenerating into protective circularity. APS avoids this only insofar as its conceptual closure remains empirically and philosophically constrained.

Conceptual closure therefore becomes diagnostically important.

It indicates that APS is attempting to describe a domain in which explanation cannot proceed through simple linear decomposition alone. Living systems are organisationally interdependent systems whose persistence depends upon reciprocal relations continuously regenerated across time and scale.

The challenge is therefore not to eliminate circularity altogether, but to demonstrate that the circularity present within the framework corresponds to the organisational structure of the phenomena themselves.

Whether APS ultimately succeeds in this task remains an open question.

Yet this openness is itself philosophically significant. APS does not claim immunity from revision. Rather, it attempts to make explicit the organisational commitments already implicit within biological explanation and to determine whether those commitments can sustain a coherent account of life as viability-oriented organised persistence.

## 6. APS and the Refinement of Process Philosophy

APS aligns strongly with the tradition of process philosophy in treating reality, particularly biological reality, as fundamentally dynamic and relational rather than static and substance-based. What persists through time is not a fixed entity remaining unchanged beneath superficial variation, but an organised continuity continuously maintained through ongoing transformation. Stability therefore becomes a structured form of change, and identity becomes a pattern of organised renewal rather than the preservation of immutable substance.

In this respect, APS belongs within a long philosophical tradition extending from Heraclitus to contemporary process philosophy in which becoming is treated as more fundamental than static being.

Yet APS does not merely adopt this orientation. It also refines and constrains it.

One of the enduring difficulties within process philosophy is that process, taken in sufficiently general terms, applies to almost everything. Flames, whirlpools, storms, ecosystems, economies, galaxies, and living organisms can all be described as dynamic systems exhibiting ongoing change and relational organisation. If process alone becomes the defining criterion, the concept risks becoming too permissive to provide explanatory discrimination.

APS addresses this problem directly.

The framework treats process not as a sufficient condition for life, but as a necessary yet incomplete one. Living systems are not distinguished simply because they change, flow, or persist dynamically through time. They are distinguished because their processes are organised in highly specific ways.

Constraint closure specifies systems capable of continuously regenerating the conditions enabling their own persistence.

Viability-orientation introduces intrinsic normativity by organising activity relative to conditions under which persistence can succeed or fail.

Agency introduces the active modulation and reorganisation of activity relative to those viability conditions.

Scale-coupling situates this organisation across interacting spatial and temporal domains, ensuring that persistence is distributed, coordinated, and extended rather than merely momentary.

Semiosis and evaluation explain how differences become biologically meaningful through their role in viability-oriented regulation.

Together these conditions transform process ontology into a biologically grounded account of living organisation.

APS therefore retains the central insight of process philosophy—that life is fundamentally processual—while introducing stronger organisational constraints capable of differentiating biological systems from generic dynamical phenomena.

This shift is philosophically important.

In many forms of process ontology, process remains a highly abstract metaphysical category. APS instead attempts to specify the particular organisational form process must take in order to constitute living systems. Process in APS is not mere change, flow, or dynamical continuity. It is viability-oriented organised activity continuously regenerating and modulating the conditions of its own persistence.

This refinement has several important consequences.

First, it stabilises process philosophy for biological use.

Without stronger organisational specification, process ontology risks collapsing into a descriptive generality equally applicable to living and non-living systems. APS restricts this explanatory scope by introducing organisational criteria capable, at least in principle, of empirical investigation. In doing so, it converts a broad metaphysical orientation into a framework more directly connected to biological explanation itself.

Second, APS transforms process philosophy from a primarily ontological claim into an explanatory architecture.

The framework does not merely assert that organisms are processes. It asks what kind of process living systems must be in order to exhibit:
- organised persistence,
- normativity,
- agency,
- adaptation,
- semiosis,
- cognition,
- and evolutionary continuity.

This shift from general process to organised persistence marks one of the central philosophical developments of APS.

Third, APS introduces a productive tension concerning the scope of biological concepts themselves.

If viability-orientation, constraint closure, agency, scale-coupling, and evaluative semiosis are required for biological organisation, then not all dynamic systems qualify equally as living systems. APS therefore resists the tendency within some process traditions to dissolve distinctions between biological and non-biological organisation entirely.

The framework does not deny that non-living systems may exhibit complex dynamics, self-organisation, or emergent behaviour. Rather, it argues that biological systems exhibit a more specific form of organisation grounded in viability-oriented persistence.

In this sense APS is simultaneously continuous with and divergent from process philosophy.

It remains continuous insofar as it rejects substance-based ontology and affirms the fundamentally dynamic and relational character of living systems.

It diverges insofar as it insists that process alone is explanatorily insufficient unless specified organisationally.

This organisational specification is what allows APS to naturalise many concepts historically treated as philosophically problematic within biology.

Normativity becomes intelligible because viability can succeed or fail.

Function becomes intelligible because processes contribute differentially to organised persistence.

Purpose becomes intelligible because activity is organised relative to viability conditions.

Semiosis becomes intelligible because differences matter for persistence.

Cognition becomes intelligible because evaluative semiosis becomes sufficiently integrated and temporally extended.

Evolution becomes intelligible because organised persistence becomes historically transformable across generations.

APS therefore transforms process philosophy from a broad metaphysical orientation into a disciplined account of biological intelligibility.

This transformation is not merely philosophical. It has direct implications for biological inquiry itself.

To identify a system as living is no longer merely to observe that it changes over time or exhibits complex dynamics. It is to determine whether its activity is organised in ways that continuously regenerate and modulate the conditions of its own persistence.

The decisive question therefore becomes not whether something is a process, but what kind of process it is — and whether its organisation satisfies the conditions required for viability-oriented organised persistence.

## 7. Normativity and the Naturalisation of Value

One of the most philosophically significant claims within APS is that living systems embody intrinsic normativity.

Living systems do not merely persist mechanically through time. Their organisation establishes an asymmetry between conditions that support continued persistence and conditions that undermine it. Some states stabilise organised activity, while others contribute to breakdown, degradation, or collapse. This asymmetry is not imposed externally through human interpretation, conscious judgement, or social convention. It emerges from the organisation of living systems themselves.

A living system therefore does not simply undergo change. It exists under conditions in which what happens to it can matter for whether it continues to exist as the kind of organised system it is.

At first glance, this claim may appear relatively uncontroversial. Biology routinely describes organisms in terms of regulation, repair, adaptation, homeostasis, stress response, dysfunction, and survival. Such concepts already presuppose distinctions between conditions favourable to persistence and conditions that threaten it.

Yet the philosophical significance of this distinction is far from straightforward.

It is one thing to observe that some biological states causally contribute to persistence while others do not. It is another to claim that this asymmetry grounds genuine normativity.

APS addresses this issue by distinguishing descriptive asymmetry from evaluative organisation.

A purely descriptive asymmetry states only that different conditions produce different consequences. Rocks erode differently under varying environmental pressures; stars stabilise or collapse depending upon thermodynamic conditions; rivers alter their flow patterns under changing terrain. Such asymmetries alone do not necessarily generate normativity.

Normativity emerges only when a system’s continued existence depends upon the active regulation and modulation of its own organisation relative to those conditions.

This distinction is crucial.

APS does not claim that every causal contribution automatically carries normative significance. Rather, normativity arises specifically in systems organised such that persistence depends upon ongoing viability-oriented regulation.

Where a system must continuously sustain and reorganise the conditions of its own persistence, differences cease to remain neutral. Some differences support continued viability; others undermine it. Conditions therefore begin to matter organisationally for the system itself.

This is the basis of biological normativity.

Normativity in APS is therefore neither externally imposed nor psychologically projected. It is an organisational consequence of viability-oriented persistence.

This organisational asymmetry becomes operationally expressed through evaluation.

Evaluation refers to the differential modulation of activity relative to conditions affecting viability. Living systems continuously stabilise, amplify, suppress, redirect, or reorganise activity according to whether conditions support or threaten organised persistence.

Evaluation is therefore not a detached cognitive judgement added onto biological systems from above. It is the operational enactment of normativity within living activity itself.

This marks a major shift in how biological value is understood.

Classical teleological accounts grounded purposiveness in external design, intrinsic ends, or final causes.

Selected-effects theories grounded biological function in evolutionary history, defining traits in terms of the effects for which they were historically selected.

Causal-role theories grounded function in the contribution of components within systems while often leaving the normative status of those contributions unresolved.

APS departs from each of these approaches in different ways.

Function becomes intelligible through present-time contribution to organised persistence.

Purpose becomes intelligible through the organisation of activity relative to viability conditions.

Normativity becomes intelligible because viability can succeed or fail.

Evaluation becomes intelligible because living systems actively modulate activity relative to those conditions.

In this way APS attempts to naturalise value without reducing it either to external teleology or to purely historical selection.

The implications of this shift are substantial.

Normativity becomes intrinsic to biological organisation rather than added at higher levels of complexity. A system does not need conscious awareness, symbolic representation, explicit goals, or reflective deliberation in order for its activity to exhibit normative organisation. It need only be organised such that its continued persistence depends upon the regulation of its own activity relative to viability.

This introduces an important continuity across biological organisation.

Cellular regulation, metabolic adjustment, immune response, physiological repair, behavioural flexibility, ecological adaptation, and cognition all become increasingly elaborated forms of viability-oriented evaluation distributed across scale and time.

Normativity is therefore not added suddenly at the appearance of mind. Rather, cognition emerges from increasingly integrated and temporally extended forms of evaluative organisation already present within life itself.

This continuity also explains the increasingly close integration within APS between normativity, evaluation, semiosis, and cognition.

Normativity establishes the asymmetry between conditions supporting or undermining persistence.

Evaluation enacts this asymmetry operationally through differential modulation of activity.

Semiosis structures these differences as biologically meaningful.

Cognition emerges when evaluative semiosis becomes sufficiently integrated and temporally extended that present activity becomes regulated relative to non-immediate conditions.

These concepts therefore form interconnected dimensions of the same organisational architecture rather than isolated theoretical additions.

[[box:the-organisational-grammar-of-aps]]

APS nevertheless avoids claiming that the problem of normativity has been completely resolved.

The existence of viability-oriented asymmetry does not automatically settle every philosophical question concerning value. Some philosophers may argue that genuine normativity requires representation, justification, rational endorsement, or reflective standpoint in ways not captured by biological organisation alone.

APS does not attempt to eliminate such debates in advance.

Instead, it reframes them.

Rather than asking how normativity can somehow be inserted into an otherwise entirely neutral world, APS asks how organised, self-maintaining systems give rise to distinctions that matter for their continued persistence. The explanatory focus therefore shifts from abstract theories of value toward the organisational dynamics of life itself.

This reframing also transforms the relation between normativity and biological explanation more generally.

Normativity is no longer treated as an optional interpretive overlay added onto mechanistic biology. It becomes part of the organisational structure that biological explanation must account for if living systems are to become intelligible as living systems at all.

In this respect, normativity is inseparable from the broader explanatory grammar of APS.

Agency expresses the viability-oriented modulation of activity.

Process provides the temporal continuity through which such modulation persists.

Scale and scale-coupling distribute these dynamics across interacting organisational domains.

Constraint closure sustains the conditions enabling viable persistence.

Semiosis and evaluation organise meaningful difference relative to viability.

Normativity therefore does not appear as a separate layer added onto biological organisation. It is intrinsic to the organisation of living activity itself.

Whether APS ultimately succeeds in fully grounding normativity remains an open philosophical question.

Yet this openness is itself important.

APS does not present normativity as a mysterious exception to nature, nor as a purely subjective projection imposed upon an otherwise indifferent world. Instead, it attempts to show how value-like organisation may emerge directly from the conditions under which living systems continuously sustain themselves as living systems.

In doing so, APS offers not merely a theory of normativity, but a broader reconfiguration of the relation between life, value, and biological intelligibility itself.

## 8. Empirical Tractability and the Scientific Status of APS

APS is unusual among philosophical frameworks in its explicit commitment to empirical tractability. It does not present itself merely as an interpretive or speculative account of life, but as a framework intended to guide, organise, and remain constrained by scientific investigation itself.

This commitment is reflected in the development of diagnostic approaches, biosignature criteria, and organisational analyses designed to operationalise concepts such as viability, persistence, constraint closure, agency, and scale-coupling within real biological systems.

[[box:aps-box-empirical-tractability]]

In this respect APS occupies a distinctive position within contemporary philosophy of biology.

Many philosophical accounts of life primarily interpret existing scientific practices retrospectively. APS instead attempts to participate directly in the organisation of biological inquiry itself. It seeks not only to clarify biological explanation conceptually, but also to shape how living systems are identified, analysed, compared, and investigated.

The framework therefore functions simultaneously:
- as an explanatory grammar,
- as an organisational ontology,
- and as a methodological orientation.

This orientation is especially visible in the diagnostic dimension of APS.

Traditional definitions of life often rely upon fixed lists of properties: metabolism, reproduction, growth, homeostasis, information storage, or adaptation. APS instead approaches biological organisation dynamically.

A system is analysed not according to whether it possesses isolated traits, but according to how its processes are organised relative to the ongoing maintenance of viability-oriented persistence.

From this perspective, biosignatures are not merely static markers. They become indicators of underlying organisational structure.

Constraint closure indicates reciprocal organisational dependence.

Agency indicates active modulation relative to viability.

Scale-coupling indicates integration across distributed spatial and temporal domains.

Evaluation and semiosis indicate the organisation of meaningful difference relative to persistence.

Persistence itself becomes observable through the capacity of systems to sustain, repair, reorganise, and regenerate the conditions of their own continued existence.

The emphasis therefore shifts from static properties toward organised activity.

This shift has significant implications for how biological systems are investigated.

Borderline cases — such as viruses, protocells, artificial systems, synthetic organisms, ecological collectives, or non-standard life-like processes — can no longer be approached solely through binary classification. Instead, they become diagnostically informative cases revealing degrees, forms, and limitations of organised persistence.

APS therefore potentially transforms the question:
> “Is this alive?”

into the more organisationally precise question:
> “To what extent does this system exhibit viability-oriented organised persistence?”

This diagnostic orientation also reinforces the importance of explanatory integration within APS itself.

Because the framework treats biological organisation as inherently relational, no single criterion becomes independently decisive. Constraint closure without viability-orientation may be insufficient. Metabolic activity without organisational persistence may be insufficient. Complex dynamics without evaluative regulation may be insufficient.

Biological organisation therefore becomes a multidimensional organisational achievement rather than a checklist of isolated traits.

At the same time, this close integration between conceptual architecture and empirical application introduces an important philosophical tension.

The diagnostic criteria developed within APS are themselves derived from the theoretical commitments of the framework. This creates the risk that APS could become partially self-confirming.

If the framework defines biological organisation according to criteria generated internally by its own conceptual structure, then empirical investigation may merely reproduce the assumptions already built into the framework.

This is a genuine challenge and one APS must take seriously.

The issue is not whether theory shapes observation. All scientific frameworks organise observation to some degree. The deeper issue is whether empirical systems can meaningfully constrain the framework rather than simply being interpreted through it.

APS therefore requires conditions under which its central commitments could, in principle, be weakened, revised, or rejected.

If systems exhibited long-term organised persistence without regenerating enabling constraints, the explanatory necessity of constraint closure would be challenged.

If viability-oriented regulation proved fully explicable without agency-like organisation, the role of biological agency would require reconsideration.

If cognition could be explained independently of evaluation, semiosis, and viability-oriented persistence, the APS account of cognition would need revision.

If biological organisation proved reducible to isolated mechanistic description without reference to distributed organisational continuity, the explanatory role of scale-coupling would weaken substantially.

Such possibilities are not threats to APS. They are conditions of its scientific legitimacy.

A framework incapable of identifying what could count against it risks becoming interpretively flexible while remaining empirically unconstrained.

APS therefore depends not only upon internal coherence, but upon sustained interaction with empirical systems capable of resisting, refining, or transforming its explanatory commitments.

This distinction may be clarified through the difference between application and constraint.

Any sufficiently flexible framework can be applied to biological phenomena retrospectively. The more important question is whether those phenomena can also constrain the framework itself.

APS remains scientifically meaningful only insofar as empirical systems retain the capacity to challenge its organisational assumptions.

At the same time, APS also recognises that empirical investigation in biology is never conceptually neutral.

Biological phenomena do not arrive pre-interpreted. Concepts such as organism, function, regulation, adaptation, cognition, and individuality already shape how systems are identified and studied. APS therefore does not attempt to eliminate the relation between conceptual structure and empirical inquiry. Instead, it attempts to make that relation more explicit, coherent, and biologically grounded.

This is one reason explanatory grammar becomes so important within the framework.

APS does not merely propose empirical criteria for life. It attempts to clarify the organisational assumptions underlying biological explanation itself.

Empirical tractability therefore becomes inseparable from explanatory intelligibility.

If successful, APS would represent a relatively unusual integration of philosophical reconstruction and scientific applicability.

Its significance would lie not only in conceptual coherence, but in its capacity to:
- generate new biological questions,
- refine diagnostic approaches,
- clarify explanatory relations,
- and identify organisational patterns previously obscured by more fragmented conceptual frameworks.

If unsuccessful, however, APS risks functioning primarily as an interpretive vocabulary organising observations without being genuinely constrained by them.

The distinction between these outcomes will not be determined by philosophical elegance alone. It will depend upon whether the framework can sustain productive interaction with empirical biology while remaining open to revision under conceptual and experimental pressure.

Empirical tractability is therefore not an optional supplement to APS.

It is a condition of the framework’s legitimacy as a biologically serious account of life.

Whether APS ultimately succeeds in meeting this condition remains an open question. Yet the attempt itself is philosophically important because it seeks to reconnect conceptual clarity with biological investigation rather than treating philosophy and empirical science as isolated domains.

APS therefore remains not a completed doctrine, but an ongoing organisational research programme situated between conceptual reconstruction and empirical inquiry.

## 9. Scope, Extension, and the Limits of Generalisation

APS is grounded in biology, yet the conceptual architecture of the framework naturally invites extension beyond the biological domain itself.

This possibility arises because APS is organised around concepts such as viability-oriented persistence, constraint closure, agency, evaluation, semiosis, and scale-coupled organisation. These features appear most clearly in living systems, but they also resonate with patterns observed in cognition, ecology, social organisation, artificial systems, and broader theories of complex organisation.

The resulting question is unavoidable:

To what extent can APS be extended beyond biology without losing the explanatory specificity that gives the framework its force?

This question is philosophically significant because it concerns the status of biological concepts themselves.

Are concepts such as agency, normativity, cognition, and semiosis uniquely biological?

Or do they identify more general organisational principles capable of appearing across multiple domains?

APS approaches this issue cautiously.

The framework neither assumes that biological organisation is completely isolated from wider organisational phenomena, nor collapses biological distinctions into a universalised process metaphysics in which all forms of organisation become equivalent.

Instead, APS treats extension as a problem requiring continuous organisational justification.

One of the clearest domains in which this issue emerges is cognition.

APS proposes continuity between life and mind by treating cognition not as a fundamentally separate mental substance, but as a developed form of viability-oriented organisation. Evaluation, semiosis, and adaptive regulation already appear in basal forms within biological organisation itself. Cognitive systems differ not because they escape biological organisation, but because evaluative semiosis becomes increasingly integrated, coordinated, and temporally extended.

In this sense, cognition emerges from organisational capacities already present within life rather than being externally added to it.

This continuity potentially allows APS to bridge biology and cognitive theory without reducing cognition either to disembodied computation or to purely mechanistic physiology.

[[box:what-aps-means-by-cognition]]

Similar possibilities arise at ecological and systems scales.

Ecosystems, symbiotic networks, developmental systems, and collective organisations exhibit forms of distributed coordination, reciprocal dependence, and scale-integrated persistence resembling some aspects of APS organisation. Processes extend across interacting domains rather than remaining confined within isolated entities. Stability often emerges through ongoing adjustment and reorganisation rather than static equilibrium.

Such parallels naturally invite the extension of APS concepts beyond the individual organism.

Could ecosystems exhibit forms of constraint closure?

Could distributed collectives exhibit forms of viability-oriented organisation?

Could artificial systems develop sufficiently integrated forms of evaluative regulation to warrant agential description?

APS does not exclude these possibilities in principle.

However, neither does it assume them automatically.

This caution is essential because the explanatory strength of APS depends precisely upon the specificity of its organisational criteria.

Constraint closure, viability-orientation, agency, evaluation, semiosis, and scale-coupling are not intended as loose metaphors for generic complexity. They identify highly specific forms of organised persistence grounded in the continuous regeneration and modulation of conditions enabling continued existence.

If these concepts are extended too freely, they risk losing their explanatory discrimination.

This creates what may be called the problem of conceptual dilution.

Many non-living systems exhibit:
- persistence,
- self-organisation,
- feedback,
- adaptation-like behaviour,
- distributed coordination,
- or dynamic stability.

Yet the presence of such features alone does not necessarily justify treating these systems as biologically organised in the APS sense.

If every sufficiently complex dynamical system is described as exhibiting agency, semiosis, or viability-oriented organisation, APS risks collapsing into a general systems vocabulary lacking clear criteria of application.

The framework would then lose the very distinctions it was developed to clarify.

APS therefore insists upon a more disciplined approach to extension.

The relevant question is not whether systems display superficial similarity to biological organisation, but whether they exhibit the specific organisational conditions required for viability-oriented persistence.

Do they continuously regenerate the constraints enabling their own activity?

Do they actively modulate organisation relative to conditions supporting or undermining persistence?

Do meaningful differences emerge through viability-oriented evaluation?

Is persistence distributed across scale-coupled organisational continuity?

Where such conditions are absent, APS concepts may still be used heuristically or metaphorically, but their application should not be treated as organisationally equivalent to biological systems themselves.

This distinction between structural continuity and superficial resemblance becomes critically important.

Some systems may genuinely share organisational principles with biological systems.

Others may merely display analogous patterns without exhibiting the deeper organisational relations grounding biological persistence.

APS therefore functions not only as a framework for extension, but also as a framework for limiting extension.

This is philosophically important because it prevents the framework from dissolving into an unrestricted process metaphysics in which all distinctions between living and non-living organisation disappear.

At the same time, APS does not deny that important continuities may exist across domains.

Indeed, one of the strengths of the framework may lie precisely in its ability to clarify where genuine continuities occur and where they break down.

The framework therefore remains open to carefully justified extension while resisting premature generalisation.

This issue also extends into broader philosophical domains such as ethics and epistemology.

If normativity emerges from viability-oriented organisation, then values may no longer appear entirely external to nature itself.

If cognition emerges from organised evaluative semiosis, then knowledge may become intelligible as an activity embedded within living organisation rather than detached representation alone.

APS does not fully resolve these philosophical questions, but it changes how they are approached.

The central issue becomes not how value, meaning, or cognition are added to an otherwise neutral world, but how they emerge from increasingly organised forms of persistence within living systems.

This reframing potentially establishes APS as more than a biological framework narrowly construed.

It becomes a broader organisational perspective capable of illuminating relations between life, mind, meaning, value, and explanation while remaining grounded in biological organisation itself.

Yet the framework’s legitimacy depends upon preserving the specificity of its organisational criteria.

Extension without constraint would weaken APS rather than strengthen it.

The challenge is therefore not simply whether APS can be generalised, but under what organisational conditions such generalisation remains meaningful.

This challenge mirrors the central concern of the framework itself.

Just as APS seeks to clarify what distinguishes living systems from non-living systems, it also seeks to clarify how far those distinctions can be extended without losing explanatory coherence.

Its primary domain remains biological organisation.

Its implications, however, may extend considerably further insofar as they illuminate the conditions under which organised systems persist, adapt, regulate themselves, and acquire significance within the ongoing dynamics of the natural world.

## 10. APS Among Contemporary Frameworks

APS does not emerge in isolation from contemporary theoretical biology or philosophy of biology. The framework develops within an intellectual landscape already shaped by systems theory, process philosophy, enactivism, autonomy theory, mechanistic biology, biosemiotics, ecological theory, and contemporary philosophy of science.

Its significance lies not in rejecting these traditions wholesale, but in reorganising many of their central insights within a more explicitly articulated account of viability-oriented organised persistence.

APS is therefore best understood not as a competitor seeking to eliminate existing frameworks, but as an attempt to clarify the organisational relations between them and the conditions under which each becomes explanatorily appropriate.

This integrative role is especially important because contemporary biology often operates through partially overlapping explanatory traditions that employ similar concepts while grounding them in very different assumptions.

Concepts such as:
- organisation,
- information,
- regulation,
- autonomy,
- cognition,
- adaptation,
- emergence,
- and complexity

frequently migrate across frameworks without clear specification of the organisational conditions under which they apply.

APS attempts to impose greater conceptual discipline upon this landscape by grounding explanation in viability-oriented organised persistence itself.

In this sense, the framework increasingly functions less as an isolated theory and more as an organisational grammar for biological explanation.

[[box:the-organisational-grammar-of-aps]]

### APS and Enactivism

APS shares important affinities with enactivism.

Both frameworks reject representationalist models of cognition and emphasise organism–environment coupling, embodied activity, and the active organisation of behaviour. Cognition is understood not as detached symbol manipulation occurring inside an isolated mind, but as an activity emerging through the dynamics of living systems interacting with their environments.

However, APS differs in explanatory orientation.

Enactivism primarily approaches biological organisation through the problem of cognition and experience. Organisms frequently appear as the enabling basis for cognition.

APS reverses this explanatory direction.

Rather than beginning from cognition, APS begins from organised persistence itself. Cognition becomes intelligible as a developed form of viability-oriented evaluative semiosis emerging within living organisation.

The explanatory priority therefore shifts:
- from mind to life,
- from cognition to organisation,
- and from representation to viability-oriented regulation.

This allows APS to integrate cognition more directly within a broader organisational account of biological reality.

### APS and Autonomy Theory

APS also shares substantial overlap with autonomy theory, particularly through the concept of constraint closure.

Autonomy theory has been especially important in demonstrating how living systems generate and maintain the conditions of their own persistence through networks of reciprocally dependent processes. APS builds directly upon this insight.

However, APS extends autonomy theory in several ways.

First, it integrates closure within a broader explanatory architecture centred on viability-oriented persistence rather than closure alone.

Second, APS introduces a stronger account of biological agency. Closure explains reciprocal self-maintenance, but APS argues that living systems also actively regulate, modulate, and reorganise constraints relative to changing viability conditions.

Third, APS integrates closure with:
- scale-coupling,
- normativity,
- evaluation,
- semiosis,
- cognition,
- and evolutionary transformation.

The framework therefore broadens autonomy theory from a primarily organisational account of self-maintenance into a more comprehensive explanatory grammar for biological organisation.

### APS and Systems Theory

APS shares with systems theory an emphasis on interaction, organisation, distributed dynamics, and relational structure.

Systems theory has been extraordinarily successful in analysing:
- networks,
- feedback relations,
- complex interactions,
- emergent dynamics,
- and distributed organisation across multiple domains.

Yet systems theory often operates at a level of generality that does not sharply distinguish biological organisation from non-biological complexity.

Economies, ecosystems, weather systems, machine networks, and organisms may all be analysed as systems exhibiting dynamic interaction and self-organisation.

APS attempts to introduce stronger organisational specification.

Constraint closure differentiates systems capable of regenerating enabling constraints.

Viability-orientation differentiates systems organised relative to conditions under which persistence can succeed or fail.

Agency differentiates systems capable of actively modulating organisation relative to those conditions.

Scale-coupling differentiates forms of persistence distributed across interacting organisational domains.

Semiosis differentiates systems in which differences become meaningful through viability-oriented regulation.

APS therefore does not reject systems theory. Rather, it attempts to specify what kind of system biological systems must be in order to count as living systems at all.

In this sense, APS may be understood as a biologically constrained refinement of systems thinking rather than a replacement for it.

### APS and Mechanistic Biology

APS also intersects importantly with mechanistic approaches, particularly the contemporary “new mechanist” tradition.

Mechanistic explanation has been highly successful in biology because it identifies the organised components and interactions responsible for producing biological phenomena. Mechanisms explain how processes occur through structured causal organisation.

APS fully recognises the indispensability of mechanistic explanation.

However, the framework argues that mechanisms alone do not fully explain biological organisation.

Mechanisms themselves must:
- persist,
- remain integrated,
- undergo repair,
- coordinate across scale,
- and continue operating under changing conditions.

Mechanistic analysis therefore presupposes forms of organised persistence not fully captured by decomposition into component interactions alone.

APS extends mechanistic explanation by situating mechanisms within viability-oriented organisational continuity.

The central question shifts from:
> “How does this mechanism produce a particular effect?”

to:
> “How are the mechanisms themselves maintained, integrated, regulated, and reorganised within living systems capable of persistence across time?”

This organisational extension is one of the major ways APS differs from strictly mechanistic accounts while still remaining compatible with mechanistic investigation itself.

### APS and Biosemiotics

APS also overlaps with biosemiotics through its treatment of meaning and semiosis.

Both frameworks reject the restriction of meaning exclusively to human symbolic cognition and recognise that living systems organise activity relative to biologically significant differences.

However, APS differs by grounding semiosis directly in viability-oriented evaluation rather than primarily in sign-theoretic structure.

Differences become meaningful because they modulate activity relative to persistence.

Semiosis therefore emerges from organisational normativity rather than from representation or symbolic interpretation alone.

This allows APS to naturalise meaning more directly within biological organisation itself.

### Integration Without Eclecticism

These comparisons reveal a consistent pattern.

Each framework captures important aspects of biological reality:
- enactivism emphasises embodied interaction,
- autonomy theory clarifies self-maintenance,
- systems theory analyses distributed organisation,
- mechanistic biology explains organised causal processes,
- process philosophy clarifies dynamical continuity,
- and biosemiotics investigates biological meaning.

APS does not discard these insights.

Instead, it attempts to reorganise them within a more unified explanatory structure grounded in viability-oriented organised persistence.

This is why APS is integrative without being merely eclectic.

The framework does not simply combine concepts from multiple traditions. It attempts to specify the organisational conditions under which those concepts become biologically meaningful.

Agency, process, scale, viability, constraint closure, semiosis, evaluation, cognition, adaptation, and evolution therefore function not as isolated theoretical additions, but as mutually constraining dimensions of a coordinated explanatory architecture.

This architecture also imposes limits.

APS resists the tendency to universalise concepts such as process, system, interaction, or organisation without organisational specification. The framework insists that explanatory concepts acquire force only when tied to the conditions under which living systems sustain and regenerate themselves as living systems.

In this respect APS functions simultaneously:
- as an integrative framework,
- as an explanatory grammar,
- and as a conceptual constraint on biological explanation itself.

Its contribution therefore lies not merely in providing another perspective on life, but in clarifying how multiple biological perspectives relate to one another, where their explanatory strengths lie, and where their limitations begin.

APS can therefore be understood not simply as one theoretical framework among others, but as an attempt to articulate the organisational architecture within which contemporary biological explanation increasingly operates.

Whether it ultimately succeeds in this role remains an open question.

Yet the attempt itself is significant because it seeks to render biological explanation more coherent, more integrated, and more responsive to the organisational realities of living systems themselves.

## 11. Conclusion

APS is best understood not merely as a conceptual framework for biology, but as a philosophical reconstruction of biological intelligibility itself.

At its core, the framework proposes that living systems are viability-oriented, constraint-closed, scale-integrated processes continuously regenerating and modulating the conditions of their own persistence. Life is therefore not adequately understood as the passive behaviour of matter arranged into increasingly complex structures, nor as the operation of isolated mechanisms considered independently of the organised systems within which they function. Living systems exist only insofar as they actively sustain themselves across time.

From this starting point APS reconstructs the explanatory architecture of biology itself.

Normativity becomes intelligible because viability can succeed or fail.

Function becomes intelligible because processes contribute differentially to organised persistence.

Purpose becomes intelligible because activity is organised relative to viability conditions.

Semiosis becomes intelligible because differences matter for persistence.

Cognition becomes intelligible because evaluative semiosis becomes sufficiently integrated and temporally extended.

Evolution becomes intelligible because organised persistence becomes transformable across generations.

In this way APS attempts to naturalise many concepts that biology has historically borrowed from teleological, psychological, or representational vocabularies while remaining grounded in the organisational dynamics of living systems themselves.

This reconstruction operates simultaneously at several interconnected levels.

As an explanatory grammar, APS specifies the organisational conditions under which biological phenomena become intelligible as biological phenomena.

As a theoretical framework, it integrates insights from systems theory, autonomy theory, process philosophy, enactivism, mechanistic biology, and biosemiotics within a more unified account of organised persistence.

As a methodological orientation, it attempts to connect conceptual analysis with empirical investigation through organisational diagnostics, biosignatures, and viability-oriented criteria of analysis.

In each of these roles APS seeks not to replace existing biological approaches, but to render their relations more coherent, more comparable, and more explicitly constrained by the organisational realities of living systems themselves.

[[box:the-organisational-grammar-of-aps]]

At the same time, APS remains an open project rather than a completed doctrine.

Its central claims concerning viability, constraint closure, agency, normativity, semiosis, cognition, and organised persistence remain subject to both empirical and philosophical scrutiny. The framework must demonstrate not only that it can organise existing biological knowledge coherently, but that it can also remain responsive to conceptual criticism, empirical resistance, and theoretical revision.

Its coherence is therefore not a final achievement but an ongoing condition of its legitimacy.

For this reason APS does not offer a final definition of life.

Instead, it reframes the question of life itself.

The problem shifts:
- from identifying fixed traits to understanding organised persistence,
- from cataloguing properties to analysing organisational relations,
- from static structures to ongoing processes,
- and from isolated mechanisms to viability-oriented organisation distributed across scale and time.

In doing so APS contributes not only to biology, but also to philosophy more broadly.

It offers a biologically grounded way of approaching longstanding questions concerning:
- organisation,
- causation,
- normativity,
- meaning,
- cognition,
- individuality,
- persistence,
- and the relation between life and mind.

Whether APS ultimately succeeds as a comprehensive account of biological reality remains an open question.

Yet the significance of the framework lies precisely in making its organisational commitments explicit and empirically contestable.

APS therefore represents not the closure of inquiry, but the opening of a more integrated form of inquiry in which biology and philosophy become mutually clarifying rather than methodologically isolated.

What the framework ultimately offers is not a final answer to the nature of life, but a disciplined and biologically grounded way of approaching the problem of how living systems become intelligible as living systems at all.
