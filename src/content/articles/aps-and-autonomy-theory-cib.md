APS and autonomy theory occupy closely related positions within contemporary organisational biology. Both reject the view that life can be adequately understood through the analysis of components in isolation and instead seek to explain living systems through the organisational relations that enable them to maintain themselves through time. Autonomy theory played a decisive role in establishing this organisational perspective, while APS builds upon these foundations by integrating agency, viability, multiscale organisation, historical continuity, and evolutionary transformation within a single explanatory framework.

Autonomy theory emerged from a broader organisational tradition that sought to understand life in terms of self-producing and self-maintaining systems rather than externally assembled mechanisms. By focusing attention on the organisational conditions that make biological persistence possible, autonomy theorists helped re-establish organisation as a central explanatory concept in theoretical biology. APS shares this commitment and should therefore be understood not as an alternative to autonomy theory but as a further development of many of its central insights.

The relationship between APS and autonomy theory is particularly important because both frameworks seek to explain how living systems maintain themselves despite continual material turnover, environmental uncertainty, and developmental and evolutionary change. Where autonomy theory identifies the organisational architecture that enables self-maintenance, APS extends this analysis by asking how organised systems regulate themselves, act upon their environments, preserve viability, and remain historically continuous across time. Understanding these continuities and differences helps clarify both the achievements of autonomy theory and the broader explanatory ambitions of APS.

## The Autonomy Insight

Autonomy theory marked a decisive shift in theoretical biology by grounding biological explanation in organisation rather than components. Earlier approaches often attempted to explain living systems primarily through their constituent parts or the mechanisms through which those parts interacted. Autonomy theorists argued that such explanations remained incomplete unless they also accounted for the organisational relations that make living systems possible in the first place.

The central insight was that living systems persist through networks of mutually dependent processes that collectively maintain the conditions required for their own continuation. Biological organisation is therefore not imposed from outside but continuously generated and sustained through the activity of the system itself. A living organism remains alive not because particular molecules persist unchanged, but because organisational relations are continually reproduced through ongoing activity.

This perspective transformed the theoretical landscape by treating biological organisation as an explanatory principle in its own right. Living systems came to be understood as self-maintaining unities whose persistence depends upon organised activity rather than passive material constitution. Constraint closure provided a formal account of this organisation and helped explain how living systems could maintain their own conditions of existence despite continual material turnover.

APS fully endorses this insight. The concept of organised persistence builds directly upon the recognition that living systems exist through the continual maintenance of organisational relations. APS therefore shares autonomy theory's commitment to organisational explanation while extending that commitment across questions of agency, scale, development, cognition, ecology, and evolution.

## Points of Convergence with APS

APS and autonomy theory share a number of foundational commitments that place them within a common organisational tradition. Both reject attempts to define life solely through constituent components, molecular inventories, or externally assigned functions and instead treat organisation as the primary object of biological explanation. Living systems are understood as entities whose continued existence depends upon ongoing self-maintaining activity rather than static structural properties.

Both frameworks further recognise that biological organisation is intrinsically normative. Living systems continuously distinguish between processes that contribute to persistence and processes that undermine it. This distinction does not arise from external observers or imposed goals but emerges from the organisational requirements of continued existence. Biological organisation therefore carries its own standards of success and failure.

Most importantly, both autonomy theory and APS recognise that living systems actively participate in their own persistence. Organisms do not simply undergo biological processes; they continuously contribute to the maintenance of the conditions that make those processes possible. Biological organisation is therefore inseparable from the activity through which that organisation is sustained.

These shared commitments explain why APS should be understood as an extension of the autonomy tradition rather than as a competing framework. APS accepts the organisational foundations established by autonomy theory and builds directly upon them.

## From Organisation to Agency

One of the most significant developments within contemporary organisational biology has been the increasing differentiation between organisation, regulation, and agency. Early autonomy theory successfully established the importance of organisational closure and self-maintenance, but subsequent work has shown that these concepts do not exhaust the explanatory requirements of living systems. APS incorporates these developments by distinguishing several interconnected but non-identical dimensions of biological organisation.

### Constraint Closure

The concept of constraint closure occupies a central position within autonomy theory. Living systems consist of networks of constraints that mutually contribute to one another's maintenance while collectively sustaining the organised processes upon which the system depends. Through this organisation, the activities of the system continuously regenerate the conditions that make those activities possible.

Constraint closure therefore provides an explanation for how living systems maintain themselves despite continual material turnover. Organisms persist not because particular molecules remain unchanged, but because organisational relations are continuously reproduced through ongoing activity. Closure identifies the organisational architecture that underlies biological persistence and provides one of the most influential accounts of biological organisation in contemporary theoretical biology.

APS fully incorporates this insight. Organised persistence presupposes forms of constraint closure capable of sustaining viability through time. Without organisational closure, living systems would lack the capacity to maintain the conditions required for continued existence.

### Regulation

Although constraint closure explains how living systems maintain organisational integrity, it does not by itself explain how those systems coordinate, modify, and control their own activities under changing conditions. This additional explanatory requirement has led autonomy theorists to develop increasingly sophisticated accounts of biological regulation.

Regulation concerns the organised processes through which living systems monitor, coordinate, and modify ongoing activity in ways that contribute to persistence. Closure identifies the mutual dependence of constraints within an organised system, whereas regulation concerns the active control of processes occurring within that system. Living systems must therefore be understood not only as self-maintaining organisations but also as systems capable of reorganising activity in response to changing circumstances.

APS incorporates regulation as a central component of viability-oriented organisation. The maintenance of viability depends not merely upon organisational closure but upon the capacity of living systems to modify ongoing activity in ways that preserve persistence under conditions of continual change.

### Agency

The concept of agency introduces a further explanatory dimension. Whereas closure explains organisational maintenance and regulation explains internal control, agency concerns the active relationship between a living system and its environment. Living systems do not simply respond mechanically to external influences. They engage with environmental conditions in ways that contribute to their continued viability.

Through movement, behaviour, physiological adjustment, environmental modification, and other forms of activity, organisms actively participate in shaping the circumstances under which persistence becomes possible. Agency therefore cannot be reduced either to organisational closure or to regulation alone. Closure explains how the system maintains itself. Regulation explains how the system coordinates internal processes. Agency explains how the system acts in relation to the wider conditions affecting viability.

From this perspective, biological agency is not an additional property imposed upon organised systems. Rather, it emerges from the organisation of living systems themselves. Organisms act because they are viability-oriented forms of organised persistence whose continued existence depends upon actively maintaining favourable relationships with their environments.

### APS Integration

APS integrates constraint closure, regulation, and agency within a unified account of viability-oriented organisation. Rather than treating these concepts as competing explanations, APS regards them as complementary dimensions of a single organisational architecture.

Constraint closure explains how living systems maintain the organisational conditions of persistence. Regulation explains how those systems coordinate and modify internal activity in ways that contribute to viability. Agency explains how organised systems engage with their environments in ways that preserve or enhance the conditions of continued existence.

Together these concepts provide a progressively richer account of living organisation. Organisms are not merely constraint-closed systems, nor merely regulatory systems, nor merely agents. They are viability-oriented forms of organised persistence whose continued existence depends upon the integration of organisational maintenance, internal regulation, and active engagement with the world.

This integration represents one of the most important ways in which APS extends contemporary autonomy theory. By explicitly connecting closure, regulation, agency, and viability within a common explanatory framework, APS provides a more comprehensive account of how living systems maintain themselves, adapt to changing conditions, and persist across time.

## The Scope of the Autonomy Framework

Despite these deep points of convergence, autonomy theory does not by itself resolve all of the explanatory questions that arise once living systems are understood as organised, self-maintaining entities. Constraint closure, regulation, and agency provide powerful tools for understanding biological organisation, but further distinctions are required if biological explanation is to encompass development, evolution, diagnosis, and multiscale organisation within a single framework.

APS therefore extends autonomy theory in several directions. These extensions do not replace the autonomy framework but seek to clarify questions that autonomy theory itself increasingly encounters. In particular, APS develops more explicit accounts of the distinction between definition and diagnosis, the organisation of living systems across scales, the integration of agency and viability, and the relationship between organisational analysis and evolutionary transformation. Each of these extensions arises from problems that autonomy theory has helped bring into focus but does not always address systematically.

The resulting relationship is best understood as one of theoretical continuity. APS accepts the organisational foundations established by autonomy theory while developing additional conceptual distinctions that allow those foundations to be integrated into a broader explanatory architecture.

## Definition and Diagnosis

Autonomy theory provides a powerful account of the organisation of living systems, but it does not always distinguish clearly between questions concerning what life is and questions concerning how life is identified. This distinction becomes increasingly important whenever organisational concepts are applied to empirical investigation, life detection, artificial systems, or borderline biological cases.

APS therefore introduces an explicit distinction between definition and diagnosis. Definition concerns the nature of living organisation itself. Within APS, life is understood as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation sustained through organised persistence. Definition seeks to identify the organisational characteristics that make living systems the kinds of systems they are.

Diagnosis concerns a different question. Rather than asking what life is, diagnosis asks how living organisation can be recognised, investigated, and assessed in practice. Diagnostic procedures therefore involve evidence, indicators, perturbation responses, organisational signatures, and other empirical methods through which viability-oriented organisation can be inferred.

Maintaining this distinction prevents conceptual confusion. Biological organisation should not be defined in terms of the methods used to detect it, just as the existence of a phenomenon should not be confused with the techniques employed to investigate it. APS therefore treats definition and diagnosis as complementary but distinct explanatory domains.

This distinction also allows APS to connect theoretical biology with empirical research more directly than organisational accounts sometimes permit. Organisational concepts can guide investigation without being reduced to investigative procedures, and empirical evidence can inform biological explanation without becoming identical with the phenomena under investigation.

## Scale and Organisation

Autonomy theory established that biological organisation cannot be reduced to the properties of isolated components. APS accepts this conclusion while arguing that organisation must also be understood across multiple interacting scales of activity. Living systems persist through processes distributed across spatial, temporal, physiological, developmental, ecological, and evolutionary scales, and no single scale possesses exclusive explanatory priority.

The importance of scale becomes particularly evident when considering the continuity of living organisation through change. Organisms maintain viability despite continual turnover of components, developmental transformation, environmental fluctuation, and evolutionary modification. The persistence of organisation therefore cannot be identified with any particular material configuration. Instead, continuity emerges through coordinated activity distributed across multiple scales of biological organisation.

APS consequently rejects both strict reductionism and simple hierarchical models of explanation. Biological organisation is neither fully determined by lower-level processes nor adequately explained through abstract higher-level descriptions alone. Explanatory understanding emerges from analysing how processes operating at different scales contribute to the maintenance of viability-oriented organisation.

This perspective also clarifies the relationship between explanatory priority and ontological priority. APS does not deny the material constitution of living systems. Rather, it argues that biological explanation requires identifying the organisational conditions under which material processes become intelligible as biological processes. Organisation therefore possesses explanatory priority without requiring ontological separation from the material systems in which it is realised.

Scale thus becomes a central component of organised persistence. Living systems are neither collections of independent parts nor static wholes. They are multiscale organisations whose continued existence depends upon the coordination of processes operating across diverse spatial and temporal domains.

## Evolutionary Continuity and Historical Transformation

A major extension introduced by APS concerns the relationship between organisation and evolution. Classical autonomy theory focused primarily upon the organisational conditions that enable individual systems to maintain themselves through time. APS accepts this foundation but argues that organisational analysis must also account for historical continuity, lineage persistence, variation, inheritance, and evolutionary transformation.

Living systems do not merely persist in the present. They emerge from historically continuous lineages whose organisational characteristics have been maintained, modified, and transformed across generations. Understanding living organisation therefore requires understanding not only how organisms maintain themselves now but also how viable forms of organisation remain historically continuous through evolutionary time.

This shift has important consequences for biological explanation. Organisational closure explains how living systems maintain themselves. Evolutionary analysis explains how forms of viable organisation become historically transformed. APS therefore interprets evolution as the historical transformation of viability-oriented organisation rather than as a process reducible to genes, traits, populations, or selection considered in isolation.

Variation plays a central role within this account. APS rejects the view that variation exists as raw material independent of living organisation and subsequently becomes shaped by evolutionary processes. Instead, variation emerges within already organised systems whose activities generate developmental, behavioural, physiological, and ecological possibilities. Novelty is therefore not imposed upon living systems from outside but arises through the transformation of historically continuous forms of organisation.

Inheritance provides the complementary process through which continuity is maintained. Organisms do not transmit static structures across generations. Rather, they contribute to the reconstruction of organisational conditions that allow viable forms of persistence to continue. Evolutionary continuity therefore depends upon both transformation and reconstruction, novelty and stability, variation and inheritance.

Natural selection remains an indispensable component of evolutionary explanation, but APS situates selection within a broader organisational framework. Selection does not create biological organisation. Instead, it differentially stabilises historically existing forms of viability-oriented organisation whose persistence has already been established through organised activity. Evolutionary explanation consequently requires understanding both the organisational conditions that make living systems possible and the historical processes through which forms of organisation become transformed across lineages.

From this perspective, autonomy theory and APS address different but complementary dimensions of biological explanation. Autonomy theory illuminates the organisational architecture of living systems. APS extends this architecture into a theory of historical continuity and evolutionary transformation, showing how organised persistence remains continuous while continually changing across evolutionary time.

## Normativity and Viability

Autonomy theory played a major role in reintroducing normativity into biological explanation. By showing that living systems maintain themselves through organised activity, it became possible to explain why certain processes contribute to persistence while others undermine it. Biological normativity no longer needed to be understood as externally imposed purpose or observer-relative evaluation. Instead, norms emerged from the organisational requirements of living systems themselves.

APS fully accepts this insight while seeking to clarify the role of viability within biological normativity. Constraint closure identifies the organisational architecture through which living systems maintain themselves, but the significance of that organisation becomes intelligible only in relation to viability. Living systems continuously confront conditions that either support or threaten their continued existence. Processes, behaviours, and organisational arrangements therefore acquire biological significance through their consequences for viability.

This emphasis on viability helps explain why biological norms differ from externally assigned functions. A process is not biologically significant because an observer attributes a purpose to it. Rather, it is significant because it contributes to the maintenance, restoration, or enhancement of the conditions under which organised persistence remains possible. Biological normativity is therefore endogenous to living organisation itself.

APS also emphasises that viability should not be equated with simple survival. Living systems often engage in activities whose significance can only be understood in relation to broader life histories rather than immediate persistence. Reproduction, development, dispersal, parental investment, and even forms of programmed death may contribute to the viability of historically continuous forms of organisation despite imposing costs upon particular organisms. Viability therefore concerns the successful realisation of organised biological continuity rather than mere short-term persistence.

Agency plays a central role within this account. Living systems do not simply possess norms; they actively realise them through viability-oriented activity. Organisms continuously regulate internal processes, modify relationships with their environments, and respond to changing circumstances in ways that contribute to the maintenance of favourable conditions for persistence. Biological normativity is therefore inseparable from biological agency. The distinction between beneficial and detrimental activity emerges through the active maintenance of viability itself.

APS consequently treats normativity, agency, and viability as mutually reinforcing aspects of organised persistence. Constraint closure explains how organised systems maintain themselves. Agency explains how those systems actively engage with the conditions affecting persistence. Viability provides the normative horizon within which such activity becomes biologically meaningful. Together they form an integrated account of why living systems act, how they maintain themselves, and what distinguishes successful from unsuccessful biological activity.

## The APS Perspective

From an APS perspective, autonomy theory represents one of the most important achievements of contemporary theoretical biology. It re-established organisation as a legitimate explanatory concept, demonstrated the importance of constraint closure, and provided a powerful account of how living systems maintain themselves through organised activity. Many of the central concepts employed throughout APS would be difficult to formulate without the organisational foundations established by the autonomy tradition.

APS therefore does not reject autonomy theory. Nor does it seek to replace closure of constraints with an alternative organisational principle. Instead, APS accepts the central insights of autonomy theory while integrating them into a broader explanatory framework centred on viability-oriented organised persistence.

This integration proceeds in several directions simultaneously. APS distinguishes definition from diagnosis, thereby clarifying the relationship between biological organisation and empirical investigation. It formalises scale as a central dimension of biological explanation and shows how organisational continuity can be maintained across changing material implementations. It integrates closure, regulation, and agency within a common viability-oriented framework. Finally, it extends organisational analysis beyond individual systems into questions of historical continuity, inheritance, variation, and evolutionary transformation.

The resulting framework preserves the strengths of autonomy theory while connecting them to a wider range of biological phenomena. Constraint closure remains indispensable, but it is situated within a richer explanatory architecture that also includes regulation, agency, viability, development, ecology, cognition, and evolution. Organised persistence consequently becomes intelligible not merely as self-maintenance but as the continuing activity through which living systems sustain themselves, adapt to changing circumstances, and participate in historically continuous processes of transformation.

APS therefore represents neither a departure from nor a correction of autonomy theory. Rather, it should be understood as a systematic extension of the organisational tradition that autonomy theory helped establish. The relationship between the two frameworks is one of continuity and development rather than competition. APS seeks to preserve the explanatory achievements of autonomy theory while integrating them into a more comprehensive account of living organisation across scales and through time.

## Summary

Autonomy theory re-established biological explanation on an organisational basis by demonstrating the importance of constraint closure, self-maintenance, and endogenous normativity. It showed that living systems are not merely collections of components but organised unities whose persistence depends upon the continual regeneration of the conditions required for their own existence.

APS builds directly upon these foundations. It accepts the central importance of organisational closure while extending the autonomy framework through a more explicit treatment of regulation, agency, viability, multiscale organisation, historical continuity, and evolutionary transformation. These extensions do not replace autonomy theory but develop its central insights into a broader explanatory architecture.

Within APS, living systems are understood as viability-oriented forms of organised persistence whose continued existence depends upon the integration of closure, regulation, agency, and historical continuity. Biological explanation therefore extends beyond the organisation of individual systems to encompass the developmental, ecological, cognitive, and evolutionary processes through which organised persistence is maintained and transformed.
