## Introduction

A physiologist studying a strawberry plant might follow water, carbon, and nutrients through a network of connected shoots. A demographer might count rooted shoots that can survive independently. A geneticist might trace those shoots to the same founding seed. Each inquiry can be precise, yet the unit it identifies can differ. A connected group of shoots can share resources, individual shoots can acquire physiological independence, and the products of the founding seed can retain a common ancestry after their connections have disappeared. Which of these observations establishes what the organism is?

The question matters because organismal boundaries carry explanatory commitments. To say that a plant recovers from drought, that a symbiotic association develops as a unit, or that a colony reproduces is to identify something whose activity and continuity the explanation must account for. Visible boundaries, genetic markers, and reproductive events can help answer that question. Their relevance depends on the kind of unity under investigation.

Jana Švorcová’s chapter “Introduction: Knowing What an Organism Is” (2024) frames the problem through agency. Her introduction to *Organismal Agency* brings philosophical treatments of Aristotle, Kant, Schelling, and process thought into conversation with biological inquiries into self-organisation, plasticity, reproduction, modularity, and the development of integrated phenotypes. The volume’s design brings a question about the character of living beings into contact with questions about what they do and how their activities are organised. Plasticity, physiological maintenance, and behaviour can all inform that inquiry, with different evidential requirements in each case.

Jan Baedke’s *The Organism* (2025) offers a complementary point of departure. He describes the organism as a *nexus concept*, one that connects questions about life, organisation, purpose, individuality, and relations with the surroundings. Its versatility helps explain why the concept recurs across biological disciplines and why the boundaries it picks out can be contested. APS takes up one question within this wider field. What makes a living system a physiologically integrated unity whose activities contribute to maintaining the conditions of its own persistence?

## From the organised body to the living process

In *Parts of Animals*, Aristotle explains bodily parts through their activities and their contributions to the life of the animal. The structure of a part, what it does, and the way the animal lives belong to the same inquiry. Modern accounts have recast that relationship in different terms, but the question remains recognisable. How does the activity of a part contribute to the life of the whole, and how does the living whole make that activity possible?

Kant gives the reciprocal relation a particularly clear formulation in the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (§§64–65). His example is a tree. A tree produces other trees, transforms material as it grows, and depends on parts that sustain one another. Kant’s account of natural purpose asks how to judge a being whose parts participate in producing and maintaining the whole through which they themselves exist. It also raises a question about the status of purposive judgement that goes beyond any particular physiological mechanism. In the tradition of *Naturphilosophie* represented by Schelling, the emphasis shifts towards the productivity of nature and the emergence of organised forms. Švorcová’s collection places these approaches alongside process philosophy, keeping open the question of how an organism comes to be and continues to exist as a unity.

The organism’s place in twentieth-century biology varied across fields. Organismal integration remained a practical concern in physiology, development, and much of ecology. In influential sectors of genetics and evolutionary theory, attention shifted towards inheritance, gene action, and population change. Nicholson (2014) describes the renewed prominence of the organism as a fundamental explanatory concept. Baedke (2025) traces that renewal across research on developmental evolution, epigenetics, and organism–environment interaction. The historical point is that the explanatory prominence of the organism changed as the questions and methods of biology changed.

Process biology makes the temporal implications explicit. Cells replace molecular constituents, tissues remodel, and developmental transitions alter anatomy and capacities. Yet these transformations can belong to the history of a single organism. Nicholson and Dupré (2018) treat organisms as relatively stable processes whose continuity depends on activity. This poses a further question for an organisational account. Which relations maintain that continuity, and under what conditions does a change modify an existing unity or bring a new one into being?

## Self-production, constraint closure, and viability

Autopoiesis gave the problem of living unity a precise theoretical formulation. Varela, Maturana, and Uribe (1974) characterised a living system through a network of processes that produces the components through which the network is continually realised. The production of a cellular boundary provides an especially clear illustration. The boundary helps maintain conditions for the reactions through which it is renewed. Its explanatory importance lies both in what it separates and in how its maintenance participates in the organisation of the cell.

Organisational autonomy broadens the inquiry into the reciprocal dependence of differentiated constraints. Montévil and Mossio (2015) use *constraint* for a relatively stable feature that channels a process over the timescale under consideration. A membrane can constrain diffusion, an enzyme can channel a reaction, and a vascular structure can direct transport. Closure concerns the relations through which constraints depend on processes channelled by other constraints and contribute in turn to the maintenance of constraints within the same organisation. Establishing closure therefore requires more than observing that processes interact or that a change feeds back on its cause. It requires evidence of reciprocal dependence among identifiable constraints and of the processes through which they are maintained.

Moreno and Mossio (2015) develop organisational closure within a theory of biological autonomy that also addresses environmental interaction, regulation, and agency. This is a close comparator for APS. Both approaches ask how living systems contribute causally to the conditions of their own existence while depending on flows of matter and energy and on relations with their surroundings. The comparison must therefore engage the developed autonomy account, including its treatment of regulation, whenever APS uses closure, viability, or ecological dependence to explain organismal persistence.

APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. It characterises an **organism** as a physiologically integrated unity of that organisation. The qualification matters because the definition of life leaves a concrete question of individuation. In a plant connected to other shoots or a host sustained by microbial partners, which relations constitute the organisation whose viability is under study? An account of organismal unity must identify the relevant dependencies, establish how far integration extends, and specify the interval over which the proposed unity persists.

*Viability* refers to the conditions under which a particular organisation can continue or recover. Whether a given temperature, loss of water, or interruption of transport supports, undermines, or permits recovery depends on the organism, its stage of development, its surroundings, and the duration of the change. *Viability orientation* refers to the contribution of organised activity to maintaining or re-establishing those conditions. The effects of regulation can vary with circumstances, and regulation can fail. Reversible dormancy also shows why continuity must be assessed over an interval that includes the conditions of recovery or resumed activity.

## Organismal agency, function, and normativity

APS defines biological agency as **viability-oriented organisational activity**. Organism and agency direct attention to different aspects of the same living system. Organismal inquiry asks what constitutes the integrated unity. Inquiry into agency asks what that organisation does in relation to the conditions of its persistence. Repair, physiological regulation, and changes in how the organism engages with its surroundings can all warrant an agency attribution when their contribution to viability can be established.

Rosslenbroich, Kümmell, and Bembé (2024) likewise locate agency in intrinsic maintenance as well as in activity directed towards the surroundings. Moreno and Mossio (2015, ch. 4) distinguish the constitutive organisation of autonomy from its interactive dimension, for which they use the term *agency*. APS includes both internal maintenance and activity in relation to the surroundings where evidence connects the activity to the viability of the candidate system. The comparative question is whether that difference in scope changes which processes warrant an attribution or improves an explanation of their contribution to persistence. An organ can contribute to an organism’s agency, while attribution of independent agency to that organ requires evidence that it sustains a viability-oriented organisation of its own.

Viability also gives a precise sense to biological normativity. A change in water balance can be favourable, damaging, or restorative in relation to the conditions sustaining a specific plant. These differences can be investigated through effects on tissues, transport, growth, and recovery. They involve organism-relative consequences and can be established without any assumption that the organism represents a goal or intends an outcome.

Function requires a further distinction. An organisational account asks whether an effect contributes to the maintenance of the organisation that sustains the conditions for its production or operation (Mossio, Saborido, and Moreno 2009). An evolutionary account can ask why a trait with a given effect became established or was retained in a population. Both questions can arise for the same trait. The present role of stomatal regulation in a plant’s water and carbon economy and the evolutionary history of particular stomatal traits require evidence about different causal relations.

## Physiological individuality and evolutionary individuality

Individuals enter biological explanations in several ways. A physiologist may identify an integrated system of metabolism, transport, and regulation. An evolutionary biologist may identify a reproductive unit, an interactor whose properties affect differential success, or a lineage whose continuity is being traced. Godfrey-Smith (2013) distinguishes metabolic living units from Darwinian individuals defined through participation in reproduction and evolutionary processes. Pradeu (2016) argues that a fuller account of biological individuality requires both physiological and evolutionary perspectives. A familiar animal can satisfy several criteria simultaneously, which helps explain why their differences become particularly apparent in plants, symbioses, and colonies.

Queller and Strassmann (2009) offer a different approach to organismality through the evolution of cooperation and conflict among components. Their account asks how a collective acquires a high degree of common interest and how conflict among its parts is limited. This is especially informative in cases where components retain some capacity to reproduce independently. Evidence of cooperation and conflict suppression addresses how a collective evolves as a unit. Evidence of physiological integration addresses how the collective currently sustains itself. When both questions arise, their relation is an empirical problem.

Clarke (2012, 2025) shows what is at stake when researchers need to count individuals in organisms with modular growth, clonal reproduction, or shifting connections. A counting rule encodes assumptions about which biological processes matter to the inquiry. APS uses *organised persistence* to investigate continuity through change and *organism* to identify a physiologically integrated living unity. These concepts overlap when the research concerns the continuity of that physiological unity. An investigation of the evolutionary history of its lineage can follow a different unit.

## Plants and the evidence for organismal boundaries

Plant biology makes it possible to examine organismal unity directly. White (1979) treated the plant as a population of repeated parts whose growth and mortality can be investigated in their own right. A shoot can respond to local light, water, or damage, while its survival and growth remain affected by inputs from elsewhere in the plant. Yilmaz and Dupré (2025) investigate plant individuality through physiological coordination, particularly the balance between tissues that supply resources and those that use or store them. This directs attention to transport and allocation across growing shoots, roots, leaves, and storage tissues.

Yilmaz Silverman (2026), reviewing Clarke’s *The Units of Life* and Baedke’s *The Organism*, distinguishes inquiries that begin with counting individuals from inquiries that begin with measuring biological processes. Plant physiology can proceed by measuring transport, resource allocation, or stress responses and using those results to investigate the extent of integration. A demographic inquiry may instead need an operational counting rule at the outset. The order matters because the choice of unit can shape what the research reveals.

Consider a rooted strawberry shoot joined by a runner to another shoot. Common descent identifies their genealogical relationship. Tracing water or carbon can establish whether they share resources under specified conditions. Measurements before and after separation can help determine how physiological dependence changes with establishment, season, or disturbance. A single observation of transport establishes exchange. Evidence that transport contributes to the maintenance and recovery of the connected system would support a stronger claim about physiological integration. To determine whether the connected shoots form a single organism, we would also need to establish whether they participate in maintaining a common physiological organisation, or whether one shoot depends on resources supplied by the other while each sustains a distinguishable organisation. Where the shoots become independently capable of sustaining their organisation, a physiological description can recognise that change even as a genealogical description continues to trace them to one founder.

This example makes the comparative burden clear. Plant physiologists and philosophers of plant individuality already investigate coordination, resource exchange, and the changing extent of integration. APS can connect those findings to questions about viability, constraint maintenance, and persistence through change. An additional explanatory contribution would require a specified result, such as a prediction about when a dependence becomes necessary for recovery that the strongest existing physiological account would leave open.

## Ecological dependence and symbiotic unity

The activities that sustain an organism take place through exchanges with its surroundings. Plants exchange gases and water with the atmosphere, obtain mineral nutrients from the soil, and can depend on microbial partners for access to resources. Animals depend on food, oxygen, and other organisms, including resident microbes that can contribute to development or metabolism. Many of these relations are regulated by the living systems involved. Their importance to persistence makes them part of an explanation of how the organism lives.

Baedke (2025) links two difficulties for organism-centred biology. One is to explain what integrates the organism. The other is to establish the scope of that unity where the organism is closely coupled to its surroundings. Symbiosis makes the connection tangible. In the holobiont debate, Douglas and Werren (2016) distinguish the diversity of host–microbe associations from stronger claims about their evolutionary unity. Pradeu (2016) shows how particular microbes can contribute to physiological individuality where they are sufficiently integrated into the functioning of the host. Physiological contribution, reciprocal maintenance, reliable transmission, and participation in selection are different relations, each requiring its own evidence.

For APS, a partner’s necessity under specified conditions establishes dependence. An attribution of organismal unity to the association requires evidence that the partners participate in sustaining an integrated organisation whose viability can be specified. The relevant inquiry might examine the persistence of the association through partner turnover, the regulation of exchanges, or the effects of interrupting those exchanges. A boundary can therefore be drawn differently for metabolic exchange, physiological integration, and evolutionary transmission, with each claim answerable to the processes being studied. [Organism–World Coupling — Why Agency Is Not Control](/articles/organism-world-coupling-agency-not-control/) develops the role of regulated ecological relations in organismal persistence.

## Organisms in development and evolution

An organism’s unity is formed during development and can be reorganised as its anatomy, activities, and environmental relations change. A new root system can shift a shoot’s dependence on a parent plant. Metamorphosis can alter feeding, locomotion, and habitat while preserving continuity across the transition. These examples make development part of the explanation of what the organism is at a given time and how it continues through change. [Development and Biological Individuality](/articles/development-and-biological-individuality/) examines the developmental and ecological relations that support such continuity.

Evolutionary explanations can then ask how forms of organismal integration arose, diversified, or were lost. They can also investigate how activities performed by organisms affect the conditions in which evolutionary change occurs. Lewontin (2000) emphasises reciprocal relations among genes, organisms, and environments. Niche construction theory develops one consequence of this reciprocity by investigating how organisms modify environments in ways that can affect subsequent selection (Odling-Smee, Laland, and Feldman 2003). A finding that an activity currently contributes to viability establishes an organismal effect. Showing that it alters selection, inheritance, or the distribution of variants across generations requires further evidence.

APS uses organised persistence to connect questions about present operation, developmental continuity, and evolutionary transformation. The history of a regulatory capacity and its operation in a living organism are related explanatory targets. Their connection can be investigated by tracing the processes through which inherited organisation develops, acts in a particular environment, and has consequences for subsequent generations. [Agency, Evolution, and the Architecture of Biological Explanation](/articles/biological-agency-evolution-and-the-architecture-of-biological-explanation/) develops those distinctions.

## What an organismal explanation must show

An explanation of organismal unity must identify what persists, how its organisation is maintained or re-established, and which spatial and temporal extents the evidence supports. A mechanistic explanation can identify how a transport process operates. A developmental explanation can show how the transport system is built or reorganised. An organisational explanation can investigate how that system contributes to maintaining the living unity and how other processes maintain the system itself. The value of relating these explanations depends on the question being asked and on the causal dependencies the evidence establishes.

The APS account gives that inquiry a compact formulation. An organism is a physiologically integrated unity of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Organised persistence concerns its continuity through change, and biological agency concerns its viability-oriented activity. The formulation provides a shared vocabulary for connecting findings from physiology, development, ecology, and evolution. Its explanatory contribution beyond organisational autonomy, process biology, or physiological accounts of individuality remains a comparative question to be resolved for particular cases.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

What makes a living system a physiologically integrated unity, and how can that unity be identified when material constituents, developmental capacities, and ecological relations change?

### Architectural Role

This article establishes organismal unity as an explanatory target. It connects the study of physiological integration to the APS account of living organisation and shows why evidence about the organisation currently sustained by a system needs to be distinguished from evidence about descent, reproduction, and evolutionary participation.

### Preceding Dependencies

The argument depends on the concepts of viability, biological organisation, constraint closure, and organised persistence. Viability specifies the conditions under which a system can continue or recover. Constraint closure concerns the reciprocal maintenance of constraints. Organised persistence concerns continuity through change. Together these concepts make it possible to ask which candidate system sustains the organisation whose persistence is being explained.

### Related and Subsequent Developments

[Organism — The Unity of Viability-Oriented Organisation](/articles/organism-the-unity-of-viability-oriented-organisation/) develops the APS account of organismal unity. [Biological Individuality — Organised Persistence Through Time](/articles/biological-individuality-organised-persistence-through-time/) and [Development and Biological Individuality](/articles/development-and-biological-individuality/) examine continuity, development, and the formation of living unities. [Organism–World Coupling — Why Agency Is Not Control](/articles/organism-world-coupling-agency-not-control/) follows the relations through which those unities persist in their surroundings.

### Related Questions

When does a connected set of plant modules sustain a common physiological organisation? Which partners in a symbiosis participate in maintaining the same living unity? How does organismal continuity survive dormancy, metamorphosis, or regeneration? Under what conditions does the physiological unit also constitute the relevant evolutionary individual?

### Position Within APS

The organism is a principal case of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Agency concerns the viability-oriented activities through which that organisation is enacted. Process concerns its constitution and continuity through change. Scale concerns the spatial and temporal extents over which the relevant dependencies, activities, and consequences are organised. An attribution of organismal unity draws on evidence about all three.
