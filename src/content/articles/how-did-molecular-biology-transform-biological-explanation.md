## Introduction

A genetic map could tell an investigator where one hereditary factor lay relative to another by showing how often they parted company in a cross. The map had explanatory and predictive value even when the chemical nature of the factors was unknown. Molecular biology added questions that the map itself could leave open. What material carried the difference? How could a cell copy a particular sequence? How did the sequence contribute to the production of a protein, and why was that protein made under some conditions and scarcely at all under others?

These questions called for different experiments. Together, their answers changed what counted as a satisfactory explanation of inheritance and gene action.

## What carried a hereditary difference?

In 1944, Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty isolated a fraction from type III pneumococci. After exposure to the fraction, some descendants of previously unencapsulated recipient bacteria produced the donor type’s capsule and transmitted that property. The persistence of the change across generations was what made the result evidence about heredity.

The active fraction’s chemical properties led the investigators to identify it as DNA. They qualified the identification by the limits of the methods used to test purity. In 1946, McCarty and Avery reported that purified deoxyribonuclease destroyed the fraction’s transforming activity, strengthening the case that DNA was responsible. The experiments identified the material carrying a particular hereditary difference in pneumococci. How DNA encoded the difference, and how the recipient produced the capsule, remained separate questions. ([Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty 1944](https://doi.org/10.1084/jem.79.2.137) · [McCarty and Avery 1946](https://doi.org/10.1084/jem.83.2.89))

## How could DNA be copied?

In 1953, the X-ray diffraction studies published by Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling, and by Maurice Wilkins, A. R. Stokes, and H. R. Wilson, provided evidence about DNA’s geometry. James Watson and Francis Crick proposed a double helix with specific pairing between bases on opposite strands. Each strand thereby specified the sequence of its complement. If the strands separated, each could serve as a template. A structure had suggested a mechanism that investigators could test. ([Watson and Crick 1953](https://doi.org/10.1038/171737a0) · [Wilkins, Stokes, and Wilson 1953](https://doi.org/10.1038/171738a0) · [Franklin and Gosling 1953](https://doi.org/10.1038/171740a0))

In 1958, Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl followed the density of DNA as *Escherichia coli* grew after a shift from heavy to light nitrogen. After one generation the DNA formed an intermediate band. A conservative model, in which the parental duplex remained intact and an entirely new duplex was made, predicted separate heavy and light bands. The intermediate band was consistent with both semiconservative copying and a simple dispersive model. After two generations, intermediate and light bands appeared together. A simple dispersive model instead predicted a single band shifted towards the light end. When the authors separated the components of intermediate DNA by heating, they recovered heavy and light fractions. Together the results supported the inference that each daughter duplex received one intact parental strand and one newly made strand. The experiment established a pattern of inheritance of DNA strands in this system. Identifying the enzymes and their roles required other evidence. ([Meselson and Stahl 1958](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.44.7.671))

## How did a sequence specify a product?

Copying DNA uses complementarity between nucleotide sequences. Protein synthesis poses a different problem. It connects an RNA sequence with a sequence made of amino acids.

In 1961, Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei added synthetic RNA composed entirely of uracil residues to a cell-free preparation from *E. coli*. The preparation incorporated phenylalanine into a polypeptide. That result established the correspondence between the repeated RNA template and the amino acid incorporated. A uniform string of uracils offers no way to count the bases in each coding unit, because every possible grouping gives the same repeated pattern.

Independent genetic experiments published later that year by Francis Crick, Leslie Barnett, Sydney Brenner, and Richard Watts-Tobin used combinations of mutations that shifted the reading frame. Their results supported a code read in groups of three bases, with a multiple of three acknowledged as a less likely possibility. The two lines of evidence addressed different questions. One identified a coding assignment and the other addressed the organisation of the code. With triplet coding established, the assignment could be stated as UUU specifying phenylalanine. ([Nirenberg and Matthaei 1961](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.47.10.1588) · [Crick et al. 1961](https://doi.org/10.1038/1921227a0))

## What determined whether a gene was expressed?

A cell can carry the same coding sequence under two conditions and produce different amounts of the corresponding protein. This made the control of expression a question in its own right.

In 1959, Arthur Pardee, François Jacob, and Jacques Monod followed the production of β-galactosidase after transferring genetic material between strains of *E. coli*. In one cross, enzyme synthesis began even without an inducer. It later ceased, and addition of an inducer restored synthesis. The timing and dominance results supported an inference that the regulatory gene produced a diffusible substance capable of repressing enzyme synthesis.

Jacob and Monod’s 1961 operon model brought that result together with evidence about the operator. A functional regulatory gene could supply repressor to targets elsewhere in the same cell. An operator mutation that made expression constitutive affected the genes associated with that particular operator. The two observations gave grounds for distinguishing a diffusible regulator from a local site of control. Gilbert and Müller-Hill subsequently isolated the lac repressor and showed that it bound DNA carrying the operator. The biochemical work made it possible to investigate directly an interaction that genetic evidence had led researchers to propose. ([Pardee, Jacob, and Monod 1959](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-2836(59)80045-0) · [Jacob and Monod 1961](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-2836(61)80072-7) · [Gilbert and Müller-Hill 1966](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.56.6.1891) · [Gilbert and Müller-Hill 1967](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.58.6.2415))

## Conclusion

The molecular transformation consisted of several connected advances. Chemical identification established what carried a hereditary difference in the transformation experiment. Complementarity and density measurements supported an account of how DNA was copied. Synthetic RNA made a coding assignment experimentally accessible. The operon model explained how the production of enzymes could depend on cellular conditions.

The reach of each explanation follows from the question and evidence. Knowing a coding sequence helps explain the amino-acid sequence of a protein. Understanding its physiological effect may also require evidence about where it is expressed, what it interacts with, and how its activity changes the behaviour of a cell or organism. For APS, the historical lesson is that the explanatory importance of a molecular mechanism can be established precisely by identifying what it makes intelligible and which further dependencies matter for the question at hand. ## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How did molecular biology make hereditary transmission and gene expression explicable through identifiable materials, structures, and mechanisms?

### Architectural Role

Connects the history of experimental genetics with the emergence of molecular explanations of heredity and cellular activity.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

Classical genetics supplied methods for tracking and locating hereditary differences. Cell biology and biochemistry supplied ways to investigate their material basis and effects.

### Related or Subsequent Explanatory Developments

Genome-scale research extends the inquiry to relationships among sequences, patterns of expression, and cellular or organismal traits.

### Related Explanatory Questions

How can an inherited difference be traced? What evidence connects sequence variation with physiological effects? Which regulatory conditions account for variation in expression?

### Explanatory Boundaries

The examples concern particular systems and distinguish evidence for a mechanism from claims about how widely it applies.

### Position Within APS

Provides historical cases in which evidence about molecular mechanisms and cellular conditions answers connected questions about biological organisation.
