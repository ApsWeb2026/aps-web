## Why Compare APS and the Free Energy Principle?

Among contemporary attempts to understand living systems, few frameworks have attracted as much attention as the Free Energy Principle (FEP). Developed initially within theoretical neuroscience and subsequently extended to biology, cognition, and complex systems, FEP proposes a mathematically unified account of how self-organising systems maintain themselves in uncertain environments. Through the language of variational free energy, active inference, and organism–environment coupling, it seeks to explain how living systems preserve their organisation while continually interacting with changing conditions.

The Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework begins from a different starting point. Rather than asking how adaptive behaviour can be modelled mathematically, APS investigates the biological organisation that makes adaptive behaviour possible. It proposes that life is best understood as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation, and that biological explanation becomes more coherent when concepts such as agency, function, development, evolution, ecology, and cognition are interpreted as complementary explanatory perspectives on organised persistence.

Because both frameworks address self-maintaining living systems, they are frequently perceived as competing attempts to explain life. Yet this interpretation risks obscuring an important possibility. Similar biological phenomena can be investigated through different explanatory priorities. One framework may seek a formal mathematical description of adaptive behaviour, while another seeks to identify the biological organisation that constitutes living systems. These approaches need not be mutually exclusive simply because they employ different conceptual vocabularies or explanatory methods.

This article argues that APS and the Free Energy Principle are best understood as investigating different dimensions of biological explanation. FEP provides a powerful formal framework for modelling adaptive systems under conditions of uncertainty. APS investigates the organisational conditions that make such adaptive systems biologically possible. The principal difference therefore concerns neither empirical observations nor commitment to self-maintaining organisation, but the explanatory priority assigned to formal description and biological organisation.

The purpose of this comparison is therefore neither to replace nor to reduce the Free Energy Principle. Instead, it seeks to clarify the explanatory role of each framework, identify their principal areas of convergence and divergence, and consider how formal mathematical modelling and organisational biological explanation may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of living systems.

## The Free Energy Principle: A Formal Framework for Self-Maintaining Systems

The Free Energy Principle (FEP) is a general theoretical framework that seeks to explain how self-organising systems maintain their integrity while continually interacting with changing and uncertain environments. Originally developed by Karl Friston within theoretical neuroscience, it has subsequently been extended to biology, cognition, and complex adaptive systems as a proposed unifying principle for understanding adaptive organisation.

At its core, FEP proposes that living systems maintain their organisation by remaining within a restricted set of viable states despite continual environmental perturbation. Formally, this persistence is described through the minimisation of variational free energy, a mathematical quantity that provides an upper bound on the surprise associated with a system's sensory states. Although living systems cannot directly minimise surprise itself, they can minimise variational free energy through continual adjustment of their internal states and interactions with the environment.

Within this framework, organisms are understood as maintaining themselves through continuous cycles of perception and action. Perception updates the system's internal states in response to sensory input, while action changes the environment in ways that reduce discrepancies between predicted and encountered states. These coupled processes have become formalised within the broader framework of Active Inference, which describes adaptive behaviour as the continual reduction of uncertainty through coordinated perception and action.

A central feature of the Free Energy Principle is its treatment of organism–environment boundaries. These are commonly described using the concept of Markov blankets, statistical boundaries that separate internal states from external states while allowing structured exchange through sensory and active states. Within FEP, Markov blankets provide the formal architecture through which organisms maintain distinguishable identities while remaining dynamically coupled to their environments.

The explanatory ambition of FEP extends beyond particular biological mechanisms. Rather than proposing a theory of metabolism, genetics, development, or evolution individually, it seeks a common mathematical framework capable of describing adaptive self-organisation across multiple domains. This broad scope has contributed to its influence within theoretical biology, cognitive science, neuroscience, and philosophy of science, where it has been presented as a candidate unifying principle for understanding living systems.

Despite this breadth, the Free Energy Principle does not prescribe a single biological mechanism through which adaptive organisation is achieved. Instead, it provides a formal language for describing how self-organising systems can remain within viable regions of their state space while continually responding to environmental uncertainty. Whether this formal description should also be regarded as a constitutive biological explanation remains an active topic of scholarly discussion, but there is broad agreement that the Free Energy Principle represents one of the most sophisticated contemporary frameworks for modelling adaptive living systems.

## APS: Biological Organisation as the Target of Explanation

The Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework approaches the study of living systems from a different explanatory starting point. Rather than seeking a general mathematical principle capable of describing adaptive behaviour, APS investigates the biological organisation that constitutes living systems and makes adaptive behaviour possible. Its central question is therefore not how biological systems may be formally modelled, but what kind of organisation distinguishes living systems from every other organised system.

APS proposes that life is best understood as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Living systems are not defined by particular molecules, energetic processes, or informational architectures, but by the continual organisation through which they maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence. This organisation is dynamic rather than static, existing as an ongoing activity rather than as a fixed structural arrangement.

Within APS, biological agency is the present-tense activity through which this organisation is enacted and sustained. Agency does not denote conscious choice, deliberation, or representation. Nor is it an additional causal force acting alongside biological mechanisms. Rather, it identifies the viability-oriented organisational activity through which living systems regulate themselves, repair themselves, and respond to changing circumstances while preserving their own continuity.

APS further proposes that many of biology's central concepts—including function, development, evolution, ecology, cognition, and communication—are best understood as complementary explanatory perspectives on this common organisational phenomenon. These concepts do not compete to explain different kinds of biological reality. Instead, they illuminate different aspects of the organisation through which living systems persist across changing spatial and temporal conditions.

The framework is therefore organised around three complementary analytical perspectives. Agency asks what living systems do as they maintain themselves. Process examines how organisational continuity is maintained through continual material change. Scale investigates where persistence is organised across interacting spatial and temporal domains. These perspectives are not independent components of reality, nor do they represent hierarchical levels of organisation. They are complementary explanatory projections through which the same viability-oriented organisation becomes scientifically intelligible.

This emphasis on organisation also shapes APS's account of biological normativity. The significance of biological states and processes is not derived from optimisation criteria, predictive success, or informational quantities. Instead, significance arises because biological organisation differentiates between conditions that contribute to continued viability and conditions that undermine it. Function, evaluation, and purpose are therefore grounded in the organisation of living systems themselves rather than imposed by external observers or abstract formal principles.

APS consequently presents itself not as a replacement for existing biological theories but as a comparative explanatory methodology. It investigates how the diverse explanatory achievements of contemporary biology relate through the organisation required for living systems to maintain organised persistence. In this sense, APS seeks to clarify the organisational foundations upon which biological explanation depends rather than to substitute new mechanisms for those already established by empirical science.

## Different Explanatory Priorities: Organisation and Formal Description

The preceding sections have shown that APS and the Free Energy Principle share important biological commitments. Both reject passive conceptions of living systems, emphasise continual organism–environment interaction, and seek general principles capable of explaining how living systems maintain themselves despite continual change. These shared commitments explain why the two frameworks are frequently regarded as competing accounts of the same phenomenon.

Closer examination, however, suggests a different interpretation. The principal distinction between APS and the Free Energy Principle does not concern the biological phenomena they investigate, but the explanatory priorities through which those phenomena are understood.

The Free Energy Principle begins with a formal mathematical framework capable of describing adaptive systems under conditions of uncertainty. Through concepts such as variational free energy, active inference, and Markov blankets, it develops a general language for modelling the dynamics of self-organising systems. The mathematical formalism is therefore primary, while biological systems are interpreted through that formal structure.

APS proceeds in the opposite explanatory direction. It begins with the organisation that constitutes living systems and asks how biological concepts become intelligible when understood as complementary perspectives on viability-oriented organised persistence. Mathematical models, informational descriptions, and computational analyses may all contribute to biological understanding, but they do so as descriptions of organisational phenomena rather than as constitutive accounts of what living systems are.

This distinction concerns explanatory priority rather than explanatory exclusivity. APS does not deny the value of formal mathematical modelling, nor does it question the explanatory achievements of the Free Energy Principle within neuroscience, cognitive science, and theoretical biology. Equally, recognising the power of formal modelling does not by itself establish that the mathematical quantities employed by a model constitute the organisational reality of the biological systems being described.

The difference may therefore be understood as one of explanatory orientation. The Free Energy Principle asks how adaptive organisation can be described within a unified formal framework. APS asks what biological organisation must exist for adaptive behaviour, regulation, development, and evolution to occur at all. These are related but distinct explanatory questions. One investigates the formal structure of adaptive dynamics; the other investigates the biological organisation that makes adaptive dynamics possible.

Seen in this way, the two frameworks need not be interpreted as mutually exclusive alternatives. Formal modelling and organisational explanation answer different scientific questions and operate at different explanatory levels of analysis. A mathematical model may successfully characterise the behaviour of a living system without thereby identifying the organisational conditions that constitute life. Conversely, an organisational account of living systems does not eliminate the need for rigorous formal models capable of describing biological dynamics.

APS therefore proposes that biological explanation should distinguish clearly between constitutive organisation and formal description. Constitutive organisation concerns the viability-oriented, constraint-closed activity through which living systems maintain themselves. Formal description concerns the mathematical, computational, or informational frameworks used to characterise aspects of that activity. These explanatory roles are complementary but not interchangeable. Confusing them risks treating successful descriptions as though they were themselves the biological organisation they describe.

Under this interpretation, the relationship between APS and the Free Energy Principle becomes one of complementary explanatory priorities rather than theoretical opposition. APS provides an organisational framework for understanding what living systems are, while the Free Energy Principle provides a formal framework for modelling important aspects of how those systems behave. Their greatest contribution may therefore lie not in replacing one another, but in clarifying the distinct explanatory roles that organisational biology and formal modelling each play within a mature theoretical biology.

## Normativity, Agency, and Biological Significance

The differing explanatory priorities of APS and the Free Energy Principle become most apparent in their treatment of normativity: the question of what makes particular biological states, processes, or outcomes matter to a living system.

Within the Free Energy Principle, adaptive behaviour is formally characterised through the minimisation of variational free energy. Organisms maintain themselves by remaining within a restricted range of viable states, and the mathematical framework describes how perception and action contribute to this continual regulation under conditions of uncertainty. In this sense, normativity is expressed through the formal dynamics of adaptive self-maintenance.

APS approaches the same phenomenon from a different direction. Rather than asking how adaptive regulation may be formally characterised, it asks why certain conditions matter biologically in the first place. The answer lies in the organisation of living systems themselves. Because living systems actively maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence, some states contribute to continued viability while others undermine it. This asymmetry is not imposed by an observer, nor derived from an optimisation criterion. It is an intrinsic consequence of viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.

From this perspective, biological agency is the activity through which living systems continually regulate their own viability. Agency is therefore not defined by prediction, inference, representation, or optimisation. It is the present-tense organisational activity through which living systems maintain themselves despite continual material and environmental change. Adaptive regulation may subsequently be described using a variety of formal frameworks, including the Free Energy Principle, but these descriptions do not constitute the organisational activity itself.

APS likewise grounds biological significance in organisational evaluation. Living systems continually differentiate between conditions that support continued persistence and those that threaten it. This differentiation is not an external judgement but an endogenous feature of living organisation. Through biological evaluation, significance is generated because the organisation of the organism itself establishes what contributes to, or detracts from, its continued viability. Normativity therefore arises through the organisation of living systems rather than through externally defined optimisation objectives or informational quantities.

This distinction has important implications for the interpretation of biological function and purpose. Within APS, function is understood as the present-tense contribution that a process, structure, or activity makes to viability-oriented organisation. Historical explanations may account for how a function evolved, but they do not constitute its present biological role. Similarly, biological purpose does not require conscious intention, internal representation, or future goals acting as causes. Purpose refers instead to the viability-oriented organisation through which living systems continually sustain themselves.

These differing conceptions of normativity do not necessarily place APS and the Free Energy Principle in opposition. The Free Energy Principle provides a powerful formal language for modelling adaptive regulation, while APS investigates the organisational conditions that make adaptive regulation biologically meaningful. Formal models describe patterns of adaptive behaviour; organisational biology explains why those patterns possess biological significance. The two frameworks therefore illuminate different dimensions of the same living phenomenon.

Recognising this distinction helps clarify the relationship between mathematical description and biological explanation. Successful formal models can characterise the dynamics of living systems with remarkable precision. APS argues, however, that the biological significance of those dynamics ultimately depends upon the viability-oriented organisation of the systems themselves. Formal description and organisational explanation therefore answer different questions concerning the same biological reality.

## Explanatory Architecture and Theoretical Biology

The comparison developed in this article has implications extending beyond the relationship between the Agency–Process–Scale framework and the Free Energy Principle. It illustrates a broader methodological issue within contemporary theoretical biology: biological phenomena can often be investigated through different explanatory architectures that address distinct but complementary scientific questions.

The history of biology demonstrates the value of explanatory diversity. Evolutionary theory explains the historical transformation of populations, physiology investigates the mechanisms through which organisms function, developmental biology examines the reorganisation of organisms across their life histories, ecology studies the relationships between organisms and their environments, and molecular biology analyses the structures and interactions underlying cellular processes. These approaches are not mutually exclusive because each addresses different aspects of living systems.

Theoretical frameworks are no different. A formal mathematical framework and an organisational biological framework need not compete merely because both investigate adaptive living systems. Their relationship depends upon the explanatory questions they seek to answer. Confusion arises when frameworks developed to answer one kind of question are interpreted as though they simultaneously resolved every other explanatory question concerning life.

The comparison between APS and the Free Energy Principle illustrates this distinction. The Free Energy Principle provides a highly general formal language for modelling adaptive dynamics under conditions of uncertainty. APS investigates the biological organisation through which living systems become viable, normatively structured, and capable of organised persistence. These are not competing descriptions of the same explanatory task. Rather, they represent different explanatory architectures directed towards different dimensions of the same biological phenomenon.

Recognising this distinction also clarifies the relationship between constitutive explanation and formal description. Constitutive explanations seek to identify the organisation that makes a phenomenon what it is. Formal descriptions seek to characterise patterns, regularities, or dynamics exhibited by that phenomenon. Both forms of explanation are scientifically valuable, but they should not be conflated. A successful formal description does not, by itself, establish the constitutive organisation of the system it describes, just as identifying constitutive organisation does not eliminate the need for rigorous formal modelling.

APS therefore proposes that one important task of theoretical biology is not simply to generate new explanations but to clarify the explanatory relationships among existing ones. Biological understanding advances not only through the accumulation of empirical discoveries or increasingly sophisticated mathematical models, but also through greater clarity concerning the questions different explanatory frameworks are intended to answer. Explanatory coherence depends as much upon distinguishing complementary explanatory roles as upon refining individual theories.

This perspective helps explain why APS is presented as a comparative explanatory methodology rather than as a replacement for existing biological theories. Its objective is to investigate how diverse explanatory traditions—including formal, mechanistic, historical, ecological, developmental, and organisational approaches—relate through the organisation required for living systems to maintain themselves. APS does not seek a universal explanatory language that subsumes every biological discipline. Instead, it seeks a coherent explanatory architecture within which different biological frameworks can be understood as contributing complementary perspectives on organised persistence.

Seen in this broader context, the comparison with the Free Energy Principle serves as a case study in explanatory architecture rather than as an isolated comparison between two theories. It demonstrates how theoretical biology may benefit from distinguishing the organisational foundations of living systems from the formal frameworks used to describe their adaptive behaviour. Clarifying this relationship contributes not only to understanding APS and the Free Energy Principle, but also to the broader task of developing a more coherent and integrated theoretical biology.

## Conclusion: Towards a More Complete Theoretical Biology

The comparison developed throughout this article suggests that the relationship between the Agency–Process–Scale framework and the Free Energy Principle is more constructive than is often assumed. Both frameworks seek to understand how living systems maintain themselves despite continual interaction with changing environments, yet they approach this shared biological phenomenon through different explanatory priorities. The Free Energy Principle develops a formal mathematical framework for modelling adaptive dynamics under conditions of uncertainty. APS investigates the biological organisation that makes adaptive dynamics possible by explaining how living systems continually maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

Recognising this distinction clarifies why the two frameworks often appear simultaneously similar and different. They are similar because both reject passive conceptions of life and emphasise active self-maintaining organisation. They differ because they ask different explanatory questions. The Free Energy Principle asks how adaptive organisation may be formally described within a unified mathematical framework. APS asks what kind of biological organisation constitutes living systems and grounds concepts such as agency, function, normativity, development, evolution, and cognition.

This distinction also clarifies the relationship between formal description and constitutive biological explanation. Mathematical models are indispensable for understanding the behaviour of living systems, but successful formal description does not by itself determine what kind of biological organisation is being described. Conversely, identifying the organisational conditions that constitute life does not diminish the importance of rigorous mathematical modelling. Rather, it specifies the biological reality to which such models apply. Organisational explanation and formal modelling therefore address complementary dimensions of biological inquiry.

APS consequently proposes that theoretical biology benefits from distinguishing clearly between the organisation of living systems and the formal frameworks used to model their behaviour. Maintaining this distinction allows organisational concepts such as agency, normativity, and biological significance to remain grounded in viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation, while recognising the substantial contribution of formal approaches such as the Free Energy Principle to the analysis of adaptive dynamics. The resulting relationship is neither one of reduction nor replacement, but of complementary explanatory contribution.

More broadly, this comparison illustrates a methodological principle extending beyond these two frameworks alone. Progress in theoretical biology need not depend upon identifying a single explanatory language capable of subsuming every aspect of living systems. It may instead depend upon understanding how different explanatory frameworks illuminate different dimensions of biological organisation. Formal, mechanistic, historical, ecological, and organisational approaches need not compete for exclusive explanatory authority if their respective contributions are clearly distinguished.

From this perspective, APS is best understood not as an alternative to the Free Energy Principle but as a comparative explanatory methodology that investigates the organisational foundations upon which biological explanation depends. By clarifying the biological organisation constitutive of living systems while recognising the complementary value of formal modelling, APS seeks to contribute to a more coherent and integrated theoretical biology in which different explanatory traditions are understood in relation to the common phenomenon they investigate: the organised persistence of life.
