**Where This Article Fits**

*This History of Ideas article follows a change in what a biological grouping could tell us. [How Did Evolution Transform Biological Explanation?](/articles/how-did-evolution-transform-biological-explanation/) gives the wider history. [Evolution, Taxonomy, and the Reorganisation of Biological Explanation](/articles/evolution-taxonomy-and-the-reorganisation-of-biological-explanation/) takes up the contemporary question of how taxonomic and evolutionary accounts bear on one another.*

## Two questions about a specimen

Imagine bringing a flowering specimen to a herbarium. A key asks you to look closely at the flower and count its stamens. The answer narrows the choices. A few steps later you have a name to check against a description and other specimens. Now ask a second question. Which other plants share the most recent common ancestor with this one? The stamen count may still be useful, but its relevance depends on how that feature arose and changed in the plants being compared.

The difference between those questions helps explain a major change in biological classification. Naturalists already distinguished convenient arrangements from systems intended to capture natural affinities. Evolution gave those affinities a historical interpretation. To place plants together could now be to make a claim about their ancestry. Identification, naming, and classification remained interdependent parts of botanical work, each with its own standards of evidence.

## What Linnaeus’s sexual system made practical

Linnaeus’s sexual system organised plants by features of their reproductive structures. The number and arrangement of stamens helped determine the class, and features of the pistils often helped determine the order within it. A botanist could examine a flower, apply the rules, and narrow the search. Shared descriptions and names made it possible for others to check which plant was meant. This is one reason the Linnaean achievement mattered beyond the convenience of giving species short names. ([Uppsala University](https://www.uu.se/en/linnaeus-garden/our-plants-and-attractions/our-plants/sexual-system) and [PlantsPeoplePlanet, Plant Industry](https://plantspeopleplanet.au/plant-industry/))

A system built around readily observed characters can be effective for finding a name. Its groups can nevertheless bring together plants that differ in many other respects, or separate plants that share a broader pattern of structure. Linnaeus recognised the practical character of the sexual system and also worked on natural groups. Both endeavours belonged to the same botanical practice. They placed different demands on the characters used. ([Lidén 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/tax.13381))

## Natural affinities before Darwin

Antoine Laurent de Jussieu’s *Genera plantarum*, published in 1789, arranged genera in natural orders. Its significance here lies in the kind of work the arrangement was meant to do. A botanist comparing descriptions across genera could consider how several features belonged together and which combinations made a group recognisable. The arrangement and the descriptions thus informed one another. It was possible to ask whether a newly examined genus fit a natural group, and to reconsider the group when that fit was uncertain. ([Jussieu 1789](https://doi.org/10.5962/bhl.title.284) and [Lidén 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/tax.13381))

The pursuit of natural affinities therefore preceded Darwin. Naturalists could argue about how to delimit a group and which features best expressed its affinities. The further question was why those affinities existed. Evolution offered an answer that gave the observed pattern a history.

## Darwin gives affinity a history

In chapter XIII of *On the Origin of Species*, Darwin argued that the affinities recognised by naturalists could reflect descent with modification. If descendants inherit features and diverge, groups within groups become intelligible as the products of branching histories. The question about a resemblance changes accordingly. Did the organisms acquire the feature from a common ancestor, or could similar conditions have favoured its independent appearance? ([Darwin 1859, chapter XIII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species_%281859%29/Chapter_XIII))

Suppose two plants have similar structures that help them cope with dry conditions. The resemblance could help someone recognise a habit of growth or identify a specimen in a local flora. To use it as evidence of close relationship, a botanist needs grounds for thinking the resemblance reflects shared inheritance at the relevant point in the history. Broad agreement among characters, and a defensible account of how those characters arose, can matter more than a single conspicuous similarity.

Darwin supplied a historical interpretation of affinity. Later methods turned that interpretation into a more explicit comparison among possible branching trees. A feature inherited from a remote ancestor may be shared by many plants and tell us little about which of them are closest relatives. A shared feature that arose on a more recent branch can be more informative. Modern analyses compare patterns across many characters, including DNA sequences, to estimate where the branches join. The resulting tree proposes relationships. A classification makes a further choice about how to express those relationships in named groups.

## Where to place the relatives of palms

The flowering-plant family Dasypogonaceae illustrates what can happen when evidence for a relationship improves. Earlier studies with sparser sampling had recovered Dasypogonaceae as a possible sister group to the palm family, Arecaceae, with weak support. Sister groups are each other’s closest relatives on the branching tree being considered. Barrett and colleagues sampled plastid genomes from palms and Dasypogonaceae and compared them in a wider analysis of monocots. The analyses strengthened support for the sister relationship in the plastid data. ([Barrett et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.13617) and [APG IV 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/boj.12385))

The Angiosperm Phylogeny Group cited that evidence when it placed Dasypogonaceae in the order Arecales in its 2016 classification. The two families remained distinct. Placing them together in one order expressed a judgement about their relationship. The study supplied evidence for branches on a tree. The classification put that result to work in a system of named groups that botanists could use. ([APG IV 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/boj.12385))

A botanist identifying a specimen still needs characters that can be observed and checked. A revised account of ancestry changes which comparisons are most informative about the history of those characters. It can also prompt a new question about traits that once seemed to support a different grouping.

## Conclusion

Return to the specimen. The key helps you find its name. The classification places it among other plants. An evolutionary account asks what evidence warrants that placement as a claim about shared ancestry. These tasks work together, and the history explains why their criteria can diverge. A character useful for identification can carry little weight in a phylogenetic analysis. Evidence that changes a phylogeny can alter the boundaries of a named group while leaving the practical character useful.

Evolution changed what a biological classification could claim. A grouping could express a hypothesis about how organisms came to be related, and new evidence could change the grouping. Classification retains its practical value because botanists still need ways to identify specimens, communicate names, and organise what is known about plants. Its historical value lies in making relationships open to investigation and revision.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How did evolution change what a biological grouping could claim and what counted as evidence for it?

### Architectural Role

This History of Ideas article traces a change in classificatory practice through historical botany and a modern revision in flowering-plant systematics.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

The account draws on the distinction between identifying a specimen, assigning a name, and proposing a grouping. Darwin’s interpretation adds the historical claim that descent with modification can account for natural affinities.

### Related Explanatory Developments

Phylogenetic studies estimate evolutionary relationships. Classifications can express those estimates through revised groupings. The APG IV case illustrates how these two activities inform one another.

### Related Explanatory Questions

When does a diagnostic character provide evidence of ancestry? How should a classification express relationships whose support is uneven? Which classificatory criteria best serve identification or other practical tasks?

### Position Within APS

The distinction between a grouping and the evidence that warrants it is relevant to APS discussions of lineage continuity and biological classification. The episodes are historical examples of how the purpose of an inquiry changes what it requires of a classification.

### Scope and Evidential Limits

The article follows selected episodes in botanical classification. The APG IV example concerns a particular published revision and the evidence its authors cited. Wider questions about cognitive categories and classifications of plants by use belong to companion inquiries.
