**Where This Article Fits**

*This History of Ideas article follows a change in what a biological grouping could tell us. [How Did Evolution Transform Biological Explanation?](/articles/how-did-evolution-transform-biological-explanation/) gives the wider history. [Evolution, Taxonomy, and the Reorganisation of Biological Explanation](/articles/evolution-taxonomy-and-the-reorganisation-of-biological-explanation/) takes up the contemporary question of how taxonomic and evolutionary accounts bear on one another.*

## Two questions about a specimen

Imagine bringing a flowering specimen to a herbarium. An identification key asks you to count its stamens. The answer narrows the choices. A few steps later you have a name to check against a description and other specimens. Now ask a second question. Which plants are its closest evolutionary relatives? The stamen count may still be useful, but its value as evidence depends on when that feature arose and how it changed in the lineages being compared.

The two questions place different demands on the same observation. The first asks whether the feature helps us tell the specimen apart from others we might mistake it for. The second asks whether the feature helps us distinguish one possible history from another. The history of classification is partly the history of learning to make that distinction.

## Plant kinds before botanical systems

Long before Linnaeus, people recognised and named plant kinds, shared knowledge about them, and grouped them for different purposes. To recognise a plant can be to recall where it grows, when to gather it, or what to expect when it is handled. A category such as plants used for weaving answers a different question. It can bring together familiar plants that are otherwise distinguished by name, appearance, or habitat.

Modern ethnobotanical studies show how much these practices can involve. Harold Conklin’s study of Hanunóo plant knowledge in the Philippines and Brent Berlin, Dennis Breedlove, and Peter Raven’s study of Tzeltal plant classification in Chiapas document detailed systems of recognition and naming. Comparative work drew attention to recurring patterns, including named groups within more inclusive groups. Berlin also argued that perceived affinities could guide grouping independently of a plant’s use. These twentieth-century studies document particular knowledge traditions and help explain what local classification can accomplish. They give us no direct account of how pre-Linnaean European naturalists classified plants. ([Conklin 1954](https://search.worldcat.org/title/The-relation-of-Hanunoo-culture-to-the-plant-world/oclc/222231662), [Berlin, Breedlove, and Raven 1973](https://doi.org/10.1525/aa.1973.75.1.02a00140), [Berlin, Breedlove, and Raven 1974](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/book/9780127850474), and [Berlin 1992](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt7ztq5q))

Printed botany made it possible to compare the same characters across accounts of plants from different places. Andrea Cesalpino gave fruit and seed characters a prominent place in grouping plants. John Ray later argued for judging affinities through agreement among several important parts. Their different choices bring a problem into view that would survive both systems. What makes a feature worth giving weight when we group plants? ([Cesalpino 1583](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/60929) and [Rouhan and Gaudeul 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-0716-0997-2_1))

## Finding a plant and finding its affinities

Linnaeus’s sexual system gave botanists a practical route to identifying specimens. The number and arrangement of stamens helped determine a plant’s class, and features of the pistils often helped determine its order within that system. A botanist could examine a flower, apply the rules, and narrow the search. Shared descriptions and names then helped others check which plant was meant. ([Uppsala University](https://www.uu.se/en/linnaeus-garden/our-plants-and-attractions/our-plants/sexual-system) and [PlantsPeoplePlanet, Plant Industry](https://plantspeopleplanet.au/plant-industry/))

A readily observed feature can be excellent for finding a name. A system based on such features can bring together plants that differ in many other respects or separate plants that share a broader pattern of structure. Linnaeus recognised the practical character of the sexual system and also worked on natural groups. The two endeavours placed different demands on the characters used. ([Lidén 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/tax.13381))

In *Genera plantarum* (1789), Antoine Laurent de Jussieu arranged genera in natural orders. The descriptions made it possible to compare combinations of characters across genera and consider what made a group recognisable. An arrangement of this kind asked the botanist to look beyond the feature that would get a specimen through a particular step in identification. The search for natural affinities was already under way when Darwin asked why such affinities existed. ([Jussieu 1789](https://doi.org/10.5962/bhl.title.284), [Stevens 1994](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-development-of-biological-systematics/9780231064408/), and [Lidén 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/tax.13381))

## Darwin gives affinity a history

In chapter XIII of *On the Origin of Species*, Darwin argued that natural affinities could reflect descent with modification. If descendants inherit features and diverge, groups within groups become intelligible as products of branching histories. A resemblance now invited a historical question. Did the organisms inherit it from a common ancestor, or could similar conditions have favoured its independent appearance? Two plants may both have fleshy stems suited to dry conditions. That resemblance describes something important about how they live. Its value as evidence of close relationship depends on its history. ([Darwin 1859, chapter XIII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species_%281859%29/Chapter_XIII))

Darwin also allowed classification to express differences in the extent of modification. This left a question for later systematists. What should a classification do when the order of branching and the degree of resemblance suggest different groupings? A lineage can change substantially after a split. It may still share a more recent ancestor with its less similar sister lineage than with a third lineage that happens to look more like it. ([Darwin 1859, chapter XIII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species_%281859%29/Chapter_XIII))

## Cladistics and the evidence of branching

Twentieth-century systematists made the alternatives explicit. Numerical taxonomy showed how overall similarity could be calculated from many recorded characters. Evolutionary taxonomy retained a role for both ancestry and the extent of divergence. Willi Hennig’s phylogenetic systematics, often called *cladistics*, gave priority to branching order. For that purpose, characters matter according to what they tell us about where the lineages join. ([Sokal 1963](https://doi.org/10.2307/1217562), [Mayr 1969](https://www.si.edu/object/principles-systematic-zoology%3Asiris_sil_22539), and [Hennig 1966](https://www.press.uillinois.edu/books/?id=p068140))

Imagine comparing three lineages of flowering plants. All have pollen. That fact tells us little about which two are most closely related, because pollen was already present before these three diverged. Suppose two of the lineages share a distinctive structure, and comparison with other plants supports the inference that it arose in their immediate common ancestor. That shared derived feature supports grouping the two together. Hennig’s approach gives such evidence a specific role. A shared derived character that supports a group is called a *synapomorphy*. Comparing several characters helps test the grouping because similar features can arise independently, and inherited features can be lost. ([Hennig 1966](https://www.press.uillinois.edu/books/?id=p068140))

Now picture a rooted tree with three tips. Two tips meet at a fork, and the branch leading back from that fork joins the third lineage further down. The two that meet at the first fork are **sister groups**. Each is more closely related to the other than to the third because they share a more recent common ancestor. Either branch can contain one species or many. Tip order on the page can change when the fork is rotated. The relationship depends on which branches meet. A *clade* comprises an ancestor and all its descendants. ([Hennig 1966](https://www.press.uillinois.edu/books/?id=p068140))

DNA sequences expanded the evidence available for comparing possible trees. In botany, Chase and colleagues used sequences from the plastid gene *rbcL* to investigate relationships across seed plants. Statistical methods made it possible to compare trees under models of sequence change and to ask how often a grouping reappears when characters are resampled. These methods assess relationships. A classification uses the resulting evidence to decide how to represent them in named groups. ([Chase et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.2307/2399846), [Felsenstein 1981](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01734359), and [Felsenstein 1985](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1558-5646.1985.tb00420.x))

## Where to place the relatives of palms

In 2009, the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group left the family Dasypogonaceae without an ordinal placement. Some studies had suggested that it was sister to the palm family, Arecaceae, but support was weak. With wider sampling of plastid genomes, Barrett and colleagues found stronger support for that relationship. APG IV cited the study when it placed both families in the order Arecales in 2016. The classification retained the two families and changed how their relationship was represented. A botanist identifying a specimen could still use diagnostic features, while a botanist investigating ancestry now had a better-supported branching hypothesis to assess. ([APG III 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1095-8339.2009.00996.x), [Barrett et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.13617), and [APG IV 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/boj.12385))

## Recognising fungi and finding their place

The distinction also matters beyond flowering plants. A mushroom may grow in place beside a plant, but a comparison of how each obtains nutrients brings a different set of similarities and differences into view. In his 1969 five-kingdom classification, Robert Whittaker recognised fungi as a separate kingdom and gave their absorptive mode of nutrition an important place in the classification. That grouping brought a major difference in how organisms live into focus. ([Whittaker 1969](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.163.3863.150))

Where do fungi belong on the evolutionary tree? In 1993, Peter Wainright and colleagues compared ribosomal RNA sequences across major lineages. Their analysis supported a closer evolutionary relationship between fungi and animals than either has with plants. The two episodes pose different questions. Nutritional organisation helped distinguish fungi as a group. The sequence comparison supplied evidence about the branch to which that group belongs. ([Wainright et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.8469985))

## Conclusion

Return to the specimen in the herbarium. The key helps you find its name by directing attention to features you can check. The classification places the named plant among other organisms. To understand that placement as an evolutionary claim, you need evidence that distinguishes the proposed relationship from other possibilities.

The palm-family case shows how stronger evidence can change where a group is placed. The fungi case shows why the features that help us recognise a group can differ from the evidence that tells us where it belongs. Evolution gave classification this historical reach. A grouping could express a hypothesis about shared ancestry and be revised as evidence changed. The practical work of identifying and naming organisms continues alongside that inquiry, and its criteria make sense in light of what someone needs the classification to do.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How did evolution change what a biological grouping could claim and what counted as evidence for it?

### Architectural Role

This History of Ideas article traces changes in what naturalists and systematists have asked classifications to do, from recognising kinds and describing affinities to representing hypotheses about branching relationships.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

The argument distinguishes identifying a specimen, attaching a name, and grouping organisms. Darwin’s account of descent with modification supplies a historical interpretation of natural affinity. Cladistics makes explicit what kinds of shared characters can support a particular branching relationship.

### Related Explanatory Developments

Numerical and molecular methods made comparisons among characters and possible trees more explicit. The flowering-plant and fungi examples show how different kinds of evidence have informed the recognition and placement of groups.

### Related Explanatory Questions

When does a feature useful for identification provide evidence of ancestry? How should a classification represent relationships whose support is uneven? What makes a grouping useful for a practical decision?

### Position Within APS

The distinction between a grouping and the evidence that warrants it bears on APS discussions of lineage continuity and biological classification. The historical episodes show why the question being asked determines what an account needs to establish.

### Scope and Evidential Limits

The article follows selected episodes in plant classification and uses fungi to test the reach of its central distinction. The ethnobotanical studies concern particular communities, and the molecular examples concern the relationships supported by the studies cited. The philosophy of cognitive categories and the classification of plants by use are separate questions for companion articles.
