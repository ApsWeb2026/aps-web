**Where This Article Fits**

*[Aristotle and the Foundations of Biology](/articles/aristotle-and-the-foundations-of-biology/) and [Theophrastus and the Foundations of Plant Science](/articles/theophrastus-and-the-foundations-of-plant-science/) trace earlier sustained inquiries into animals and plants. This article asks how inquiries with different objects, methods, and institutions came to be understood as parts of a general science of living beings. It sets the scene for [How Did the Cell Become the Unit of Life?](/articles/how-did-the-cell-become-the-unit-of-life/) and [How Did Evolution Transform Biological Explanation?](/articles/how-did-evolution-transform-biological-explanation/), which examine later changes in what that science could explain.*

## Introduction

A botanist trying to identify a plant, an anatomist comparing the limbs of different animals, and a physician investigating what happens when breathing stops could all be studying living beings. Their questions called for different kinds of evidence. The botanist needed characters that others could recognise. The anatomist needed to determine which parts were comparable and how they were arranged. The physician needed evidence about how a change in one activity affected another. Collections, publications, and correspondence helped connect these inquiries. A specimen collected and described by a naturalist could later become evidence in an anatomical comparison.

There are good grounds for tracing some of these practices to antiquity. Aristotle compared the parts and activities of animals and asked how particular structures contributed to what an animal did. Theophrastus systematically compared plants, including their forms and ways of growing. Description, comparison, and questions about function therefore have a long history. If the question is when such inquiries became systematic, antiquity supplies part of the answer. If the question is when writers explicitly proposed a general science of living beings, around 1800 is a more informative date. The formation of a discipline with its own teaching and research institutions is a third development, which took longer. ([Aristotle and the Foundations of Biology](/articles/aristotle-and-the-foundations-of-biology/) · [Theophrastus and the Foundations of Plant Science](/articles/theophrastus-and-the-foundations-of-plant-science/))

The change around 1800 concerned the scope of the questions. Natural history, comparative anatomy, and physiology already supported claims that extended across species and settings. A general science of life proposed that findings from such inquiries could bear on shared questions about how living beings are organised, how they develop, and how their activities are sustained. The practices that contributed to this programme retained their own ways of establishing what an observation or experiment could show.

## Natural history made living diversity comparable

A plant description can travel further than the observer who wrote it. Its value depends on whether someone elsewhere can use it to recognise the plant, compare it with another, or question the identification. Eighteenth-century natural history extended this capacity through specimens, illustrations, published descriptions, and systems of naming. In *Species Plantarum* (1753), Carl Linnaeus used short names alongside diagnostic descriptions. A shared name helped investigators refer to the same described entity while they assessed the description or disputed the boundaries of the species. ([Linnaeus 1753](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/669))

Classification was one of several interests within natural history. In the *Histoire naturelle*, begun in 1749, Buffon considered animals in relation to reproduction, variation, and geographical distribution. Such questions drew on observations from different places and, in the case of reproduction, across generations. This work mattered to causal inquiry. Before asking why a difference occurred, a naturalist needed grounds for deciding whether it recurred and under what circumstances it appeared. ([Buffon 1749 onwards](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb301741843))

Natural history encompassed minerals as well as living beings. Its methods also made observations available for purposes beyond those for which they were originally collected. Identifying and describing a specimen gave later investigators grounds for deciding what they were comparing when they asked how its parts worked together.

## Comparative anatomy related parts to what animals could do

A specimen can preserve a tooth, a joint, or a limb after the activities in which it participated have ceased. Comparative anatomists asked what the form and arrangement of such parts could reveal about those activities. Georges Cuvier’s *Leçons d’anatomie comparée* illustrates how the material assembled through natural history could serve an anatomical inquiry. A jaw and its teeth could be considered together with other structures involved in obtaining and processing food. Comparing such arrangements across animals gave investigators grounds for asking which features occurred together and what activities their combination made possible. ([Cuvier 1800–1805](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/qg5sbwqa))

The same feature could serve two purposes. A tooth might help identify an animal by its shape. To explain how the animal fed, an investigator could consider how that shape worked with the movement of the jaw and the action of its muscles. The explanatory work lay in showing how the parts contributed together to an activity.

Anatomical comparison also raised a question that preserved specimens could leave open. If two structures appeared to work together, what happened to the activity of one when the other was disturbed? Answering that question called for evidence about processes unfolding in time.

## Experimental physiology made dependencies among activities testable

In *Recherches physiologiques sur la vie et la mort* (1800), Xavier Bichat investigated the course of death by examining how the activities of the heart, lungs, and brain depended on one another. The order of failure was part of what needed explaining. An activity might cease because another had already failed, and the sequence could vary with the initial disturbance. Comparing what happened after different interruptions gave investigators a way to test which dependencies accounted for the observed course. ([Bichat 1800](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/ekds5gfs) · [Bichat 1809, English translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Physiological_Researches_upon_Life_and_Death))

Bichat’s experiments addressed particular relations among functions. His broader account of what distinguishes living activity from physical processes involved further theoretical commitments. The experiments show how a dependency could be investigated through the order of observable changes. Comparative anatomy could establish how parts were arranged, and physiological intervention could test whether and under what conditions one activity depended on another.

## When did biology become a science in its own right?

One way to see what a general question about living organisation could involve is to consider Immanuel Kant’s account of organisms as *natural purposes* in the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790, §§64–65). Kant asked how to understand a being whose parts contribute to the whole and whose parts, in turn, arise and are sustained through the organisation of that whole. The question ranged across kinds of living beings. It concerned how to understand their organisation as a whole. Bichat’s experiments addressed a specific causal question within that broader field of inquiry by investigating how particular functions depended on one another. ([Kant’s Philosophy of Science, §5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-science/#Biol))

In 1802, Jean-Baptiste Lamarck used *biologie* in *Hydrogéologie* for the study of living bodies. In the same year, Gottfried Reinhold Treviranus published the first volume of *Biologie oder Philosophie der lebenden Natur*. The title made the scope of his project explicit. Its subtitle addressed both naturalists and physicians, communities whose investigations began from different problems and could contribute to a general inquiry into living beings. ([Lamarck 1802](https://redi.imss.fi.it/lamarck/ouvrages/docpdf/Hydrogeologie.pdf) · [Treviranus 1802](https://digisam.ub.uni-giessen.de/ubg-ihd-thaer/content/titleinfo/5319610) · [Barsanti 1997](https://www.persee.fr/doc/acths_0998-5662_1997_act_119_1_4121))

The fact that Treviranus addressed both groups helps explain how a programme could precede a common institutional home. A naturalist working with a collection and a physician teaching anatomy could find common questions about living organisation while continuing to work in institutions with different purposes. The date 1802 marks the explicit articulation of that ambition in these publications. How it took hold in teaching and research is a longer history.

The programme also left open what should count as a unit of biological explanation. Organisms, organs, tissues, and species were central to different inquiries. Cell theory and evolutionary explanation would later change how biologists related these objects of study to one another.

## Conclusion

The beginnings of systematic inquiry into living beings, the articulation of a general science of life, and the formation of biology as a discipline have different histories. Around 1800 matters because writers explicitly proposed that inquiries into living diversity, form, and activity could contribute to a common science. The standing of that proposal depended on the work of showing how findings obtained by different means bore on shared questions.

For APS, the consequence is methodological. A description can establish what varies, an anatomical comparison can show how parts contribute to an activity, and an intervention can test what the activity depends on. An explanation that draws on all three must show how the evidence for each supports the connection it claims.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How did inquiries into living diversity, form, and function come to be understood as parts of a general science of living beings?

### Architectural Role

Distinguishes the history of systematic inquiry into living beings from the emergence of a general programme and the later formation of a discipline. Provides context for subsequent changes in biological explanation.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

Earlier investigations of animals and plants, the descriptive and classificatory practices of natural history, and anatomical and medical inquiry supplied questions, materials, and methods that could be brought into relation.

### Related or Subsequent Explanatory Developments

Cell theory changed what could be treated as a unit of living organisation. Evolutionary theory made descent and historical change explanatory. Ecology investigated relations between organisms and environments. Research on physiological regulation asked how living activities contribute to internal stability.

### Related Explanatory Questions

What makes observations comparable across places? When does a relation among parts explain a function? How can an intervention establish a dependency between activities? What warrants extending an explanation from a particular organism to living beings more generally?

### Explanatory Boundaries

The article uses selected European publications and practices to explain the emergence of a general programme around 1800. The wider history of knowledge about living beings and the subsequent institutional development of biology require accounts of their own.

### Position Within APS

Places APS’s concern with biological organisation in a history of inquiries that connect evidence about diversity, structure, and activity.
