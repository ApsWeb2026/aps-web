**Where This Article Fits**

*This evolving extension develops a specific question raised in [APS and the Social Sciences](/articles/aps-and-the-social-sciences/). It examines how economic arrangements shape access to food and other material conditions of life, using the 1974 Bangladesh famine as a worked case. [APS and Institutions](/articles/aps-and-institutions/) provides the wider context for how rules and practices help sustain coordinated activity.*

## Introduction

People depend on continuing access to food, water, shelter, and care. How they secure these conditions depends in part on economic arrangements, including how they earn an income, what they can obtain through exchange, and what they can claim from public institutions or draw on through household and community relationships. The term *provisioning* refers here to the activities and arrangements through which people secure these conditions. This article asks how provisioning is sustained or disrupted, and whose access is protected when circumstances change.

Economists and other social scientists already investigate these problems. Ostrom’s (2010) work on the governance of shared resources, for example, shows why the rules and practices of collective organisation deserve attention alongside markets and state administration. APS brings a particular question to this existing inquiry. When an arrangement persists through change, what does its persistence mean for the people who rely on it? The answer requires distinguishing the continuity of institutions and markets from the continuity of livelihoods and from the biological viability of the people involved.

## The Persistence of Arrangements and the Viability of People

People have material requirements for continued functioning. A livelihood is one way of securing those requirements over time, and an institution or market may help sustain that livelihood. Each can have a different trajectory. A market may continue to operate while some households lose the income needed to buy food. An institution may preserve the rules under which it operates while the protection those rules provide reaches some groups more readily than others. The persistence of an arrangement, its contribution to people’s lives, and the fairness of its effects therefore require separate evidence.

Economic coordination depends on resources and on arrangements that govern access to them. Food stocks and transport affect what can be supplied, while wages, prices, ownership, eligibility rules, and public distribution affect who can obtain it. Rules and practices can persist through repeated use, enforcement, and adaptation. A stronger claim of constraint closure would require showing which constraints are reciprocally maintained through the activities they help organise. Continued coordination by itself establishes little about that reciprocal dependence.

The language of agency also needs care. Households, traders, employers, and public bodies have different capacities to act, and their actions can have different consequences for other people’s livelihoods. An account of economic action should specify who can act, with what resources and authority, and with what consequences. Here the biological link is the material dependence of people on what provisioning supplies. Any attribution of biological agency to an economy as a whole would require a separate argument.

## Food Access in the 1974 Bangladesh Famine

The 1974 Bangladesh famine provides a case in which aggregate food availability and people’s ability to obtain food must be examined together. Sen (1981) argued that measures of aggregate availability could miss a decisive change in what particular groups were able to obtain. In his analysis, agricultural labourers were especially vulnerable because their ability to acquire rice depended on employment, wages, and the price of food. Flooding disrupted work, and rising rice prices could further reduce what households could buy. Sen’s entitlement approach connects access to the resources people hold and the opportunities they have to convert those resources into food.

Other accounts extend the comparison. Ravallion (1987) examined the operation of rice markets and the role of expectations about future scarcity in price movements. Hossain (2026) brings political commitment, state capacity, and the international politics of relief into the analysis. Together these studies give the APS comparison a demanding starting point. They already connect household access to labour markets, price formation, public action, and conditions beyond the household.

## What Agency, Process, and Scale Bring into View

An **Agency** inquiry asks who could act and how their actions affected access to food. For a household reliant on agricultural wages, the scope for action depended on employment opportunities and purchasing power. Traders’ decisions about stocks and public decisions about procurement or relief affected the conditions in which households acted. Analysing those decisions together requires attention to the unequal means and authority of the actors involved.

A **Process** inquiry asks how provisioning continued, broke down, or was reorganised as conditions changed. It traces how interruptions to employment, changes in purchasing power, shifts in rice prices, and relief measures fitted together over the course of the crisis. A household’s loss of food access is then part of a trajectory whose explanation depends on the order and interaction of changes. The same public decision can enter an Agency analysis through who made it and whom it affected, and a Process analysis through its place in the course of disruption or recovery.

A **Scale** inquiry asks which spatial and temporal extents have to be followed to explain that trajectory. The relevant relationships may connect a household’s employment and local food prices with wider patterns of trade, stocks, procurement, or aid. Timing matters as well. An annual supply figure addresses a different question from how much food households can obtain during the weeks when employment or purchasing power contracts. The explanatory work lies in tracing the specific relations through which changes elsewhere or at other times affect access in the place and period under study.

## What the Comparison Establishes

For this case, the explanatory target is the loss of effective access to food among vulnerable wage-dependent households during the famine. Sen’s entitlement analysis already explains why that access can deteriorate independently of aggregate supply. Ravallion’s market analysis and Hossain’s political analysis extend the resources available for explaining the timing and distribution of that deterioration. A fair assessment of APS must give those accounts full credit for the dependencies they identify.

The contribution shown here is to organise questions about actors, changes in provisioning, and the spatial and temporal reach of the relevant dependencies around one stated target. Additional explanatory gain would require a result that survives comparison with the established accounts. For example, an APS-guided investigation might ask whether differences in the timing of relief relative to employment losses help explain why otherwise comparable households experienced different durations of food deprivation. To count as a contribution, that inquiry would need suitable evidence and would have to establish what the existing literature already explains about the timing and reach of relief.

## Conclusion

Economic arrangements help shape the conditions under which people can continue to live and work. Their persistence can support livelihoods, and it can also coexist with severe deprivation. The Bangladesh case gives APS a specific role in economic inquiry by asking whose access changed, which activities mattered, and how dependencies across places and periods shaped the outcome. Whether this way of organising inquiry yields an additional explanation remains a question for comparative research.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How do economic arrangements sustain access to the material conditions of life, and why can access fail for particular groups while parts of those arrangements continue to operate?

### Architectural Role

This evolving extension applies APS to a bounded problem of provisioning. It uses the Bangladesh famine to examine the relationship between the continuity of economic arrangements and people’s ability to sustain their livelihoods.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

The analysis draws on the APS distinctions among viability, organised persistence, and the three analytic projections. The case depends empirically on scholarship on entitlements, rice markets, and famine politics. Ostrom’s account of collective resource governance supplies a related institutional perspective.

### Subsequent or Related Explanatory Developments

The article connects with [APS and Institutions](/articles/aps-and-institutions/) and [APS and Norms](/articles/aps-and-norms/) through questions about how rules are enacted, maintained, and changed. A further empirical development would trace how the timing and reach of relief interact with the changing access of specific groups.

### Related Explanatory Questions

Which people can secure food when employment or prices change? Which rules shape eligibility for relief, and how do they operate in practice? What evidence would distinguish the effect of relief timing from other explanations of differences in food access?
