*## Where This Article Fits

This Synthesis article examines the search for a fundamental unit of life and asks why different biological investigations employ different explanatory units. It connects the historical prominence of cells, genes, organisms, species, lineages, and ecosystems with contemporary questions about biological individuality and organised persistence.

[What Is an Organism?](/articles/what-is-an-organism/) examines the evidence for organismal unity, while *Biological Individuality — Organised Persistence Through Time* considers how living systems are individuated and how they continue through change. [What Is a Gene in APS?](/articles/what-is-a-gene-in-aps/) and [What Is a Species in APS?](/articles/what-is-a-species-in-aps/) examine two other units whose significance depends on the inquiry in which they are used.

The article brings these questions together through the APS vocabulary of agency, process, and scale. It distinguishes the value of connecting established explanations from the stronger claim that APS provides a distinctive explanatory advance.

## Introduction: The Search for Life's Fundamental Unit

The success of atomic theory encouraged the idea that identifying basic constituents could transform a science. Biology has repeatedly benefited from comparable discoveries. Cell theory made it possible to investigate organisms of very different kinds through recurring structures and activities, while genetics made inherited differences traceable across generations. Yet the unit that makes inheritance intelligible may be different from the one that makes development or physiological regulation intelligible. What, then, would it mean for any of these units to be fundamental to life?

The difficulty is already apparent in an ordinary botanical example. Several shoots may belong to the same clone, exchange resources while connected, and later survive independently. A study of ancestry, a measurement of resource exchange, and a survey of independently surviving plants may therefore draw different boundaries around the same material system. The investigator's choice affects both what is counted and what can be inferred from the count.

This article begins with the explanatory achievements that brought particular units to prominence. It then considers how research on individuality helps distinguish a unit selected for investigation from a claim about what constitutes an organism, an evolutionary individual, or a living system. The APS account builds on these distinctions in asking how living organisation persists through change.

## Why Biology Searches for Fundamental Units

The appeal of a fundamental unit lies partly in explanatory economy. Recurring units make otherwise disparate phenomena comparable, support generalisation, and give researchers a basis for deciding what to measure or manipulate. Much of the power of cell theory, for example, came from making investigations of growth, reproduction, and pathology comparable across organisms that had previously been studied in quite different terms.

The word *fundamental*, however, can mark several kinds of importance. A researcher may be looking for the smallest structure ordinarily capable of living activity, the unit through which hereditary differences are transmitted, or the system whose integrated functioning needs to be explained. An evolutionary investigation may focus on the entities whose variation and reproduction contribute to change across generations. An experiment may select a unit because it is the appropriate domain for a particular measurement. The same entity can occupy several of these roles, but each role calls for its own justification.

The differences concern organisation as well as size. A cell may be an appropriate unit for investigating membrane transport, while the coordinated behaviour of an animal requires attention to interactions among many cells and to the animal's surroundings. A population may be the unit in which evolutionary change is estimated, even though the activities that enable its members to survive and reproduce occur in particular organisms. The relevant boundaries follow from the dependencies or forms of continuity that the investigation seeks to understand.

Mayr (1982) traced changes in biological thought through the study of diversity, inheritance, and evolution. More recent process-oriented accounts have questioned whether these inquiries ultimately need to converge on one fixed kind of entity (Nicholson and Dupré 2018; Dupré 2021). On this view, determining which unit matters is part of formulating the explanatory problem. The history of cell theory shows how productive such a choice can be and how its scope becomes clearer as the questions change.

## The Cell as the Fundamental Unit

Nineteenth-century investigations associated with Matthias Schleiden, Theodor Schwann, and Rudolf Virchow established the importance of cells in the structure, development, and reproduction of organisms. The familiar formulation of cell theory brings together three principles that emerged and were refined over time. Organisms are composed of cells, cells are basic units of biological organisation, and cells arise from pre-existing cells. These principles gave researchers a common basis for investigating plants, animals, fungi, and microorganisms, with consequences for physiology, development, pathology, and microbiology.

The cell's explanatory importance comes as much from what it does as from its place in biological structure. Cells maintain boundaries, transform energy and materials, regulate internal conditions, and respond to disturbances. In unicellular organisms, much of what needs explaining about the organism can therefore be investigated through the activities of a single cell. In multicellular organisms, the activities of differentiated cells depend on interactions with other cells, tissues, and extracellular structures. Explaining how a cell contributes to development or regulation may require following those interactions beyond its boundary.

Syncytial and coenocytic organisation brings a further distinction into view. Multiple nuclei can share a cytoplasm, and the boundaries relevant to nuclear activity, resource distribution, or development may differ from those suggested by the familiar image of a collection of separately bounded cells. The achievements of cell theory thus provide a basis for asking how cellular activities are coordinated and when the system that must be studied extends beyond a single cellular boundary.

## The Gene as the Fundamental Unit

Where cell theory supplied a common basis for investigating living structure and activity, genetics supplied ways to trace hereditary differences. Mendelian inheritance, chromosome theory, and the identification of DNA's molecular structure transformed explanations of how differences are transmitted and how inherited variation contributes to molecular function and evolutionary change.

The gene acquired several explanatory roles in the course of these developments. It can be treated as a unit of inheritance, as a molecular sequence associated with a biological product or function, or as a replicator in an evolutionary model. Population genetics can track the frequencies of inherited variants over successive generations, and gene-centred approaches use that continuity to explain aspects of evolutionary change. An account of how a variant contributes to a developmental outcome, however, needs to establish what happens when the sequence is expressed in a particular cellular and environmental context.

Transcription, translation, regulation, replication, and repair depend on organised biochemical conditions. The same sequence can contribute to different outcomes in different contexts, and similar phenotypic functions can arise through different molecular arrangements. These dependencies make the conditions of gene activity part of the causal explanation. They also lead to a question for organism-centred inquiry: how do developmental and physiological processes coordinate the activities to which genes contribute?

## The Organism as an Integrative Individual

Organisms occupy a distinctive place in biology because they offer familiar examples of activities whose consequences can be understood through their contribution to a continuing system. During development, cells differentiate and enter into changing relations with one another. The organism persists through transformations in its material constituents, structure, and activity. Understanding this continuity requires tracing how the products of one developmental process alter the conditions in which other processes occur.

Physiological regulation makes the same point in a different way. A response to disturbance may involve changes in metabolism, transport, growth, repair, and interaction with the surroundings. The relation among these activities helps explain how the organism maintains or restores conditions under which it can continue functioning. Accounts of organismality, biological autonomy, and processual organisation have long examined such dependencies.

Organismal unity nevertheless takes different forms. A bacterium, a tree, a lichen, a colonial animal, and an animal with a complex microbiome can require different kinds of evidence about boundaries, integration, and continuity. The explanatory importance of organismal coordination makes that evidence worth seeking. It also makes it important to distinguish the system selected for an investigation from the individual whose unity the investigation may seek to establish.

### Biological Units and Biological Individuals

Researchers identify biological units for purposes such as counting, measurement, intervention, and explanation. Attributing individuality makes a further claim about the unity or continuity of the entity under study. Sometimes researchers begin with recognised individuals and measure their properties. In other cases, they begin with a process and work out which units need to be distinguished to measure or explain it.

Waters (2018) directs attention to the role of research practices in determining which kinds of individuality matter. Yilmaz Silverman (2026) develops this issue through the botanical conception of the organism, distinguishing investigations that begin by counting individuals from those that begin by measuring processes and identifying the relevant units. In plants, as Yilmaz and Dupré (2025) show, attention to physiological organisation can bring a different unit into view from the one suggested by genetic identity or descent.

The choice has consequences for what the evidence can support. A unit selected to measure resource exchange may help establish the extent of physiological integration. Establishing that the corresponding system constitutes an organism or an evolutionary individual requires evidence appropriate to that attribution. APS can ask how viability-oriented organisation persists and changes wherever there is evidence of such organisation. The empirical work of establishing its extent remains part of the inquiry.

## The Problem of Biological Individuality

The question of what constitutes a biological individual can concern physiological integration, continuity through development, reproductive organisation, or participation in Darwinian processes. Clarke (2010, 2013) examines the conceptual and evolutionary difficulties of identifying individuals, while Godfrey-Smith (2009) investigates the conditions under which entities form Darwinian populations. The following cases show why those criteria matter in practice and why a visible boundary settles so little by itself.

### Plants and physiological individuality

A set of clonally related shoots may be counted as one genet, while its ramets can be distinguished for demographic, ecological, or physiological purposes. Connected shoots may exchange resources and later become physiologically independent. Their genetic continuity persists through this transition, even as the relations that supported their physiological integration change.

The choice between counting shoots, genets, or physiologically integrated systems affects estimates of population size, growth, competition, and reproduction. It also shapes the interpretation of measurements of resource allocation. Clarke (2012) examines the difficulties of plant individuation, and Yilmaz and Dupré (2025) draw attention to the explanatory importance of physiological organisation. The botanical case thereby makes concrete the methodological point developed by Yilmaz Silverman (2026): understanding what is being measured may be necessary to determine what should be counted.

### Symbiosis and organismal boundaries

Where clonal plants show how physiological integration can change among genetically related parts, symbioses show how integrated activity can arise among organisms with different histories. In lichens, intimate associations between fungal and photosynthetic partners, often accompanied by additional microorganisms, can support a coherent physiological and ecological system. The partners also retain biological identities that matter to explanations of how the association forms and continues.

Host-associated microbiomes raise a related question. Microorganisms can contribute to nutrition, immunity, and development, giving researchers good reasons to investigate the host and its microbial associates together. Bordenstein and Theis (2015) formulate principles concerning holobionts and hologenomes, while Zilber-Rosenberg and Rosenberg (2008) develop the hologenome theory of evolution. Establishing the physiological importance of an association addresses one part of the inquiry. Its evolutionary individuality also depends on how the association is inherited and maintained across generations.

### Individuality through evolutionary transformation

Endosymbiosis shows how relations between formerly independent organisms can become constitutive of a new form of biological organisation. The evolutionary histories of mitochondria and plastids involve the incorporation and transformation of microbial lineages. Margulis (1970) helped establish the importance of symbiosis in explaining the origins of eukaryotic cellular organisation.

These histories make individuality itself something that can change over evolutionary time. To explain such a transition, researchers must examine how integration, dependence, and inheritance changed together. The existence of a present-day association supplies a starting point for that inquiry. The evidence for the transition lies in the history of the relations that came to sustain the resulting organisation.

### Colonial and collective organisation

Colonial animals raise questions about unity within developmentally connected systems. In corals, bryozoans, and siphonophores, distinguishable modules or zooids can contribute to the functioning of a larger system. Their degree and kind of interdependence matter to whether one is explaining the development of a module, the functioning of the colony, or the continuity of a reproductive unit.

Some slime moulds add the possibility of a transition between solitary and aggregated forms, while the coordinated activities of eusocial insects have prompted comparisons with superorganisms. These cases involve different developmental and ecological processes. Their value for the study of individuality lies in the questions they make possible about the relations that hold a collective together, how long those relations persist, and what sort of unity the evidence supports.

## Beyond Organisms: Species, Lineages, and Ecosystems

Questions about individuality also arise at spatial and temporal extents that exceed the life of an organism. Evolutionary inquiry traces continuity through descent, while ecology investigates dependencies among organisms and their surroundings. In each case, the relations of interest help determine where to draw the boundaries of the explanatory unit.

### Species and evolutionary individuality

Ghiselin (1974) and Hull (1978) argued that species can be understood as historical individuals. On this account, the unity of a species depends on historical relations among its members, whereas a class can be defined by shared properties. The argument concerns evolutionary identity. It therefore calls for evidence of a different kind from that used to establish physiological integration in an organism.

Species concepts also give different weight to reproductive relations, genealogical continuity, ecological differentiation, and phylogenetic history. Which criterion is informative depends in part on whether the inquiry concerns, for example, the maintenance of reproductive boundaries or the reconstruction of evolutionary diversification.

### Lineages and historical continuity

Lineages make continuity through descent explicit. They connect biological entities across generations and provide the historical context for explaining inherited variation, diversification, and the emergence of new forms. An organism can persist through material turnover as its activities sustain its physiological organisation. A lineage persists through relations of descent among successive individuals. These kinds of continuity can enter the same explanation, as when organismal activities affect reproductive success and thereby influence the subsequent history of a lineage.

Godfrey-Smith (2009) and Okasha (2006) provide resources for specifying the units and processes involved in evolutionary change. Their work also clarifies why a claim about the unit of selection requires an account of the relevant variation, reproduction, and inheritance, whichever entities the inquiry brings into view.

### Ecosystems and ecological interdependence

Ecological inquiry can take a watershed, forest, soil system, or marine habitat as its domain. The appropriate boundary depends on the process under investigation. Measurements of nutrient cycling, energy transfer, or population change may call for different delineations of the same surroundings. Organisms also modify the conditions in which these processes occur, so an account of ecological interdependence can cross several organismal boundaries.

Ecosystems can exhibit feedback, persistence, and dynamics that resemble regulation. These properties provide reasons to investigate how particular ecological patterns are sustained. Attributing organismality, constraint closure, or biological agency in the APS sense requires additional evidence about the organisation and activities of the system to which the claim is applied.

## Borderline Cases and the Limits of Simple Units

A study of resource exchange in a clonal plant may take a group of connected shoots as its unit of measurement. If the aim is to explain how resources reach a developing shoot, the relevant boundary depends on which connections actually carry those resources. A demographic study may distinguish shoots that can survive independently, while a genetic study may group those same shoots as members of one genet. Evidence of resource exchange bears directly on physiological integration. Claims about demographic independence or genetic continuity require evidence of their own.

The example brings four decisions into relation. Researchers must determine what to measure, whether and in what sense to individuate the system, what phenomenon they seek to explain, and what evidence would justify the proposed boundary or attribution. Waters (2018) and Yilmaz Silverman (2026) help make clear why the order of these decisions can vary. A study may begin by comparing recognised individuals, or it may use measurements of a process to establish the boundaries of the system it needs to explain.

The same reasoning applies when nutritional dependence motivates a study of a host and its microbiome or when the coordination of zooids motivates a study of a siphonophore colony. Associations can be informative explanatory units while their status as organisms or evolutionary individuals remains open. Making the intended attribution explicit allows researchers to identify what their evidence establishes and which questions require further investigation.

## Why No Single Unit Fully Explains Life

The explanatory importance of a biological unit arises from what it allows investigators to account for. A genetic variant can be indispensable to an explanation of inheritance, and the organisation of a cell can be indispensable to explaining how that variant is expressed. Understanding the resulting phenotype may also require developmental history and environmental conditions. Each part of the explanation introduces dependencies that the investigator must establish at the spatial and temporal extents over which they operate.

These dependencies need not form a hierarchy in which every relevant unit fits neatly inside the next. A process such as nutrient exchange can connect parts of an organism with other organisms and their surroundings. A lineage, although historically continuous, is delimited through descent. Eronen (2013) questions the assumption that causal explanation requires fixed levels, while process-oriented accounts emphasise relations and transformations whose boundaries can change over time (Nicholson and Dupré 2018; Dupré 2021).

Explanatory plurality therefore carries an evidential obligation. A proposed unit is adequate insofar as its boundaries, properties, and relations allow the relevant phenomenon to be explained. Where several units are needed, the explanation must show how the processes associated with them are connected.

## The APS Perspective: Life as Agency, Process, and Scale

APS begins by distinguishing the organisation that qualifies a system as living from the units that can help explain biological phenomena. It defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. Viability concerns the system-relative conditions that support, undermine, or restore the continued operation of a living organisation. Constraint closure concerns the reciprocal dependence of constraints that contribute to maintaining that organisation. The APS definition requires that this closure be viability-oriented.

APS defines biological agency as **viability-oriented organisational activity**. It includes maintenance, regulation, repair, and activity in relation to the surroundings where evidence shows a contribution to viability. Internal activity can qualify on that basis, and the attribution does not require intention, cognition, or deliberation. In a particular case, it depends on identifying the living organisation and establishing how the activity contributes to its continued operation.

**Process** directs attention to the activities and transformations through which that organisation is maintained, altered, or lost. Living systems continually exchange materials and undergo changes in the relations among their constituents. Explaining their persistence therefore requires an account of how organisation continues through these transformations. **Scale** directs attention to the spatial and temporal extents over which the relevant activities and dependencies operate. The inquiry determines which extents matter and how they are related.

Together, these perspectives allow APS to ask how the units of an explanation relate to the organisation whose viability is at issue. A gene can be central to explaining inheritance, a lineage to explaining descent, and an ecosystem to explaining nutrient cycling. Each may contribute to an account of living organisation through a different relation. An attribution of life or agency to any particular system rests on the evidence for viability-oriented organisation or activity in that system.

## Organisms as Sites of Regulatory Integration

Organisms are particularly informative for APS because their responses to disturbance often allow relationships among maintenance, regulation, and viability to be studied together. A physiological disturbance can affect transport or metabolism, elicit changes in growth or repair, and alter how the organism interacts with its surroundings. Tracing those changes can show how activities contribute to restoring conditions on which continued functioning depends. The processes and their coordination vary among organisms, so the evidence must come from the particular system.

Such an inquiry can also disclose dependencies that extend beyond the organismal boundary. An organism may depend on microbial partners, ecological relationships, or conditions that its own activities help modify. In clonal plants, the extent of physiological integration can change as connections between shoots develop or are lost. These cases make boundaries an empirical question. Evidence of coordination can establish how certain activities are integrated, while claims about developmental or evolutionary individuality call for evidence about the corresponding form of continuity. Organismality therefore involves several dimensions of unity, and evidence of stronger physiological coordination settles only one part of the question.

The explanatory significance of organisms follows from the organisation they allow researchers to investigate. In APS terms, many are especially informative sites for studying viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Their importance in such inquiries is compatible with the use of populations, lineages, or ecosystems wherever the question concerns the processes and dependencies those units make accessible.

## The Comparative Status of the APS Account

Several established approaches already supply resources for the argument developed here. Process biology examines how the identities of living systems depend on activity and history (Nicholson and Dupré 2018; Dupré 2021). Explanatory pluralism gives reasons to use different forms of explanation for different questions. Research on biological individuality shows why physiological, evolutionary, and investigative criteria can identify different units (Clarke 2010, 2013; Waters 2018; Yilmaz Silverman 2026). Accounts of biological autonomy and organism-centred explanation also address the maintenance of organisation through change.

APS draws these issues together through a compact vocabulary of viability, activity, persistence, and explanatory extent. This may improve conceptual clarity by making it easier to distinguish the attribution of life or agency from the selection of an explanatory unit. A shared vocabulary may also help researchers working in different disciplines specify how their accounts relate. Where those connections make dependencies among processes easier to identify and assess, the vocabulary may contribute to explanatory integration. Clarity, communication, and integration are each worth assessing in their own right.

A claim of distinctive explanatory gain carries a further comparative burden. It requires identifying the strongest existing account of the same phenomenon, establishing what that account already explains, and showing that a remaining difference allows APS to explain a relation or discriminate between possibilities more effectively. The present article establishes no such difference. Its contribution is a conceptual synthesis and an account of how APS proposes to connect established lines of biological inquiry.

## Conclusion: The Fundamental Unit Question Reconsidered

Asking what is fundamental to life becomes more productive once the object of explanation is clear. A unit may be fundamental to the transmission of hereditary differences, the coordination of physiological activity, or the continuity of a lineage. Those are substantive claims about different phenomena. Each can be assessed by asking what the investigation measures, what kind of unity or continuity it attributes, and which processes justify the proposed boundaries.

For APS, the question of what is fundamental to *being alive* concerns the organisation through which a system sustains its viability. Organisms are especially informative where physiological and developmental activities can be shown to contribute to that organisation. Explaining how they do so can require evidence from cells, genes, environmental interactions, and histories of descent. The fundamental-unit question earns its place in biology because it makes these explanatory choices explicit and gives investigators grounds for judging whether the units they have chosen are adequate to the phenomenon.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

Does biology require a single fundamental unit of life, or do different biological questions legitimately require different explanatory units?

### Architectural Role

This article synthesises historical and contemporary debates concerning the explanatory roles of cells, genes, organisms, species, lineages, and ecosystems. It distinguishes the units selected for investigation from claims about biological individuality, organismhood, life, and agency.

### Preceding Dependencies

The argument depends on distinctions among biological organisation, biological individuality, organismality, and biological agency. It draws on process biology, explanatory pluralism, and research on evolutionary and physiological individuality.

### Related and Subsequent Developments

The APS articles on organisms, biological individuality, genes, species, development, and regulation investigate particular forms of biological unity and their explanatory roles. These inquiries provide opportunities to assess whether the APS vocabulary improves communication or explanatory integration and whether any such improvement exceeds what established approaches already provide.

### Related Questions

- When does a unit selected for experimental investigation also qualify as a biological individual?
- How do physiological, developmental, and evolutionary criteria of individuality differ?
- Under what conditions does an association of biological units constitute an integrated organism?
- How should the boundaries of a living system be established when its components and environmental dependencies change?
- What distinguishes useful conceptual integration from a demonstrable explanatory advance?

### Position Within APS

Agency concerns viability-oriented organisational activity. Process concerns the activities and transformations through which biological organisation is maintained, altered, or lost. Scale concerns the spatial and temporal extents relevant to those activities and dependencies. These are complementary perspectives whose application depends on the biological question and the available evidence.

### Corpus Significance

This article connects APS terminology with established debates about organismhood, individuality, explanatory plurality, and biological continuity. It clarifies the distinction between identifying an explanatory unit and attributing life or agency. Its contribution is conceptual synthesis, with claims of distinctive explanatory advance open to comparison with the strongest existing alternatives.
