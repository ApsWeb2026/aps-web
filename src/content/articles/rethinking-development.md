> **Where this article fits:** This article clarifies what APS means by development. It moves beyond blueprint and programme models without replacing them with a universal continuity-maintenance account. Development concerns the generation and transformation of living organisation through time. Developmental processes may contribute to viable continuity and may interact with physiological, ecological, regenerative and evolutionary processes, but those relations must be established for the biological target concerned.

Development is one of the central explanatory problems of biology.

Living systems are generated and transformed through time. Cells divide and differentiate. Structures emerge and are remodelled. Physiological organisation changes. Behavioural capacities can develop. Organisms interact with changing environments, undergo repair or regeneration in some circumstances, mature and age.

The explanatory problem is not adequately captured by asking how a predetermined adult form is produced from an initial state.

APS instead asks:

> How is living organisation generated and transformed through time?

This formulation shifts attention from the execution of an antecedent plan to the materially realised processes, constraints, regulatory relations and histories through which developmental change occurs.

It does not assume that all developmental change preserves viability or that persistence is what makes a process developmental. Development and organised persistence are related explanatory problems, but they are not identical.

## Beyond Blueprint and Programme Models

Development has often been described using blueprint, instruction, code and programme metaphors.

Such language has helped organise important research into genes, inheritance, signalling and regulation. It can nevertheless become misleading when it suggests that developmental outcomes are already specified in an internal informational description that biological processes merely execute.

Genes are indispensable to many developmental explanations, but genetic sequence alone does not independently specify the complete material organisation through which development occurs. Gene expression takes place in cells with existing structures, regulatory relations and histories. Cellular activities occur within tissues and physiological systems, while some developmental outcomes also depend materially on environmental conditions or interactions with other organisms.

Developmental explanation therefore requires attention to the relations actually responsible for the target phenomenon.

For some explananda, genetic and cellular mechanisms may provide most of the required explanation. Others may require tissue interactions, physiological regulation, developmental history, organismal activity, or environmental relations. No fixed list of explanatory components should be imposed in advance.

Moving beyond blueprint models consequently does not mean replacing genetic explanation with an equally universal systems description. It means determining which materially realised processes and relations generate the developmental outcome concerned.

## Development as Organised Transformation

Development involves transformations in living organisation.

Growth, differentiation, morphogenesis, physiological reorganisation, behavioural development and other developmental processes can alter structures, functions and relations through time. Some changes are gradual; others reorganise substantial features of the developing system.

These transformations are organised in the minimal sense that developmental outcomes depend upon relations among biological processes rather than upon unconstrained material change. Regulation, structural relations, physical conditions, developmental history and existing organisation can enable some trajectories while limiting others.

This does not mean that development is defined by successful continuity.

Developmental change may contribute to the maintenance of viable organisation, but developmental processes can also produce dysfunction, fail, be disrupted, or generate outcomes incompatible with continued viability. Conversely, living systems can maintain aspects of their organisation through processes that need not be classified as developmental.

APS therefore distinguishes developmental transformation from organised persistence. Development asks how living organisation is generated or changed through time. Persistence asks how viable organisational continuity is maintained or re-established through change. Particular biological explanations may connect these questions, but the connection must be demonstrated.

## Regulation, Constraint, and Developmental Trajectories

Developmental processes are often regulated and constrained.

Signalling, feedback, timing, gene regulation, cellular interactions, physiological coordination and other processes can influence developmental trajectories. Structural and material constraints can limit what transformations are possible, while regulatory processes can stabilise, redirect or coordinate developmental activity.

Regulation should not be understood as the execution of a fixed developmental endpoint.

A regulated process can remain responsive to changing conditions, and similar developmental outcomes can sometimes arise through different trajectories. Conversely, perturbations can redirect development, reveal dependencies, or produce outcomes outside viable ranges.

Constraint is equally important. Development does not explore an unrestricted space of possible forms. Existing organisation, material properties, regulatory relations, developmental history and environmental conditions can enable some changes while making others inaccessible.

The explanatory task is therefore to identify which regulatory and constraining relations make a difference to the developmental target.

This approach allows developmental stability and flexibility to be analysed together without assuming that regulation exists principally to preserve continuity or that every developmental trajectory is organised around a predetermined outcome.

## Development in Environmental Context

Environmental conditions can make a material difference to development.

Temperature, nutrition, physical surroundings, chemical conditions, interactions with other organisms, social relations, constructed environments and other external conditions can affect developmental trajectories in particular biological systems.

Their relevance, however, must be established rather than assumed.

An environmental influence on development does not automatically make the environment part of developmental organisation. Some environmental conditions function as inputs, resources or boundary conditions. Others may participate more directly in recurrent developmental relations. Still others may have little explanatory importance for the target under investigation.

APS therefore does not define development as organism–environment coupling.

Instead, developmental explanation should extend into environmental or relational conditions when doing so identifies dependencies required to explain the developmental outcome. This preserves the importance of ecological developmental biology, developmental niches and scaffolding without collapsing developmental and ecological explanation into one another.

The extent of developmental explanation is consequently target-dependent.

## Plasticity and Developmental Possibility

Developmental plasticity concerns differences in developmental outcomes or trajectories associated with differences in conditions.

Plasticity can allow organisms to develop differently under nutritional, ecological, physiological, social or other variation. It can therefore be important to understanding how developmental systems respond to changing circumstances.

Plasticity should not, however, be defined simply as a continuity-maintaining capacity.

Some plastic responses may contribute to viability under particular conditions. Others may be neutral with respect to persistence, impose costs, or produce adverse outcomes. Whether a plastic response is beneficial, adaptive or viability-supporting must be established for the biological case concerned.

Developmental possibility is shaped by both responsiveness and constraint. Existing organisation, regulatory architecture, material properties and developmental history can delimit the range of trajectories available to a system, while environmental differences can alter which trajectories are realised.

Plasticity is therefore explanatory when it identifies how developmental outcomes depend upon variable conditions and the organisation capable of responding to them.

It is not evidence, by itself, that development exists to preserve continuity or that flexible developmental change is necessarily adaptive.

## Repair, Regeneration, and Developmental Change

Repair and regeneration can involve developmental processes, but their relation to development should not be fixed by definition.

Repair concerns restoration following damage and may involve physiological, cellular, structural or other processes. Regeneration can involve substantial reconstruction or replacement of damaged or missing organisation and may recruit processes also found in development.

In some systems, developmental regulation, differentiation, pattern formation or morphogenesis may therefore contribute importantly to repair or regeneration.

That does not make every repair process developmental, nor does it make repair and regeneration equivalent.

The explanatory question is which processes restore or reorganise the damaged system and what role, if any, specifically developmental processes play. Where developmental mechanisms are recruited, identifying them can add explanatory specificity. Where restoration is adequately explained without them, developmental redescription adds no necessary gain.

Breakdown also requires care. Developmental processes can fail or become disrupted, and developmental history can affect later fragility. But degeneration, ageing, malfunction and death are not thereby developmental phenomena in general.

Development intersects with these problems where developmental dependencies are materially relevant to the case concerned.

## Development at Evolutionary Interfaces

Development and evolution interact, but they address distinct explanatory targets.

Developmental processes can influence phenotypic expression, plasticity and the production of some forms of variation. Inherited resources can affect developmental processes, while evolutionary history can alter developmental systems across generations.

These relations can be important to evolutionary explanation.

They do not establish a universal sequence in which development produces adaptation, inheritance, variation or evolutionary transformation. Nor does the fact that evolving organisms develop make development constitutive of evolution.

A developmental process may constrain or enable an evolutionary outcome, alter the expression of inherited variation, contribute to a phenotype relevant to selection, or affect organism–environment relations with evolutionary consequences. Conversely, evolutionary change can alter developmental regulation, trajectories and possibilities.

Which of these relations matters must be established for the evolutionary explanandum concerned.

APS therefore treats development and evolution as interacting but non-identical domains. Their integration depends upon demonstrated explanatory relations, not upon assigning them positions within a predetermined continuity hierarchy.

## What Rethinking Development Changes

Rethinking development changes the form of the explanatory question.

If development is treated as execution of a programme, explanation tends to ask where the programme is encoded and how its instructions are implemented. If development is treated instead as materially organised transformation, the questions become more discriminating.

What processes generate the transformation?

What constraints enable or limit the trajectory?

Which regulatory relations make a difference?

Which earlier states or histories are explanatorily relevant?

Does the environment merely influence the process, or does the target require environmental relations within the explanatory extent?

Does plasticity identify a genuine dependency or simply describe variation in outcome?

Do repair or regeneration recruit developmental processes in the case concerned?

Does a developmental relation add anything to an evolutionary explanation, or merely restate the fact that organisms develop?

These questions do not privilege one explanatory scale or require every developmental phenomenon to be explained through an encompassing systems account.

They instead require the explanatory extent to match the target.

This is the principal APS clarification. Moving beyond blueprint models does not license an unrestricted expansion of development into physiology, ecology, persistence, agency or evolution. It requires more precise identification of the materially realised relations through which developmental transformation occurs.

Development can therefore participate in wider biological explanations while retaining a distinct explanandum.

## Conclusion

APS treats development as the materially organised transformation of living organisation through time.

This characterisation rejects the idea that development is adequately explained as execution of a predetermined biological programme, while avoiding the opposite error of treating every process relevant to biological continuity as developmental.

Developmental explanations may involve genetic, cellular, physiological, organismal, historical and environmental relations. Which of these are required depends upon the biological target. Regulation and constraint can shape developmental trajectories; plasticity can make developmental outcomes conditional on circumstances; developmental processes can participate in repair or regeneration; and development can intersect with evolutionary explanation.

None of these relations is universal by definition.

Development is not identical with organised persistence, is not intrinsically organism–environment coupling, is not automatically adaptive, and is not constitutive of evolutionary transformation merely because evolving organisms develop.

The explanatory task is to establish how living organisation is generated and transformed in the case concerned and to identify the processes, constraints, histories and relations that make that transformation intelligible.

Rethinking development therefore means replacing both predetermined blueprint models and unrestricted continuity accounts with a target-matched explanation of organised biological transformation through time.
