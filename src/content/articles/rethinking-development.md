> ***Where this article fits:** This article clarifies what APS means by development. It moves beyond blueprint and programme models without replacing them with a universal continuity-maintenance account. Development concerns the generation and transformation of living organisation through time. Developmental processes may contribute to viable continuity and may interact with physiological, ecological, regenerative and evolutionary processes, but those relations must be established for the biological target concerned.*

*Development is one of the central explanatory problems of biology because living systems are generated and transformed through time. Cells divide and differentiate, structures emerge and are remodelled, physiological organisation changes, behavioural capacities can develop, and organisms interact with changing environments, undergo repair or regeneration in some circumstances, mature and age. The explanatory problem is therefore not adequately captured by asking how a predetermined adult form is produced from an initial state. APS instead asks a broader and more discriminating question: **How is living organisation generated and transformed through time?***

*This formulation shifts attention from the execution of an antecedent plan to the materially realised processes, constraints, regulatory relations and histories through which developmental change occurs. It does not assume that all developmental change preserves viability or that persistence is what makes a process developmental. Development and organised persistence are related explanatory problems, but they are not identical.*

## Introduction - Beyond Blueprint and Programme Models

Development has often been described using blueprint, instruction, code and programme metaphors. Such language has helped organise important research into genes, inheritance, signalling and regulation, but it can become misleading when it suggests that developmental outcomes are already specified in an internal informational description that biological processes merely execute.

Genes are indispensable to many developmental explanations, but genetic sequence alone does not independently specify the complete material organisation through which development occurs. Gene expression takes place in cells with existing structures, regulatory relations and histories; cellular activities occur within tissues and physiological systems; and some developmental outcomes also depend materially on environmental conditions or interactions with other organisms. Developmental explanation therefore requires attention to the relations actually responsible for the target phenomenon.

For some explananda, genetic and cellular mechanisms may provide most of the required explanation, whereas others may require tissue interactions, physiological regulation, developmental history, organismal activity or environmental relations. No fixed list of explanatory components should be imposed in advance. Moving beyond blueprint models consequently does not mean replacing genetic explanation with an equally universal systems description; it means determining which materially realised processes and relations generate the developmental outcome concerned.

## Development as Organised Transformation

Development involves transformations in living organisation. Growth, differentiation, morphogenesis, physiological reorganisation, behavioural development and other developmental processes can alter structures, functions and relations through time. Some changes are gradual, while others reorganise substantial features of the developing system. These transformations are organised in the minimal sense that developmental outcomes depend upon relations among biological processes rather than upon unconstrained material change. Regulation, structural relations, physical conditions, developmental history and existing organisation can enable some trajectories while limiting others.

To characterise development in this way is not to define it by successful continuity. Developmental change may contribute to the maintenance of viable organisation, but developmental processes can also produce dysfunction, fail, be disrupted or generate outcomes incompatible with continued viability. Conversely, living systems can maintain aspects of their organisation through processes that need not be classified as developmental.

APS therefore distinguishes developmental transformation from organised persistence. Development asks how living organisation is generated or changed through time, whereas persistence asks how viable organisational continuity is maintained or re-established through change. Particular biological explanations may connect these questions, but the connection must be demonstrated rather than assumed from their conceptual proximity.

## Regulation, Constraint, and Developmental Trajectories

Developmental processes are often regulated and constrained. Signalling, feedback, timing, gene regulation, cellular interactions, physiological coordination and other processes can influence developmental trajectories, while structural and material constraints can limit what transformations are possible. Regulatory processes may stabilise, redirect or coordinate developmental activity, but regulation should not be understood as the execution of a fixed developmental endpoint. A regulated process can remain responsive to changing conditions, similar developmental outcomes can sometimes arise through different trajectories, and perturbations can redirect development, reveal dependencies or produce outcomes outside viable ranges.

Constraint is equally important because development does not explore an unrestricted space of possible forms. Existing organisation, material properties, regulatory relations, developmental history and environmental conditions can enable some changes while making others inaccessible. The explanatory task is therefore to identify which regulatory and constraining relations make a difference to the developmental target. This allows developmental stability and flexibility to be analysed together without assuming that regulation exists principally to preserve continuity or that every developmental trajectory is organised around a predetermined outcome.

## Development in Environmental Context

Environmental conditions can make a material difference to development. Temperature, nutrition, physical surroundings, chemical conditions, interactions with other organisms, social relations, constructed environments and other external conditions can affect developmental trajectories in particular biological systems. Their relevance, however, must be established rather than assumed.

An environmental influence on development does not automatically make the environment part of developmental organisation. Some environmental conditions may function as inputs, resources or boundary conditions; others may participate more directly in recurrent developmental relations; still others may have little explanatory importance for the target under investigation. APS therefore does not define development as organism–environment coupling.

Developmental explanation should instead extend into environmental or relational conditions when doing so identifies dependencies required to explain the developmental outcome. This preserves the importance of ecological developmental biology, developmental niches and scaffolding without collapsing developmental and ecological explanation into one another. The extent of developmental explanation is consequently target-dependent.

## Plasticity and Developmental Possibility

Developmental plasticity concerns differences in developmental outcomes or trajectories associated with differences in conditions. It can allow organisms to develop differently under nutritional, ecological, physiological, social or other variation and can therefore be important to understanding how developmental systems respond to changing circumstances. Plasticity should not, however, be defined simply as a continuity-maintaining capacity.

Some plastic responses may contribute to viability under particular conditions, whereas others may be neutral with respect to persistence, impose costs or produce adverse outcomes. Whether a plastic response is beneficial, adaptive or viability-supporting must therefore be established for the biological case concerned. Developmental possibility is shaped by both responsiveness and constraint: existing organisation, regulatory architecture, material properties and developmental history can delimit the range of trajectories available to a system, while environmental differences can alter which trajectories are realised.

Plasticity is consequently explanatory when it identifies how developmental outcomes depend upon variable conditions and the organisation capable of responding to them. It is not evidence, by itself, that development exists to preserve continuity or that flexible developmental change is necessarily adaptive.

## Repair, Regeneration, and Developmental Change

Repair and regeneration can involve developmental processes, but their relation to development should not be fixed by definition. Repair concerns restoration following damage and may involve physiological, cellular, structural or other processes, whereas regeneration can involve substantial reconstruction or replacement of damaged or missing organisation and may recruit processes also found in development. In some systems, developmental regulation, differentiation, pattern formation or morphogenesis may therefore contribute importantly to repair or regeneration, but this does not make every repair process developmental or make repair and regeneration equivalent.

The explanatory question is which processes restore or reorganise the damaged system and what role, if any, specifically developmental processes play. Where developmental mechanisms are recruited, identifying them can add explanatory specificity; where restoration is adequately explained without them, developmental redescription adds no necessary gain.

Breakdown requires the same discrimination. Developmental processes can fail or become disrupted, and developmental history can affect later fragility, but degeneration, ageing, malfunction and death are not thereby developmental phenomena in general. Development intersects with these problems only where developmental dependencies are materially relevant to the case concerned.

## Development at Evolutionary Interfaces

Development and evolution interact, but they address distinct explanatory targets. Developmental processes can influence phenotypic expression, plasticity and the production of some forms of variation; inherited resources can affect developmental processes; and evolutionary history can alter developmental systems across generations. These relations can be important to evolutionary explanation, but they do not establish a universal sequence in which development produces adaptation, inheritance, variation or evolutionary transformation. Nor does the fact that evolving organisms develop make development constitutive of evolution.

A developmental process may constrain or enable an evolutionary outcome, alter the expression of inherited variation, contribute to a phenotype relevant to selection, or affect organism–environment relations with evolutionary consequences. Conversely, evolutionary change can alter developmental regulation, trajectories and possibilities. Which of these relations matters must be established for the evolutionary explanandum concerned.

APS therefore treats development and evolution as interacting but non-identical domains. Their integration depends upon demonstrated explanatory relations rather than upon assigning them positions within a predetermined continuity hierarchy.

## What Rethinking Development Changes

Rethinking development changes the form of the explanatory question. If development is treated as execution of a programme, explanation tends to ask where the programme is encoded and how its instructions are implemented. If development is treated instead as materially organised transformation, explanation must ask which processes generate the transformation, which constraints enable or limit its trajectory, which regulatory relations make a difference, and which earlier states or histories are explanatorily relevant.

The same discipline applies when developmental explanation extends beyond relatively local processes. Environmental conditions should be included when the target requires environmental relations within the explanatory extent rather than merely because development occurs in an environment. Plasticity matters when it identifies a genuine dependency rather than simply describing variation in outcome. Repair or regeneration should be related to development when developmental processes are actually recruited in the case concerned. Similarly, a developmental relation belongs in an evolutionary explanation only when it adds something beyond restating the fact that organisms develop.

These questions neither privilege one explanatory scale nor require every developmental phenomenon to be explained through an encompassing systems account. They require the explanatory extent to match the target. This is the principal APS clarification: moving beyond blueprint models does not license an unrestricted expansion of development into physiology, ecology, persistence, agency or evolution. It requires more precise identification of the materially realised relations through which developmental transformation occurs. Development can therefore participate in wider biological explanations while retaining a distinct explanandum.

## Conclusion

APS treats development as the materially organised transformation of living organisation through time. This characterisation rejects the idea that development is adequately explained as execution of a predetermined biological programme while avoiding the opposite error of treating every process relevant to biological continuity as developmental.

Developmental explanations may involve genetic, cellular, physiological, organismal, historical and environmental relations, but which of these are required depends upon the biological target. Regulation and constraint can shape developmental trajectories; plasticity can make developmental outcomes conditional on circumstances; developmental processes can participate in repair or regeneration; and development can intersect with evolutionary explanation. None of these relations is universal by definition.

Development is therefore not identical with organised persistence, intrinsically organism–environment coupling, automatically adaptive, or constitutive of evolutionary transformation merely because evolving organisms develop. The explanatory task is to establish how living organisation is generated and transformed in the case concerned and to identify the processes, constraints, histories and relations that make that transformation intelligible. Rethinking development, in this sense, means replacing both predetermined blueprint models and unrestricted continuity accounts with a target-matched explanation of organised biological transformation through time.
