## Purpose — The Organisation of Viability-Oriented Activity

Biology is saturated with purposive language. Physiologists describe the heart as maintaining circulation and the kidneys as regulating fluid and electrolyte balance. Ecologists describe animals as seeking resources, avoiding predators, and reproducing under changing environmental conditions. Plant biologists describe roots as growing towards water and nutrients, shoots as orienting towards light, and damaged tissues as activating repair and defence responses.

Such descriptions are not confined to popular biology. They permeate physiology, ecology, developmental biology, plant biology, behaviour, and evolutionary explanation, where living systems are routinely described as organising activity around survival, reproduction, repair, regulation, and persistence.

Does purposive language merely provide a convenient shorthand for mechanistic and evolutionary explanation, or does it identify an organisational feature of living systems that any adequate biological explanation must itself explain?

Organisms regulate themselves, repair damage, seek resources, avoid threats, adapt to changing conditions, maintain internal organisation, and preserve continuity across time.

Such descriptions are not confined to popular biology. They permeate physiology, ecology, developmental biology, behaviour, and evolutionary explanation, where living systems are routinely described as acting for survival, reproduction, repair, regulation, or persistence.

The persistence of this language poses a longstanding scientific and philosophical question. Does it merely provide a convenient shorthand for underlying physical and evolutionary processes, or does it identify a genuine feature of living organisation that biological explanation must account for?

Living systems are organised around the maintenance and re-establishment of the conditions of their own persistence, yet purposive language has long remained philosophically controversial.

Some traditions interpret purposiveness as evidence of external design, conscious intention, or irreducible teleology. Others seek to eliminate purposive language altogether, treating it as a convenient way of describing processes that can ultimately be explained entirely through mechanistic or evolutionary accounts.

APS rejects both alternatives.

Purpose is neither externally imposed nor an explanatory illusion. It is a real organisational feature of living systems that arises from the viability-oriented organisation of biological activity through which organisms actively maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

This article argues that purposiveness can be naturalised without appealing to conscious intention, represented future goals, or mystical teleology. In doing so, it explains why purposive language remains both scientifically legitimate and biologically indispensable.

## Purpose as Viability-Oriented Organisation

If purpose is neither imposed from outside living systems nor reducible to metaphor, what kind of biological phenomenon is it?

APS answers this question by treating purpose not as a property added to organisms, but as an aspect of their organisation.

In APS, purpose is the organisation of activity relative to viability-oriented organised persistence.

Living systems continuously organise activity such that some conditions preserve continuity, some trajectories support viability, some reorganisations restore persistence, and some conditions threaten organisational collapse. Purpose therefore concerns the organisation of these viability-relative differences rather than the pursuit of externally represented goals.

Purpose is not something added to living systems from outside, nor is it an independent force directing biological activity. It arises from the organisation through which living systems actively maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

To describe a living system as purposive is therefore to say that it differentiates among conditions relative to viability, modulates activity accordingly, and continuously regulates its own organisation in ways that sustain organised persistence across changing circumstances.

Purpose is enacted in the present through viability-oriented biological activity. It is not caused by future states but by the ongoing organisation through which living systems preserve the conditions of their own persistence.

## Organised Persistence and Continuity Regulation

If living systems are continually changing, in what sense do they persist?

This question lies at the heart of biological explanation. Organisms undergo continuous material turnover, developmental transformation, energetic fluctuation, ecological perturbation, repair, adaptive reconstruction, and ongoing interaction with their environments. Almost every aspect of their material composition and activity changes over time.

Yet living systems do not merely endure these changes—they preserve their organisation through them. Biological persistence is therefore not the maintenance of static material structure but the active maintenance and re-establishment of organisational continuity across continual change.

APS describes this as organised persistence.

Purpose concerns the viability-oriented organisation through which this continuity is maintained.

Living systems actively regulate the conditions required for their own persistence by sustaining metabolic organisation, developmental continuity, ecological coordination, behavioural regulation, and adaptive responsiveness. These activities do not preserve a fixed state; they preserve the organisational conditions under which the system continues to exist as the same living individual.

Purpose therefore reflects the continuity-oriented organisation of biological activity rather than the pursuit of externally represented future goals. Living systems persist because they continuously regulate the conditions of their own persistence.

## Purpose, Agency, and Normativity

If purpose organises biological activity, why do some conditions become significant to living systems while others do not?

APS answers this question through the organisational relationship between agency, purpose, and normativity.

Agency is the viability-oriented activity through which living systems actively maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own organised persistence. Purpose is the organisational orientation of that activity relative to viability. Neither concept is intelligible in isolation; agency enacts the activity through which purpose is realised, while purpose describes the organisational orientation that gives that activity biological significance.

Normativity follows because viability-oriented organisation can succeed or fail.

Some organisational states stabilise persistence, support continuity, restore degraded organisation, or preserve viability. Others undermine persistence, disrupt continuity, or contribute to organisational collapse.

This asymmetry is intrinsic to living organisation. It is not imposed by an external observer, moral judgement, or conscious evaluation. Rather, it arises because living systems continuously regulate activity relative to the conditions required for their own persistence.

Purpose therefore helps explain why some conditions matter biologically. Living systems differentiate among environmental and internal conditions according to their consequences for viability, making some states biologically favourable, others unfavourable, and still others irrelevant.

Agency, purpose, and normativity therefore describe analytically distinct but organisationally inseparable aspects of viability-oriented organised persistence. Together they explain how living systems generate biological significance through the ongoing organisation of their own activity.

## Purpose and Function

How do biological functions become functions?

This question lies at the centre of the philosophy of biology. Hearts pump blood, leaves photosynthesise, roots absorb water, kidneys regulate electrolyte balance, and immune cells eliminate pathogens. Biology routinely describes these activities as the functions of particular structures or processes. Yet what makes these contributions functional rather than merely causal remains a matter of longstanding philosophical debate.

APS argues that functions cannot be understood independently of the purposive organisation of living systems.

Purpose concerns the viability-oriented organisation of biological activity as a whole. Function concerns the specific contributions that processes, structures, or behaviours make to maintaining and re-establishing that organisation. Function is therefore not an intrinsic property of a component but a relational property arising from its contribution to the organised persistence of the living system.

Purpose therefore has explanatory priority over function. Living systems are not purposive because they possess functions. Rather, processes become functional because they contribute to a system whose activity is already organised around viability-oriented persistence.

A metabolic pathway, repair mechanism, developmental process, behavioural response, or physiological regulation is functional because it contributes to maintaining the organisational conditions through which the living system continues to exist.

APS therefore naturalises biological function without appealing to external design, historical selection alone, or observer-imposed purpose. Functions derive their biological significance from their present contribution to viability-oriented organised persistence.

## Mechanistic Realisation of Purposive Organisation

If living systems are composed of mechanisms, why are mechanistic descriptions alone insufficient to explain biological purposiveness?

Mechanistic explanation has transformed modern biology by revealing how metabolic pathways, signalling systems, neural circuits, developmental processes, physiological regulation, and behavioural interactions generate the activities of living organisms. APS fully accepts the indispensable role of mechanistic explanation in biology.

The question is not whether living systems are mechanistically realised, but whether mechanisms alone explain why those activities are organised around the maintenance of viability.

APS argues that they do not.

Mechanisms explain how particular biological processes occur. They do not, by themselves, explain why those processes constitute the organised activity of a living system rather than an arbitrary collection of causal interactions. That explanatory step requires reference to the viability-oriented organisation within which mechanistic processes acquire their biological significance.

Mechanisms therefore realise purposive organisation without independently constituting purposiveness. Metabolic pathways, developmental programmes, repair mechanisms, behavioural responses, and physiological regulation are biologically intelligible because they contribute to maintaining and re-establishing the organised persistence of the organism.

APS therefore preserves mechanistic biology while locating mechanistic explanation within a broader organisational framework. Mechanisms explain the realisation of biological activity; purposive organisation explains why those mechanisms together constitute the organised persistence of a living system.

## Purpose Without Representation or Design

Must biological purpose depend upon mental representation or external design?

This question has long divided philosophy and biology. Purposive behaviour is often associated with conscious intention, symbolic representation, explicit goal-directed planning, or intelligent design. If these assumptions are correct, then genuine purposiveness would be restricted to cognitive agents or would require an external designer.

APS rejects both conclusions.

Purpose does not require internal symbolic representation, conscious foresight, explicit goal modelling, external design, or optimisation towards predetermined endpoints.

The reason is straightforward. Purpose does not originate in cognition but in the viability-oriented organisation of living systems. Organisms regulate activity relative to the conditions of their own persistence whether or not they possess nervous systems, symbolic thought, or conscious awareness.

Plants grow towards light, roots proliferate where water and nutrients are available, bacteria modify their movement in response to chemical gradients, and single cells repair damage and regulate metabolism. These activities are purposive because they contribute to maintaining and re-establishing the conditions of organised persistence, not because the organism represents future states or consciously intends particular outcomes.

Representation may become biologically important in organisms possessing sufficiently sophisticated cognitive capacities, but it is one possible organisational achievement of life rather than the source of biological purposiveness itself.

APS therefore naturalises purpose by locating it in the present organisation of viability-oriented activity. Purpose is realised through the ongoing regulation of organised persistence, while representation—where it exists—extends rather than constitutes that purposive organisation.

## Purpose, Evaluation, and Semiosis

How do differences in the world become biologically significant?

Living organisms continuously encounter changes in their internal and external environments. Most of these changes are biologically irrelevant, while others influence whether the organism can maintain and re-establish the conditions of its own persistence. The question is therefore not simply how organisms detect differences, but how some differences come to matter biologically.

APS answers this question through the organisational relationship between purpose, evaluation, and semiosis.

Purpose organises biological activity relative to viability-oriented persistence. Evaluation operationalises that organisation by differentiating among conditions according to their consequences for viability. Through evaluation, living systems distinguish favourable from unfavourable conditions, stabilising from destabilising relations, and continuity-supporting from continuity-threatening circumstances.

Semiosis follows because evaluated differences acquire biological significance.

Environmental cues, chemical gradients, mechanical interactions, ecological conditions, and signals become meaningful not because they intrinsically carry meaning, but because they influence the organism's capacity to maintain and re-establish the conditions of its own persistence.

Purpose therefore explains why evaluation is possible, evaluation explains how biological significance is generated, and semiosis explains how biologically significant differences become organised within ongoing interactions between organism and environment.

Together, purpose, evaluation, and semiosis describe successive aspects of the viability-oriented organisation through which living systems generate biological significance.

## Teleonomy Reconsidered

Why has biology retained the concept of teleonomy after rejecting classical teleology?

For more than half a century, teleonomy has provided biologists with a way of describing the goal-directed organisation of living systems without invoking Aristotelian final causes or external design. Yet the concept has remained philosophically unsettled. It is often presented either as a convenient linguistic substitute for teleology or as a way of describing the appearance of goal-directedness without attributing genuine purposiveness to living systems.

APS argues that neither interpretation is sufficient.

Teleonomy is not merely a way of speaking about organisms, nor does it describe only an apparent orientation towards future goals. It identifies a real organisational feature of living systems grounded in viability-oriented organised persistence.

Living systems are organised such that their activities contribute to maintaining and re-establishing the conditions of their own persistence. Metabolic regulation preserves organisational integrity, adaptive responses restore viability under changing conditions, developmental processes maintain continuity through transformation, and organism–environment interactions continually reorganise the conditions under which persistence remains possible.

Teleonomy therefore does not arise from represented future states or externally imposed purposes. It arises from the present organisation of biological activity relative to viability. The apparent goal-directedness of living systems reflects a genuine organisational orientation through which they actively maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

APS therefore retains the concept of teleonomy while naturalising it more fully. Teleonomy is neither an explanatory fiction nor a surrogate for classical teleology. It is the organisational expression of viability-oriented organised persistence.

## What APS Does—and Does Not—Claim About Purpose

What follows from understanding purpose as the viability-oriented organisation of biological activity?

APS's account differs from several familiar interpretations of purposiveness, but it should not be understood as rejecting either modern biology or mechanistic explanation. Rather, it provides an organisational account of why purposive language remains scientifically appropriate.

Purpose in APS is not conscious intention, explicit goal representation, externally assigned function, mystical final causation, optimisation towards predetermined endpoints, or evidence of intelligent design.

Nor does APS reject mechanistic biology, evolutionary explanation, or the importance of natural selection in explaining the historical emergence of biological organisation.

Instead, APS argues that purposiveness is an intrinsic feature of living organisation. It arises because living systems actively maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence through viability-oriented organisation, continuity-preserving regulation, endogenous normativity, and biological agency.

Understanding purpose in this way allows purposive language to retain its explanatory role without invoking mentalistic or supernatural assumptions. Purpose is neither imposed upon living systems nor projected onto them by observers. It is a real organisational feature of viability-oriented organised persistence.

## Why Purpose Matters

Why does clarifying purpose matter for biological explanation?

Purpose is one of the oldest and most contested concepts in biology. For much of the history of science it has appeared to force a choice between two unsatisfactory alternatives: either purposiveness reflects external design or irreducible teleology, or it is merely a convenient way of describing processes that are fully explained by mechanistic or evolutionary accounts.

APS rejects this opposition.

By understanding purpose as the viability-oriented organisation of biological activity, APS explains why purposive language has remained indispensable throughout biology without requiring conscious intention, represented future goals, external design, or mystical final causes.

This account has important explanatory consequences. It explains why living systems regulate activity relative to viability, why biological functions acquire significance through their contribution to organised persistence, why normativity arises naturally from the possibility of organisational success and failure, why evaluation generates biological significance, why semiosis becomes possible, and how adaptation contributes to maintaining and re-establishing organised persistence across changing conditions.

More broadly, it clarifies why living systems differ fundamentally from artefacts and externally designed machines. Organisms are not organised around purposes imposed from outside but around the ongoing activity through which they maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

APS therefore naturalises purposiveness without reducing it either to classical teleology or to mechanistic reductionism. Purpose becomes neither an explanatory anomaly nor a metaphor, but an intrinsic feature of viability-oriented organised persistence.

## Conclusion

What, then, is biological purpose?

APS argues that purpose is neither an externally imposed design, a product of conscious intention, nor a convenient metaphor for mechanistic processes. It is the viability-oriented organisation of biological activity through which living systems actively maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own organised persistence.

Understanding purpose in this way clarifies why purposive language remains indispensable throughout biology. Functions become intelligible through their contribution to organised persistence, mechanistic processes realise rather than constitute purposive organisation, normativity arises because living organisation can succeed or fail, and evaluation and semiosis explain how biological significance emerges from viability-oriented activity.

Purpose is therefore not an isolated concept but one aspect of the organisational architecture of life itself. Together with agency, function, normativity, evaluation, and semiosis, it contributes to a unified account of how living systems organise and sustain themselves across continual change.

APS consequently naturalises biological purposiveness without invoking external design, conscious intention, or irreducible teleology. Purpose is an intrinsic feature of viability-oriented organised persistence and an indispensable concept for understanding living organisation.
