## Where This Article Fits

*Inheritance and Continuity in APS* is a Core article within the Evolutionary Dynamics topic.

Its central question is how living organisation is reconstituted across generations, allowing biological lineages to maintain continuity while remaining capable of variation and evolutionary transformation.

The article develops the APS interpretation of inheritance as a relationship between transmitted developmental resources and the processes through which viable biological organisation is established.

This interpretation does not deny the explanatory significance of genes or other inherited components. It distinguishes the transmission of such resources from the wider developmental achievement of producing a living system capable of sustaining its organisation.

The article builds upon the APS accounts of biological organisation, agency, viability, constraint closure, Process, development, and organised persistence.

It also draws upon established research in genetics, developmental systems theory, extended inheritance, evolutionary developmental biology, and processual approaches to biological explanation.

Its relationship to **Evolution as the Historical Transformation of Organised Persistence** is especially important. The Evolution anchor develops the wider APS account of evolutionary change. The present article concentrates upon the inheritance and developmental continuity that contribute to evolutionary processes.

It also connects with **Cultural Inheritance**, which examines socially mediated transmission and reconstruction. Biological and cultural inheritance involve distinguishable mechanisms and explanatory targets, although they can interact through gene–culture coevolution and inherited ecological arrangements.

The purpose of this article is not to establish an alternative theory of heredity that supersedes genetics. It is to clarify what must be explained when inherited resources contribute to the reconstitution of viable living organisation.

## Introduction — Inheritance as Organisational Continuity

Inheritance occupies a central position in evolutionary biology because it connects successive generations.

Organisms reproduce, offspring develop, and biological lineages persist despite the death of individual organisms and continual changes in their material composition.

The processes underlying this continuity have been investigated through genetics, embryology, developmental biology, physiology, ecology, and evolutionary theory.

Genetic inheritance provides one of the most successful and precisely developed explanations in biology. The transmission of DNA sequences, together with their patterns of segregation, recombination, and mutation, is fundamental to understanding heredity and evolutionary change.

Yet the production of a living organism involves more than the transmission of genetic material.

An inherited genome becomes biologically effective within a cellular and developmental context. Its products participate in molecular interactions, regulatory processes, physiological relationships, and environmental exchanges through which a living system is formed and maintained.

This distinction between the transmission of inherited resources and their developmental realisation provides the starting point for the APS interpretation.

Within APS, inheritance is examined in relation to the reconstitution of viability-oriented organisation across generations.

The central question is not whether genes are inherited. They clearly are.

It is how inherited genetic and other developmental resources contribute to the formation of a living system capable of sustaining itself.

This question connects inheritance with the broader APS account of organised persistence.

Living systems persist through activities that maintain the conditions of their continued functioning. Reproduction and development make it possible for related forms of organisation to be established in successive generations.

Biological continuity therefore involves both the transmission of resources and the reconstruction of functioning organisation.

The distinction is important because evolutionary transformation requires historically connected biological systems, but it does not require that every component or organisational relationship remain unchanged.

Inheritance can preserve continuity while permitting variation.

APS approaches this relationship organisationally, without assuming that the organisational description replaces genetic, developmental, or population-level explanations.

## Beyond Component Transmission

[[box:inheritance-is-not-information-transfer]]

Many familiar accounts of inheritance begin with the transmission of genetic material.

This emphasis is justified by the extraordinary explanatory success of genetics. Genes and genetic variants can be identified, their transmission followed, and their contributions to development and evolutionary change investigated experimentally.

Population genetics provides rigorous models of how inherited genetic variation changes through mutation, selection, drift, migration, and other processes.

These achievements should not be diminished by an organisational interpretation.

However, genetic transmission and the developmental production of viable organisms are distinguishable explanatory phenomena.

A genome does not independently construct an organism.

Its biological effects depend upon cellular machinery, regulatory relationships, metabolic activity, developmental interactions, and environmental conditions. These processes determine how inherited genetic resources contribute to the formation and maintenance of living organisation.

APS therefore distinguishes the inheritance of components from the reconstitution of the organisation in which those components become effective.

This does not mean that genes cease to be legitimate units of inheritance or evolutionary analysis.

It means that their causal contributions must be understood in relation to the biological processes through which they operate.

The distinction is consistent with several established developments in theoretical biology.

Developmental systems theory, particularly the work of Griffiths and Gray (1994), and Oyama, Griffiths, and Gray (2001), challenges explanations that assign all developmental responsibility to genetic resources while treating other contributing conditions as merely external.

Extended-inheritance approaches, including Jablonka and Lamb (2005), examine additional mechanisms through which biological and behavioural resources can contribute to continuity across generations.

These traditions already investigate many of the relationships that APS seeks to emphasise.

APS should therefore not present the recognition of non-genetic developmental resources as an exclusively APS discovery.

Its particular concern is to relate these resources to the organisational conditions under which viable living systems are re-established.

The explanatory question becomes how transmitted components, developmental processes, and relevant environmental relationships jointly contribute to the continuity of living organisation.

## Development as Reconstitution

Inheritance becomes biologically effective through development.

Development is not simply the passive expression of inherited material. It involves organised processes through which cellular structures, tissues, physiological relationships, and organismal capacities emerge and change.

From the earliest stages of reproduction, development depends upon interactions among genetic resources, cellular organisation, regulatory processes, metabolism, and environmental conditions.

These interactions contribute to the formation of a living system capable of maintaining the activities necessary for its continued functioning.

APS interprets this process as the developmental reconstitution of viability-oriented organisation.

The term *reconstitution* does not imply that development reconstructs an exact copy of a previous organism.

Offspring differ from their parents, developmental pathways vary, and organisms may undergo substantial changes in form and function while remaining members of continuing biological lineages.

Reconstitution instead identifies the establishment of an organisation capable of sustaining living activity.

This perspective is compatible with the rejection of simple genetic-blueprint metaphors found in developmental systems theory and evolutionary developmental biology.

It also accords with the emphasis placed by West-Eberhard (2003) on developmental plasticity and the evolutionary significance of developmentally generated variation.

However, APS should avoid treating development as though it merely executes a pre-existing organisational pattern.

Development is productive.

It generates structures, relationships, and capacities through interactions whose outcomes depend upon inherited resources and the conditions under which those resources operate.

Some developmental processes are highly reliable. Others exhibit considerable plasticity or sensitivity to environmental circumstances.

The resulting organisation is therefore neither wholly predetermined nor unconstrained.

It emerges through materially realised processes whose organisation can be investigated at molecular, cellular, physiological, organismal, and ecological scales.

This helps explain why inheritance and development are closely related but conceptually distinguishable.

Inheritance concerns the resources and relations through which continuity is established between generations.

Development concerns the processes through which those resources contribute to the formation and maintenance of living organisation.

Neither can be fully understood without considering the other, but their explanatory responsibilities are not identical.

APS therefore treats developmental reconstitution as central to understanding the biological significance of inheritance.

## The Role of Environment in Continuity

Biological development occurs through interactions between organisms and their environments.

Environmental conditions influence growth, metabolism, behaviour, physiology, and the developmental pathways through which living organisation is established.

Some of these conditions are relatively stable across generations. Others are modified by parental activity, ecological processes, or the continuing behaviour of organisms within a lineage.

These relationships raise important questions about environmental and ecological inheritance.

An organism may encounter a nest, burrow, soil environment, microbial community, or other ecological arrangement partly constructed or maintained by earlier generations.

Such resources can contribute to the development and persistence of subsequent organisms.

Parental provisioning, habitat modification, symbiotic transmission, and other processes can establish forms of cross-generational continuity that are not adequately described by genetic transmission alone.

However, an important distinction must be maintained.

Not every environmental influence is inherited.

Temperature, light, nutrient availability, and ecological interactions may strongly affect development without necessarily constituting inherited resources in a particular explanatory context.

To describe an environmental condition as inherited, it is necessary to establish a relevant relationship of cross-generational continuity.

This may involve the transmission of material resources, the persistence of a constructed environment, the reproduction of a symbiotic association, or other identifiable processes connecting successive generations.

APS should therefore distinguish environmental dependence from environmental inheritance.

The former concerns the conditions affecting biological activity and development. The latter concerns particular relationships through which developmental resources or conditions are maintained across generations.

This distinction is consistent with the broader APS treatment of organism–environment coupling.

Living systems depend upon environmental relationships, but the explanatory significance of those relationships must be established for the biological phenomenon concerned.

The relevant question is which environmental conditions participate in the developmental reconstitution of viable organisation, and which of those conditions exhibit a demonstrable inheritance relationship.

## Reliability Without Replication

Inheritance can produce substantial biological continuity without requiring exact replication of every component or organisational relationship.

Organisms undergo continual material turnover. Their developmental trajectories differ, and their environments change. Even closely related organisms may exhibit significant differences in morphology, physiology, behaviour, and developmental history.

Yet lineages can remain historically continuous.

The explanation depends partly upon the reliable transmission of inherited resources and partly upon the developmental processes through which those resources contribute to living organisation.

Genetic replication can exhibit high fidelity, but developmental outcomes are not exact copies of earlier organisms.

Similarly, the transmission of cellular structures or environmental resources may contribute to continuity without producing identical developmental circumstances.

APS therefore distinguishes replication fidelity from organisational reliability.

Replication fidelity concerns the accuracy with which particular inherited components or structures are reproduced.

Organisational reliability concerns the extent to which developmental processes establish living systems capable of maintaining their organisation within relevant conditions.

These properties are related, but they are not equivalent.

High genetic fidelity does not guarantee successful development under every environmental circumstance. Conversely, developmental systems may produce viable outcomes despite genetic variation or changes in environmental conditions.

The relevant organisation may exhibit robustness, plasticity, compensatory regulation, or other capacities that contribute to developmental continuity.

These capacities are themselves empirical phenomena requiring explanation.

APS does not assume that viability can always be maintained or that developmental variation necessarily remains within acceptable limits.

Some developmental outcomes fail. Others produce viable organisms with altered characteristics. Still others generate differences that become relevant to subsequent evolutionary processes.

Inheritance therefore supports both continuity and the possibility of transformation.

The historical persistence of a lineage does not depend upon the perfect duplication of its members, but it does require sufficient continuity in the processes connecting successive generations.

The nature and degree of that continuity depend upon the biological case.

## Inheritance and Evolutionary Transformation

Inheritance contributes to evolutionary transformation because it connects successive generations through processes that can preserve and transmit biologically relevant variation.

Without such historical relationships, changes occurring in one generation would not necessarily contribute to evolutionary differences in subsequent generations.

However, inheritance alone does not explain evolutionary change.

Mutation, recombination, developmental variation, selection, drift, migration, ecological change, and other processes can influence the characteristics and historical trajectories of populations and lineages.

The significance of inheritance is that it provides some of the relationships through which differences become historically connected.

APS interprets these relationships through organised persistence, but it does not infer a universal evolutionary sequence from the ordering of its concepts.

Biological agency, viability, organised persistence, inheritance, variation, fitness, natural selection, and evolutionary transformation identify distinguishable explanatory concerns.

Their causal relationships must be established through biological investigation.

Inheritance is particularly important because it concerns the continuity of resources and developmental processes across generations.

Variation concerns differences among biological systems and the processes through which those differences arise.

Fitness concerns reproductive success or other appropriately specified measures of evolutionary performance, depending upon the theoretical and empirical context.

Natural selection concerns differential reproductive success associated with heritable variation in the relevant population-level account.

These distinctions matter because organisational persistence and evolutionary fitness are not identical concepts.

An organism may maintain its own viability for an extended period without making a substantial reproductive contribution to future generations.

Conversely, evolutionary success may depend upon reproductive relationships that cannot be inferred from the physiological persistence of an individual organism alone.

APS should therefore avoid treating fitness as simply the duration of organised persistence.

The relationship between viability, reproduction, inheritance, and evolutionary change must be established for the system under investigation.

The wider APS interpretation of these relationships belongs to **Evolution as the Historical Transformation of Organised Persistence**.

The present article concentrates upon the contribution of inheritance to biological continuity.

## From Information to Organisation

Information is an important concept in genetics, development, and evolutionary biology.

DNA sequences can be analysed as carriers of genetic information. Regulatory signals influence cellular processes. Developmental systems respond to environmental cues, and organisms acquire and use information through physiological and behavioural activities.

Such descriptions can be scientifically productive.

APS does not reject informational explanations merely because they employ the language of codes, signals, or instructions.

The relevant concern is whether informational descriptions adequately specify the material processes through which their biological effects occur.

Genetic information, for example, becomes effective through transcription, translation, regulation, molecular interaction, and the wider organisation of living cells.

An informational description may accurately identify a relationship while leaving further questions about its material implementation.

APS therefore asks how informationally described resources participate in the organisation of biological processes.

This is not equivalent to claiming that established genetic or informational theories necessarily treat information as an independent causal substance.

Many such approaches already investigate the molecular and developmental mechanisms through which information is realised.

The APS distinction is consequently methodological and explanatory.

It encourages clarification of the relationship between descriptions of inherited information and the material organisation through which inherited resources contribute to development.

This perspective is compatible with the broader processual emphasis associated with Nicholson and Dupré (2018), and with organisational approaches to biological explanation.

However, the recognition that information requires material implementation is not itself a distinctive scientific discovery of APS.

Its value within the framework depends upon whether it helps formulate more precise questions or identify explanatory relationships that would otherwise remain obscure.

Inheritance can therefore be investigated through genetic, informational, developmental, and organisational descriptions, provided their respective explanatory contributions are kept clear.

## Inheritance Within Evolutionary Organisation

Inheritance occupies an important position within the APS account of evolutionary organisation.

It concerns how biological continuity extends across generations, allowing living systems to remain historically connected despite individual mortality, developmental variation, and changing environments.

This continuity depends upon the transmission of inherited resources and the developmental processes through which those resources become effective.

Genes, cellular structures, regulatory relationships, parental contributions, and some environmental resources may participate in these processes.

Their contributions differ among organisms and biological circumstances.

APS therefore does not require that all forms of inheritance be explained through one uniform mechanism.

Nor does it require that every inherited resource contribute equally to the reconstitution of viable organisation.

The explanatory task is to identify the relevant resources, processes, and relationships for the case concerned.

This approach also clarifies the distinction between inheritance and the wider processes of evolution.

Inheritance may preserve characteristics across generations, but the distribution and evolutionary consequences of inherited variation depend upon additional processes.

Selection, drift, migration, mutation, and ecological change can alter evolutionary trajectories in ways that cannot be derived from inheritance alone.

An organisational account of inheritance must therefore remain compatible with population genetics and other established approaches to evolutionary explanation.

The APS framework provides a way of asking how the inherited resources of living systems participate in the continuing organisation of biological lineages.

It does not establish that every evolutionary phenomenon is best explained at the level of organismal organisation.

Some questions require molecular genetic explanations. Others require developmental, ecological, population-level, or multiscale analyses.

The appropriate explanatory scale depends upon the phenomenon being investigated.

Inheritance consequently connects the APS account of living organisation with evolutionary history while preserving the explanatory autonomy of the mechanisms responsible for evolutionary change.

## Key Point

Inheritance contributes to the continuity of biological lineages through the transmission of developmental resources and the processes through which those resources help reconstitute viable living organisation.

APS emphasises this organisational dimension without denying the fundamental explanatory importance of genes, developmental mechanisms, or population-level evolutionary processes.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

How do inherited resources and developmental processes contribute to the reliable reconstitution of viable living organisation across generations while permitting variation and evolutionary transformation?

### Architectural Role

This article is a Core article within Evolutionary Dynamics.

It develops the APS interpretation of inheritance as a relationship between the transmission of inherited resources and the developmental reconstitution of viability-oriented organisation.

Its responsibility is to clarify the biological significance of cross-generational continuity rather than to replace genetic theories of heredity or the broader APS account of evolution.

### Builds On

The article builds upon the APS accounts of biological organisation, viability, organised persistence, constraint closure, development, organism–environment coupling, biological agency, Process, and Scale.

It also draws upon genetics, developmental systems theory, extended-inheritance approaches, evolutionary developmental biology, and processual accounts of biological organisation.

### Relationship to Development

Inheritance and development are closely related but distinguishable.

Inheritance concerns the transmission and cross-generational continuity of biologically relevant resources and relationships.

Development concerns the processes through which those resources contribute to the formation and maintenance of living organisation.

APS examines their interaction without reducing either to the other.

### Relationship to Evolution

**Evolution as the Historical Transformation of Organised Persistence** develops the wider evolutionary architecture.

The present article concentrates upon inheritance and developmental continuity.

Variation, fitness, natural selection, drift, and other evolutionary processes remain distinguishable explanatory phenomena whose relationships with inheritance must be established for the biological case concerned.

### Relationship to Cultural Inheritance

**Cultural Inheritance** examines the transmission, reconstruction, and transformation of socially established practices, knowledge, and organisational resources.

Biological and cultural inheritance have different mechanisms and explanatory targets, although they can interact through development, ecology, and gene–culture coevolution.

Neither should be reduced to the other.

### Explanatory Boundaries

Genetic inheritance remains a legitimate and fundamental subject of biological explanation.

Not every developmental influence is inherited, and not every environmental condition affecting an organism constitutes ecological inheritance.

The developmental reconstitution of viable organisation is distinguishable from genetic replication, but it does not render genetic or population-level explanations dispensable.

Organisational persistence must not be equated without qualification with evolutionary fitness or reproductive success.

APS does not claim that its organisational interpretation has demonstrated explanatory superiority over developmental systems theory, extended-inheritance approaches, or established evolutionary biology.

Such claims require comparison with the strongest relevant alternatives in specified biological cases.

### Corpus Significance

Within APS, inheritance connects the organisation of living systems with the historical continuity of biological lineages.

It clarifies how transmitted resources become effective through development and how viable organisation can be re-established despite material turnover, variation, and changing environmental conditions.

Its principal architectural contribution is to distinguish component transmission from developmental reconstitution while integrating both within the wider APS investigation of organised persistence.
