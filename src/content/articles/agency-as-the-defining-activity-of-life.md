> **Where this article fits:** This article develops one of the central claims of the APS framework: that agency is the defining activity of life. It explains why APS begins with agency rather than genes, metabolism, information, reproduction, or evolution, and shows how viability-oriented agency provides the conceptual foundation for biological organisation, persistence, adaptation, development, ecology, cognition, and diagnosis. For a broader introduction to the framework, see *What Is APS?*, *Agency in Contemporary Biology*, and *Viability — The Organising Principle of Biological Persistence*.

## Why APS Begins with Agency

Biology has long sought a principle capable of distinguishing living systems from the vast range of non-living processes found throughout the natural world. Different traditions have emphasised different candidates. Some have looked to metabolism, others to reproduction, information, evolution, complexity, self-organisation, or autonomy. Each of these approaches captures something important about living systems, yet each also encounters limitations when treated as the primary explanatory foundation of life.

APS begins from a different starting point. Rather than asking which component, mechanism, or property is most fundamental, it asks what living systems actually do. The answer proposed by APS is that living systems continuously engage in activities directed toward the preservation of their own viability. They regulate internal conditions, repair damage, maintain boundaries, adapt to changing circumstances, coordinate interactions with their environments, and reorganise themselves in response to perturbation. These activities differ enormously across organisms and across scales, yet they share a common organisational orientation toward continued persistence.

APS refers to this distinctive form of activity as agency. Agency is not introduced as an additional feature layered onto living systems after they have already been identified as alive. Rather, agency is proposed as the activity through which living organisation is sustained in the first place. Living systems are not merely things that possess agency; they are systems whose continued existence depends upon ongoing agential activity.

This shift in perspective has important consequences. If agency is fundamental, then many familiar biological phenomena can be understood as different expressions of the same underlying organisational principle. Adaptation, development, regulation, resilience, evolution, and cognition become connected not because they share a common mechanism but because they all participate in the viability-oriented activity through which living systems sustain themselves across time.

APS therefore begins with agency because agency identifies the active organisational character of life itself. It focuses attention on what living systems continually do in order to remain living rather than on any single structure, substance, or mechanism through which that activity happens to be expressed.

## Agency Is Activity, Not a Trait

One reason the concept of agency remains controversial in biology is that it is often interpreted through concepts derived from human psychology. Agency is frequently associated with conscious decision-making, intention, deliberation, or choice. When understood in this way, agency appears too specialised to serve as a foundational biological concept because many living systems clearly lack such capacities.

APS rejects this interpretation. Biological agency is not defined by intelligence, self-awareness, symbolic representation, or reflective thought. It is defined by the organisation of activity relative to viability. A system exhibits agency when its activity contributes to the maintenance of the conditions required for its continued persistence.

This understanding shifts attention away from mental states and toward organisational dynamics. Cells regulate internal conditions despite continual material turnover. Organisms repair damage, respond to perturbation, and modify behaviour in ways that preserve viability. Ecological systems maintain relationships that support persistence across broader scales of organisation. In each case, the relevant feature is not the presence of consciousness but the presence of viability-oriented activity.

Agency therefore should not be understood as a trait possessed to a greater or lesser degree in the way an organism might possess a particular morphological characteristic. Rather, it is an ongoing organisational accomplishment. Living systems remain living only because they continually engage in activities that preserve the conditions of their own continued existence.

This distinction is crucial because it prevents agency from becoming restricted to a narrow subset of biological phenomena. Once agency is understood as viability-oriented activity rather than psychological intention, it becomes possible to recognise a common organisational principle operating across the full diversity of life. What distinguishes living systems is therefore not the possession of a particular cognitive capacity but participation in the ongoing activity through which viability is maintained.

## Viability-Orientation and Biological Normativity

The defining feature of agency in APS is its orientation toward viability. Living systems do not interact with all states of the world equally. Some conditions support persistence, while others undermine it. Some trajectories preserve the organisation of the system, while others contribute to its degradation or destruction. Agency emerges because these distinctions matter to the continued existence of the system itself.

This viability-orientation introduces a normative dimension into biology. Normativity is often treated as something requiring minds, values, or external standards of evaluation. APS instead argues that biological normativity arises naturally from the organisation of living systems. Conditions are significant because they affect viability, and activities are significant because they contribute either positively or negatively to the maintenance of organised persistence.

The result is a naturalised account of biological evaluation. Living systems continually regulate activity relative to conditions that matter for their persistence. Repair processes respond to damage because damage threatens viability. Adaptive responses occur because changing conditions alter the requirements for continued persistence. Regulatory systems coordinate activity because organisational breakdown would undermine viability. Across all of these cases, the distinction between beneficial and harmful emerges from the viability-oriented organisation of the system itself.

Agency therefore provides the foundation for understanding biological normativity. The capacity of living systems to evaluate conditions relative to their own persistence is not an additional feature layered onto biological organisation. It is a direct consequence of the viability-oriented activity through which organised persistence is maintained.

## Agency Across Process

If agency is the defining activity of life, it cannot be understood as a momentary event or isolated behaviour. Living systems persist only because they continually regenerate the organisation upon which their continued existence depends. Agency is therefore inseparable from process. It unfolds through ongoing activity rather than residing in static structures or material components.

This processual character of life has become increasingly recognised across contemporary biology. Organisms are not stable objects that occasionally undergo change. They are dynamic organisations whose apparent stability is continuously produced through transformation. Materials flow through living systems, structures are repaired and renewed, developmental trajectories unfold, and interactions with the environment remain in constant motion. The persistence of a living system is therefore an achievement rather than a given condition.

Agency is the activity through which this achievement is realised. Metabolism maintains energetic organisation despite continual material turnover. Regulatory processes preserve internal coherence despite external variability. Repair mechanisms counteract degradation, while adaptive responses reorganise activity when existing arrangements become insufficient. Living systems therefore remain viable not because they resist change, but because they continually reorganise themselves through it.

Understanding agency in processual terms helps explain why APS places such emphasis on organised persistence. Persistence is not the absence of transformation. It is the maintenance of continuity through transformation. Agency is the activity that makes this possible. Through agency, living systems preserve organisational coherence despite operating in conditions of continual change.

This perspective also helps unify many apparently distinct biological phenomena. Development, adaptation, resilience, regulation, and homeostasis all involve different forms of continuity-preserving activity. Although they operate through different mechanisms and across different timescales, each contributes to the processual maintenance of viability. Agency therefore provides a common explanatory framework for understanding how living systems remain organised despite continual transformation.

## Agency Across Scale

The processual character of agency is inseparable from its operation across scale. Living systems are not organised at a single level, nor can agency be confined to one privileged domain of biological activity. The viability-oriented processes through which persistence is maintained occur across a nested and interacting architecture of biological organisation extending from cellular processes to ecological and evolutionary systems.

At cellular scales, agency is expressed through the regulation of metabolism, the maintenance of boundaries, the repair of damage, and the coordination of internal activity. At organismal scales, agency becomes visible through behaviour, physiology, development, and adaptive reorganisation. At ecological scales, organisms participate in relationships that contribute to the persistence of broader systems of interaction. Across evolutionary timescales, agency shapes the adaptive activities through which lineages remain viable under changing historical conditions.

APS does not treat these scales as independent explanatory domains. Activities occurring at one scale influence possibilities at others. Cellular regulation affects organismal viability. Developmental organisation influences ecological interaction. Ecological conditions shape evolutionary trajectories. Evolutionary transformations alter the capacities available to future organisms. Agency therefore operates within an interconnected architecture rather than a hierarchy of isolated levels.

Understanding agency across scale also helps explain why APS rejects attempts to define life through a single mechanism or privileged organisational domain. No single process is sufficient to explain the persistence of living systems because viability is maintained through coordinated activity distributed across scales. Agency names this distributed activity and provides a framework for understanding how continuity is sustained across the full architecture of biological organisation.

By integrating process and scale, APS moves beyond descriptions of life that focus exclusively on structure, information, reproduction, or evolution. These remain important biological phenomena, but they are better understood as participating in a broader architecture of viability-oriented agency. Agency therefore provides a unifying perspective capable of connecting diverse biological processes without reducing them to a common mechanism. By integrating viability, process, and scale within a single explanatory framework, it reveals how living continuity is sustained across the full architecture of biological organisation.

## Why Agency Is Not Behaviour

One of the most persistent misunderstandings surrounding agency is the assumption that agency is equivalent to behaviour. Because agency is often discussed in the context of animals, it is frequently associated with movement, decision-making, or observable responses to environmental stimuli. Although behaviour can be an important expression of agency, APS argues that agency is considerably broader than behaviour alone.

Many of the activities most essential to biological persistence occur independently of behaviour. Cells regulate internal chemistry without behaving in the conventional sense. Organisms repair tissue damage, coordinate developmental processes, maintain physiological stability, and regulate metabolism regardless of whether any overt behaviour is visible. These activities contribute directly to viability and therefore belong to the domain of agency as understood by APS.

The distinction becomes especially important when considering forms of life that display little or no obvious behavioural complexity. Plants, microbial communities, developing embryos, and many physiological systems engage in extensive viability-oriented activity despite lacking the kinds of behaviour often associated with agency. Restricting agency to behaviour would therefore exclude many of the processes most central to biological organisation.

APS instead treats behaviour as one possible manifestation of agency. Behaviour becomes significant because it contributes to viability-oriented organisation, not because it defines agency itself. Agency is the broader organisational activity through which living systems preserve the conditions of their continued persistence. Behaviour is one among many mechanisms through which that activity may be expressed.

## Why Agency Is Not Intelligence

A similar misunderstanding arises when agency is equated with intelligence. In both scientific and popular discourse, agency is often associated with problem-solving, planning, representation, or cognitive sophistication. These capacities undoubtedly involve agency, but they do not exhaust its meaning.

Intelligence represents a specialised form of viability-oriented activity that emerges in certain biological systems. Agency, by contrast, is present wherever living organisation actively contributes to the maintenance of its own viability. Long before organisms evolved nervous systems, perception, learning, or symbolic representation, living systems were already regulating internal conditions, adapting to changing environments, repairing damage, and sustaining organised persistence.

Recognising this distinction allows APS to place cognition within a broader biological framework. Cognitive capacities are not the origin of agency. Rather, they represent evolutionary developments within an already agential world. Intelligence extends and enriches the ways in which living systems can sustain viability, but the underlying organisational principle remains the same.

This perspective also helps clarify why APS rejects definitions of life based upon information processing, representation, or cognition. Such capacities are biologically important, yet they presuppose more fundamental forms of viability-oriented activity. Agency therefore precedes intelligence conceptually, developmentally, and evolutionarily.

Understanding intelligence as a specialised expression of agency rather than its defining feature allows APS to preserve continuity across the biological world. The same organisational principle that supports cellular regulation and physiological maintenance also provides the foundation upon which cognition and intelligence eventually emerge.

## Agency, Organisation, and Autonomy

The concept of agency intersects closely with contemporary discussions of biological autonomy. Both perspectives reject views of organisms as passive assemblies of components whose behaviour can be explained entirely through external causes. Both emphasise the active maintenance of organisation and the distinctive character of living systems as self-sustaining entities. APS therefore shares important common ground with autonomy-based approaches while developing a different explanatory emphasis.

Autonomy theories have typically focused on the organisational conditions that allow living systems to maintain themselves through networks of mutually supporting constraints. APS incorporates these insights but places greater emphasis on the activity through which such organisation is continually sustained. Organisational structures do not maintain themselves automatically. They persist only because living systems continuously regulate, repair, adapt, and reorganise the conditions upon which their viability depends.

This distinction is subtle but important. Autonomy identifies a characteristic organisational achievement of living systems. Agency identifies the ongoing activity through which that achievement is maintained. APS therefore treats autonomy not as an alternative to agency but as one of the organisational consequences of viability-oriented activity. Living systems are autonomous because they are agential, not vice versa.

Understanding the relationship in this way helps clarify why APS places agency at the centre of biological explanation. Organisational closure, self-maintenance, resilience, adaptation, and persistence all depend upon continuing activity directed toward viability. Agency therefore provides the dynamic foundation upon which autonomous organisation is built and sustained.

## Agency and the Definition of Life

Questions concerning the definition of life have long generated disagreement because living systems exhibit remarkable diversity while sharing no single mechanism, structure, or material composition. Definitions based on metabolism encounter cases that metabolise only intermittently. Definitions based on reproduction exclude sterile individuals. Definitions based on evolution struggle to classify individual organisms. Definitions based on information, complexity, or self-organisation often capture important features of life without identifying what makes living systems distinctive as living systems.

APS approaches this problem from a different direction. Instead of beginning with a list of characteristics, it begins with the activity through which living systems persist. The defining feature of life is not a particular substance, component, or mechanism, but the viability-oriented agency through which organised persistence is maintained across changing conditions.

This approach does not imply that metabolism, reproduction, development, evolution, information, or self-organisation are unimportant. On the contrary, these phenomena become intelligible precisely because they participate in the maintenance of viability-oriented organisation. They are important not as independent defining criteria but as expressions of the broader activity through which living systems sustain themselves.

Agency therefore provides APS with a unifying explanatory foundation. It allows diverse biological phenomena to be understood as different manifestations of a common organisational principle while avoiding the search for a single privileged mechanism that defines life in every circumstance.

For this reason, APS treats agency not merely as a useful biological concept but as the defining activity of life itself. Living systems are distinguished by what they do: they actively sustain the conditions of their own continued persistence. This shift from traits to activity not only reframes the definition of life but also provides the basis for investigating life empirically.

## Borderline Cases and Diagnosis

The significance of agency becomes especially clear when considering systems that occupy uncertain positions at the boundaries of life. Viruses, protocells, synthetic biological systems, artificial life models, and other borderline entities frequently challenge traditional definitions because they exhibit some characteristics associated with life while lacking others. As a result, debates often become focused on whether a particular checklist criterion has been satisfied.

APS reframes these questions by directing attention toward viability-oriented activity. Rather than asking whether a system possesses a particular trait, APS asks whether the system actively contributes to the maintenance of the conditions required for its own continued persistence. This shift does not eliminate ambiguity, but it provides a clearer explanatory framework for evaluating it.

Agency therefore plays an important diagnostic role. Evidence for agency may appear through regulatory activity, adaptive reorganisation, boundary maintenance, repair processes, resilience under perturbation, organism–environment coupling, or other forms of viability-oriented organisation. Conversely, systems that merely exhibit complex behaviour without contributing to their own persistence may resemble living systems superficially while lacking the organisational characteristics associated with biological agency.

This diagnostic perspective connects conceptual questions about life directly to empirical investigation. If agency is the defining activity of life, then the challenge is not merely to define life conceptually but to identify the biosignatures, perturbation responses, organisational dynamics, and continuity-preserving processes through which agency becomes observable.

## Agency as the Foundation of APS

Agency occupies a foundational position within APS because it provides the conceptual centre from which the broader framework unfolds. The framework does not begin with agency because agency explains everything. Rather, it begins with agency because agency identifies the distinctive activity through which living systems remain living. Once this activity is recognised, many apparently separate biological questions become aspects of a common explanatory project.

The concept of viability explains what agency is oriented toward. The concept of process explains how agency unfolds through time. The concept of scale explains how agency is distributed across biological organisation. Organised persistence emerges from the interaction of these dimensions, providing the central explanatory theme that unifies the framework.

From this foundation, the major domains of APS become easier to understand. Evolution concerns the historical transformation of viability-oriented agency. Development concerns the organisation of agency across life histories. Ecology concerns the organisation of agency across interacting systems and environments. Cognition concerns specialised forms of evaluative agency. Diagnosis concerns the identification and investigation of agency through empirical evidence.

Agency therefore does not sit alongside these domains as one topic among many. It provides the organising principle through which they become connected. APS begins with agency because agency supplies the common explanatory thread linking the diverse phenomena studied throughout biology.

## Conclusion

The central claim of APS is that life is best understood through the activity by which living systems sustain themselves. Organisms are not distinguished primarily by particular structures, molecules, mechanisms, or traits. They are distinguished by their capacity to engage in viability-oriented activity that preserves the conditions of their own continued persistence. Agency names this activity.

Understanding agency in this way requires moving beyond familiar associations with behaviour, intelligence, or conscious choice. Biological agency is more fundamental. It is present wherever living systems regulate internal conditions, repair damage, adapt to changing circumstances, maintain organisational coherence, and reorganise themselves in ways that preserve viability. These activities differ enormously across biological contexts, yet together they reveal a common organisational principle at work throughout life.

APS therefore treats agency as the defining activity of life because agency integrates viability, process, and scale within a single explanatory framework. Viability explains why agency matters. Process explains how agency is continuously enacted. Scale explains how agency is distributed across the architecture of biological organisation. Together these dimensions explain how organised persistence remains possible despite continual transformation.

Seen from this perspective, life is neither a static condition nor a collection of defining traits. It is an ongoing organisational achievement. Living systems persist because they actively sustain the conditions required for their own continued existence. Agency is the name APS gives to that achievement, and it is for this reason that agency stands at the foundation of the framework as the defining activity of life.
