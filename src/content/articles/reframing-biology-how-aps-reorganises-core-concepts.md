## Introduction

Biology possesses an extraordinary collection of explanatory concepts. Life, function, adaptation, evolution, development, cognition, individuality, and purpose are among the most powerful ideas in science. Yet these concepts are often interpreted through different theoretical traditions, supported by different explanatory assumptions, and applied at different scales of investigation. As a result, biology frequently appears conceptually fragmented. Evolutionary explanations may emphasise historical selection, developmental explanations may focus on construction and regulation, physiological explanations may prioritise mechanisms, and cognitive explanations may invoke information, representation, or evaluation. Although these approaches often describe the same living systems, they do not always share a common explanatory framework.

The Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework begins from the premise that this fragmentation is neither inevitable nor desirable. Rather than treating biological concepts as largely independent theoretical tools, APS seeks to understand them as interconnected expressions of a common organisational reality. The central claim of APS is that living systems are distinguished by their capacity to sustain themselves through ongoing viability-oriented activity. Life is not merely a collection of traits, a chemical composition, or a category of objects. It is an organised process through which systems actively maintain the conditions of their own continued existence.

From this perspective, agency is not a specialised feature possessed only by cognitively sophisticated organisms. It is the defining activity of life itself because organisms persist only through continuous regulation, reorganisation, and coordination of processes that preserve viability across changing circumstances. Once biological organisation is understood through the lens of viability-oriented activity, many of biology's foundational concepts acquire new relationships to one another. Function becomes contribution to persistence, purpose becomes immanent to living organisation, evolution becomes the historical transformation of organised persistence, development becomes the construction and maintenance of organised persistence, and cognition becomes a specialised form of evaluative organisation grounded in viability. APS therefore does not seek to replace biology's core concepts. It seeks to reorganise them within a unified explanatory grammar grounded in agency, process, scale, and viability-oriented organisation.

## Why Biology Needs Reframing

### The Problem of Conceptual Fragmentation

Modern biology is remarkably successful in generating knowledge, but empirical success does not automatically produce conceptual integration. Many of biology's central concepts emerged within different historical contexts and were developed to answer different explanatory questions. Natural selection arose within evolutionary theory, function became associated with adaptation and selected effects, cognition developed largely within psychological and neuroscientific traditions, development was often treated separately from evolution, and ecology focused on organism–environment relations at broader scales. Each tradition generated powerful explanatory tools, but each also developed its own assumptions regarding causation, organisation, and explanation.

The result is a discipline containing multiple explanatory vocabularies that often coexist without full integration. Mechanistic explanations emphasise component interactions, evolutionary explanations emphasise historical selection, developmental explanations emphasise construction and regulation, ecological explanations emphasise environmental relations, and cognitive explanations emphasise information and evaluation. Although each perspective captures important aspects of living systems, the relationships among them are not always clear. Debates concerning reductionism and emergence, genes and organisms, mechanism and teleology, development and evolution, or cognition and behaviour frequently arise because biological phenomena are being interpreted through partially incompatible explanatory frameworks.

APS approaches this situation from a different direction. Rather than asking which explanatory tradition should dominate, it asks whether these traditions can be understood as addressing different aspects of a common organisational phenomenon. The goal is not to eliminate existing biological concepts but to situate them within a framework capable of explaining why they remain connected despite their apparent diversity.

### APS as an Organisational Framework

APS proposes that biological explanation should begin not with isolated components, traits, or categories, but with the organised activity through which living systems maintain themselves. This organisational perspective is structured through three complementary dimensions. Agency concerns the viability-oriented activity through which living systems sustain themselves. Process concerns the ongoing transformations through which living organisation is realised. Scale concerns the spatial, temporal, and organisational relations through which biological processes become coordinated.

These dimensions are not independent variables that can be studied in isolation. Agency is always realised through processes, processes always occur across scales, and scales become biologically significant through their contribution to viability-oriented activity. Together they provide a framework for understanding living systems as organised processes of persistence rather than collections of static components. The consequence is a shift in explanatory emphasis. Instead of asking primarily what biological systems are made of, APS asks how biological systems maintain themselves through time. Instead of treating organisation as a secondary outcome of lower-level processes, APS treats organisation itself as the primary object of biological explanation.

This shift does not reject mechanistic investigation, evolutionary theory, developmental biology, ecology, or cognitive science. Rather, it provides a common framework within which these domains can be understood as examining different aspects of the same underlying organisational reality. APS therefore functions not as a competing biological theory but as an explanatory architecture capable of integrating existing biological knowledge.

## The Foundational Shift

### From Objects to Activities

One of the deepest changes introduced by APS concerns the way biological systems are conceptualised. Traditional biological descriptions often begin with entities. Organisms, cells, genes, organs, and traits are treated as the primary objects of investigation, while biological explanation seeks to determine how these entities interact and what properties they possess. APS does not deny the importance of biological structures, but it argues that structures alone cannot explain why living systems remain living.

A cell remains a cell only because it continuously performs the activities required for its own maintenance. An organism remains an organism only because it continually regulates and reorganises itself in ways that preserve viability. The persistence of living systems therefore depends not merely on what they are, but on what they do. Biological organisation is not something added to otherwise complete structures. It is the ongoing activity through which those structures are generated, maintained, repaired, and coordinated.

This perspective shifts explanatory attention from static objects to organised activities. Life is not fundamentally a thing but an ongoing accomplishment. The central explanatory question therefore changes from asking what properties define living systems to asking what forms of organised activity allow living systems to sustain themselves through time. This shift provides the foundation for the APS reinterpretation of biology's major concepts because it relocates explanation from classification toward organisation and persistence.

### From Components to Viability-Oriented Organisation

A similar shift occurs in the understanding of causation and organisation. Traditional explanations often focus on individual components such as genes, proteins, cells, organs, and traits. APS recognises the importance of these components but argues that their biological significance derives from the organisation within which they participate. Genes matter because they contribute to organised persistence. Cells matter because they contribute to organised persistence. Organs matter because they contribute to organised persistence. Their explanatory importance is therefore inseparable from the viability-oriented organisation that gives them biological meaning.

This perspective places viability at the centre of biological explanation. Living systems face continual challenges to their continued existence. They must acquire resources, regulate internal conditions, respond to perturbations, and maintain organisational coherence despite ongoing change. Biological organisation exists because these activities are successfully coordinated. APS describes this organisation as constraint-closed and viability-oriented because living systems generate and maintain networks of mutually supporting constraints that contribute to their own persistence.

From this perspective, biology becomes the study of organised persistence: the ways in which living systems maintain themselves, transform themselves, and extend themselves across time. The consequences of this shift become clearer when the major concepts of biology are reconsidered through the APS framework.

## Reframing Core Biological Concepts

The reorganisation proposed by APS can be summarised as a systematic shift in how biology's central concepts are understood. Traditional formulations often emerged within specialised domains and therefore reflected the explanatory priorities of those domains. APS does not reject these concepts, but it relocates them within a common organisational framework centred on viability-oriented activity and organised persistence.

| Classical Concept | Conventional Framing | APS Reformulation | Explanatory Consequence |
|------------------|--------------------|------------------|--------------------------|
| **Life** | Trait lists or chemical criteria | Constraint-closed organisation sustaining viability | Life becomes operationally diagnosable |
| **Causation** | Linear, component-level | Reciprocal and recursive across scales | Restores multi-scale biological causation |
| **Organisation** | Discrete hierarchical levels | Scale-coupled processual organisation | Replaces hierarchy with continuous interacting scales |

| **Individuality** | Genetic or morphological unity | Constraint-closed organisational coherence | Recognises graded individuality |
| **Function** | Selected effect | Contribution to viability | Function becomes present-tense and normative |

| **Normativity** | Observer-imposed value | Intrinsic viability conditions | Grounds biological success and failure |
| **Prediction** | Model-based inference | Prospective regulation | Unifies anticipatory organisation across life |

The significance of these reformulations lies not in any individual definition but in the relationships among them. Each concept becomes intelligible through its contribution to the maintenance, transformation, or extension of organised persistence. APS therefore replaces a collection of partially independent biological categories with an integrated explanatory system in which concepts derive meaning through their role within living organisation.

## Life as Activity Rather Than Category

### Agency as the Defining Activity of Life

Traditional attempts to define life have often focused on identifying a set of characteristic properties. Lists of traits such as metabolism, reproduction, growth, responsiveness, or evolution have been proposed as criteria for distinguishing living from non-living systems. While such approaches capture important features of biological systems, they frequently encounter difficulties because no single trait appears both necessary and sufficient across all cases.

APS approaches the problem differently. Rather than beginning with the question of which properties living things possess, it begins with the question of what living systems do. The defining characteristic of life is not the possession of a particular structure or substance but the performance of a distinctive form of activity. Living systems actively sustain the conditions of their own continued existence through organised, viability-oriented processes.

Agency therefore occupies a foundational position within APS. Agency is not understood as conscious deliberation, symbolic reasoning, or complex cognition. Nor is it treated as an optional feature possessed only by certain organisms. Instead, agency refers to the capacity of a system to organise and regulate activity in ways that contribute to its own persistence. In this sense, agency is not an additional property layered onto life. It is the defining activity through which life exists.

This perspective transforms the explanatory role of agency. Rather than asking how agency emerges from life, APS asks how life is realised through agency. The distinction is subtle but profound. Living systems do not first exist and then become agents. They exist because they continuously perform the viability-oriented activities that constitute agency.

### Organised Persistence

The concept of organised persistence provides a unifying framework for understanding this relationship. Living systems persist despite continual material turnover, environmental fluctuation, developmental transformation, and evolutionary change. Their continuity therefore cannot be explained by the preservation of fixed structures alone. Instead, continuity depends upon the maintenance of organisational relations capable of sustaining viability across time.

Organised persistence captures this phenomenon. It refers to the ongoing maintenance of viability-oriented organisation despite continuous change in the material processes through which that organisation is realised. A living system remains the same kind of system not because its components remain unchanged, but because the organisation responsible for sustaining viability continues to be maintained.

This perspective provides a common explanatory foundation for phenomena that are often treated separately. Physiological regulation, developmental stability, behavioural flexibility, ecological interaction, and evolutionary continuity all become different manifestations of organised persistence operating across different temporal and organisational scales. The concept therefore functions as one of the principal integrative ideas within APS, linking agency, process, and scale within a single explanatory framework.

## Function, Purpose, and Normativity

### Why Functions Matter

Function has long occupied a central position in biological explanation, yet it has often proven difficult to define. Selected-effect accounts explain functions through evolutionary history, while causal-role accounts explain functions through present contributions to system behaviour. Each approach captures important aspects of biological organisation, but each also faces limitations when applied across the full diversity of living systems.

APS approaches function through viability. A biological function is understood as a contribution to the maintenance of organised persistence. Components, processes, and behaviours possess functions because they participate in sustaining the viability of the systems within which they occur. Their significance therefore derives not merely from historical selection or mechanistic participation but from their role within an ongoing organisational process.

This perspective retains the insights of evolutionary and mechanistic accounts while situating them within a broader organisational framework. Historical selection helps explain why certain functions evolved. Mechanistic analysis helps explain how those functions are realised. APS explains why those functions matter biologically in the first place: they contribute to the maintenance of viability-oriented organisation.

### Why Purpose Is Not Eliminated

Purpose has often been treated with suspicion within biology because of its historical association with external design or metaphysical teleology. As a result, biological discourse frequently attempts to eliminate purposive language or reinterpret it as shorthand for mechanistic or evolutionary processes.

APS takes a different approach. Living systems genuinely exhibit goal-directed behaviour, but the source of this directedness is not external design. It emerges from the organisational requirements of viability itself. Organisms must acquire resources, regulate internal conditions, repair damage, avoid threats, and coordinate activity in ways that preserve persistence. These requirements generate forms of directed activity that are intrinsic to living organisation.

Purpose therefore becomes immanent rather than external. Organisms act in ways that are directed toward the maintenance of viability because viability is the condition of their continued existence. Teleological language retains explanatory value because it describes real organisational features of living systems rather than merely reflecting observer convenience.

### Why Normativity Is Real

The same logic applies to biological normativity. Within APS, success and failure are not imposed by external observers. They emerge from the viability conditions of living systems themselves. Some states support persistence, while others undermine it. Some behaviours enhance organisational coherence, while others contribute to breakdown.

Normativity therefore arises from the distinction between what supports viability and what threatens it. A heart that fails to circulate blood is malfunctioning not because an observer judges it negatively, but because its activity no longer contributes to the persistence of the organism. Similarly, behaviours, developmental processes, physiological responses, and ecological interactions can be evaluated according to their consequences for viability.

APS thus grounds biological normativity within living organisation itself. Function, purpose, and normativity become interconnected aspects of a single organisational reality rather than separate conceptual problems requiring independent explanations.

## Evolution, Development, and Ecology

### Three Perspectives on Organised Persistence

Evolution, development, and ecology are often treated as distinct biological disciplines concerned with different phenomena and operating at different scales. APS does not deny these differences, but it proposes that all three domains can be understood as investigating different dimensions of organised persistence.

Evolution concerns the historical transformation of organised persistence across generations. It examines how viability-oriented organisation changes, diversifies, and stabilises through time. Development concerns the construction, maintenance, and reorganisation of organised persistence within individual life histories. It investigates how viable organisation emerges, stabilises, and transforms during the existence of particular organisms. Ecology concerns the environmental embedding of organised persistence. It examines how living systems sustain themselves through interactions with the wider networks of organisms, resources, and conditions upon which viability depends.

Although these domains differ in emphasis, they are united by a common explanatory concern. Each investigates the conditions under which organised persistence is maintained, modified, or disrupted. APS therefore interprets them not as isolated branches of biology but as complementary perspectives on the same organisational phenomenon.

### Beyond Isolated Domains

This perspective helps dissolve several long-standing conceptual divisions. Development and evolution are no longer viewed as fundamentally separate processes connected only through occasional interactions. Development becomes one of the mechanisms through which organised persistence is realised, while evolution becomes the historical transformation of those organisational forms. Ecology similarly ceases to function as a merely external context and instead becomes an integral dimension of the conditions through which viability is sustained.

The result is a more integrated understanding of biological organisation. Evolution, development, and ecology remain distinct fields of inquiry, but they become connected through a common explanatory grammar centred on viability-oriented organisation and organised persistence. APS therefore provides a framework capable of explaining not only individual biological phenomena but also the relationships among biology's major domains of investigation.

## Cognition, Behaviour, and Evaluation

### Cognition Beyond Brains

Cognition is frequently associated with nervous systems, representation, information processing, or conscious awareness. While these phenomena are important forms of cognition, APS argues that they do not exhaust the biological significance of cognitive activity. Restricting cognition to neural systems risks overlooking the broader organisational processes through which living systems evaluate conditions, regulate behaviour, and maintain viability.

APS approaches cognition through the concept of evaluation. Living systems continually encounter circumstances that matter to their continued existence. Nutrient availability, environmental change, physiological disturbance, developmental challenges, and ecological opportunities all possess significance because they affect viability. To persist, organisms must respond to these conditions in ways that preserve or restore organisational integrity.

Cognition, in this broad biological sense, refers to the evaluative organisation through which living systems detect, discriminate, and respond to conditions relevant to viability. More sophisticated forms of cognition build upon this foundation, but the foundation itself is already present wherever living systems regulate activity according to conditions that matter for persistence. Cognition therefore becomes continuous with the broader organisational logic of life rather than an isolated phenomenon restricted to particular organisms.

### Meaning, Information, and Representation

This perspective also clarifies several concepts that have often generated confusion within biology. Information, meaning, and representation are frequently treated as independent explanatory categories, sometimes imported from communication theory, computer science, or philosophy of mind. APS instead grounds these concepts in the organisational requirements of living systems.

Information becomes biologically significant when it contributes to viability-oriented regulation. Meaning arises from the consequences that states, signals, or conditions have for organised persistence. Representation, where it exists, functions as a specialised organisational achievement that supports the regulation of viability-relevant activity. These concepts therefore derive explanatory significance from their contribution to living organisation rather than constituting independent foundations of biological explanation.

APS does not deny the importance of information, meaning, or representation. It situates them within a broader framework in which evaluation, viability, and organised persistence remain explanatorily primary. In this way, cognition becomes integrated with the rest of biology rather than standing apart as a fundamentally different domain.

## Diagnosis and Evidence

### Reframing Biological Investigation

APS does not merely reorganise biological concepts. It also changes how biological systems are investigated. If living systems are defined by viability-oriented organisation, then biological inquiry must develop methods capable of identifying, evaluating, and characterising such organisation in empirical settings.

Traditional approaches to life detection and biological classification often rely upon lists of characteristic features. Metabolism, reproduction, growth, complexity, or particular chemical signatures may be treated as indicators of life. While such criteria can be useful, they often function as indirect proxies for the underlying organisational processes that distinguish living systems.

APS redirects attention toward the organisation itself. The central diagnostic question becomes whether a system exhibits the forms of viability-oriented activity characteristic of organised persistence. Diagnosis therefore shifts from identifying isolated traits to investigating organisational capacities.

### From Biosignatures to Perturbation

This shift has important methodological consequences. Observable biosignatures remain valuable because they can provide evidence of biological organisation. However, APS argues that stronger evidence often emerges when systems are examined under perturbation. Perturbation reveals how a system responds when the conditions supporting viability are altered, challenged, or disrupted.

Living systems characteristically exhibit forms of reorganisation directed toward preserving organisational integrity. They regulate, compensate, adapt, and modify behaviour in ways that maintain viability despite disturbance. These responses provide evidence not merely of complexity but of viability-oriented organisation itself.

For this reason, APS places particular emphasis on diagnostic approaches that investigate persistence, resilience, recovery, and adaptive reorganisation. Viability gradients, perturbation responses, and organisational stability become important indicators because they reveal the underlying processes through which living systems sustain themselves.

The result is a more operational approach to biological investigation. APS seeks not merely to define life conceptually but to make biological organisation empirically tractable. In doing so, it strengthens the connection between theoretical explanation and practical diagnosis.

## Biology After APS

### From Fragmented Concepts to Organised Explanation

The cumulative effect of these reformulations is not the replacement of biology's traditional concepts but their integration within a common explanatory framework. Life, agency, function, purpose, normativity, development, evolution, ecology, cognition, and diagnosis cease to appear as largely independent topics requiring separate conceptual foundations. Instead, they become interconnected aspects of viability-oriented organisation.

This reorganisation alters the explanatory priorities of biology. Rather than beginning with traits, structures, or categories and asking how they interact, APS begins with organised persistence and asks how biological systems maintain themselves through time. Traits become significant because they contribute to viability. Functions become significant because they contribute to viability. Cognitive processes become significant because they contribute to viability. Across domains, explanatory importance derives from participation in the maintenance and transformation of organised persistence.

APS therefore shifts attention away from explanatory fragmentation and toward organisational integration. Biological phenomena remain diverse, but they become intelligible through their relationship to a common organisational reality. The result is a framework capable of linking domains that are often treated separately while preserving the distinctive insights of each.

### An Explanatory Grammar of Life

The significance of APS ultimately lies not in any individual concept but in the explanatory grammar that emerges from their integration. Agency, process, scale, viability, constraint closure, and organised persistence form a mutually supporting conceptual system through which biological phenomena can be understood within a common framework.

This explanatory grammar does not compete with empirical biology. It provides a way of organising biological knowledge so that concepts developed within different traditions become intelligible as parts of a larger whole. Evolutionary explanations, developmental explanations, ecological explanations, physiological explanations, cognitive explanations, and diagnostic investigations all remain valuable. APS clarifies how they relate to one another by identifying the organisational principles that underlie them.

The broader architecture of this explanatory framework is developed in [The Explanatory Geometry of Biology](/articles/explanatory-geometry-of-biology/). There, the relationships among agency, process, and scale are examined directly as dimensions of biological explanation. The present article focuses on the consequences of that architecture for biology's central concepts. Together, these perspectives show how APS functions not merely as a theory of life but as a framework for reorganising biological explanation itself.

## Conclusion

APS invites a shift in how biology is understood. Rather than treating core concepts as isolated, historically contingent, or theoretically disconnected, it interprets them as coordinated expressions of viability-oriented organisation. Life, agency, function, purpose, normativity, evolution, development, ecology, cognition, and diagnosis become intelligible as interconnected aspects of organised persistence.

This shift does not discard existing biological knowledge. APS preserves the empirical achievements of contemporary biology while providing a more integrated conceptual framework within which those achievements can be understood. Mechanisms remain important, but they are situated within organisation. Evolution remains essential, but it is interpreted as the historical transformation of organised persistence. Cognition remains distinctive, but it is grounded in the evaluative organisation characteristic of life itself.

The result is not a new collection of biological concepts but a new way of understanding their relationships. APS reorganises biology by placing viability-oriented organisation at the centre of explanation and by treating agency as the defining activity through which living systems sustain themselves across time and scale.

## Key Point

APS reorganises biology by treating agency as the defining activity of life and viability-oriented organisation as the primary object of explanation, integrating life, function, evolution, development, ecology, cognition, and diagnosis within a unified framework of organised persistence.
