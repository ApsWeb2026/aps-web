## Introduction

Reductionism has earned its place in modern biology through genuine explanatory success. Molecular analysis, mechanistic decomposition, biochemical investigation, genetics, physiology, and increasingly sophisticated forms of modelling have transformed biological understanding. They have revealed mechanisms that could not have been discovered by treating organisms as undifferentiated wholes, and they remain indispensable to contemporary biological science.

APS has no reason to diminish those achievements. It accepts material realism and the explanatory power of decomposition. The philosophical problem begins only when success in identifying material components and mechanisms is taken to establish a more general rule about explanatory sufficiency. To say that a living system is wholly realised through physical and chemical processes is a claim about material constitution. It does not yet determine which relations must be represented to explain a particular biological phenomenon.

This distinction matters because different biological questions can require different explanatory resources. A relatively local mechanistic account may be entirely adequate for one explanandum, while another may depend on organisational relations that become difficult to identify when components are considered separately. The issue is therefore not whether biology should choose mechanism or organisation. It is whether the explanation captures the materially realised relations that make a difference to the target.

Where *Analysis, Synthesis, and the Direction of Explanation* examines how different explanatory strategies can be related, the present clarification asks a narrower question: can material constitution itself establish that every biological explanandum is exhaustively reducible to component-level description? APS argues that it cannot. But neither does APS infer the converse—that an organisational account must always improve upon a mechanistic one. Whether it does so must be established relative to what is being explained and to what the relevant comparator already explains.

[[box:analysis-vs-synthesis-two-directions-of-explanation]]

APS therefore rejects neither mechanism nor decomposition. Its alternative is organisational in a more restricted sense. Within APS, **life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. Components of living systems can accordingly be investigated both through their material properties and through the relations in which they participate within living organisation. Which of those relations must enter an explanation remains a question for the explanandum rather than something fixed in advance by the framework.

## The Reductionist Picture

Reductionism is often associated with the expectation that biological systems can ultimately be explained through their constituent parts and the physical principles governing them. In its stronger forms, explanatory priority is assigned to smaller or supposedly more fundamental components, while the organisation of larger systems is expected to follow from sufficiently complete knowledge of their interactions. The familiar language of lower and higher levels, together with bottom-up explanation, expresses this picture.

There is an important truth within it. Living systems are materially constituted by physical and chemical processes, and biological phenomena cannot escape the constraints imposed by that material constitution. Mechanistic biology has consequently achieved extraordinary explanatory success in fields ranging from molecular interaction and gene regulation to metabolism and physiology. APS preserves that achievement without qualification.

What does not follow from it is the stronger proposition that material composition alone determines explanatory sufficiency. Knowing what a system is made of and identifying mechanisms operating within it may answer the question being asked. But the fact that an explanation is materially detailed does not guarantee that it captures every organisational dependency relevant to every possible biological target.

This is why the APS clarification is directed at explanatory reduction rather than at mechanistic science itself. The framework remains materially realist while resisting the inference that decomposition must always be explanatorily exhaustive.

[[box:realization-is-organisational-not-merely-functional]]

## Ontological Dependence and Explanatory Priority

The distinction between ontological dependence and explanatory priority helps make this position precise. Living systems depend completely upon physical and chemical processes. Nothing in APS requires an additional substance, force, or non-material causal domain. But the material dependence of a biological phenomenon does not determine what must become central in its explanation.

Explanatory priority is question-relative. A molecular interaction may properly occupy the centre of one explanation, physiological regulation another, and an organism–environment relation a third. The relevant priority follows from the explanandum, available evidence, and the relations required to explain it. Explanatory success at any of these points does not by itself establish a corresponding hierarchy in biological reality.

[[box:explanatory-priority-is-not-ontological-priority]]

APS makes living organisation and organised persistence explicit explanatory targets, but it does not thereby assign them universal explanatory priority. Their importance arises when the phenomenon being explained depends upon relations involved in the maintenance, re-establishment, or transformation of living organisation.

This can be seen across familiar biological concepts. Repair concerns what happens when damaged organisation is re-established. Resilience concerns responses to perturbation and changing conditions. Inheritance involves materially realised relations contributing to continuity across generations, while evolutionary explanation concerns historical transformation across those generations. Function, too, may require attention to the contribution an activity makes within a living organisation. In such cases, organisational relations can become central because of the explanatory target, not because APS has declared organisation universally prior.

The same discipline applies to genes, enzymes, membranes, regulatory systems, and signalling pathways. APS does not deny them or subordinate their mechanistic explanation. A gene can be studied as a molecular sequence, through the mechanisms governing its expression, or through its contribution to development, regulation, inheritance, repair, or other biological processes. Which of these relations matters depends on what is being explained.

The useful distinction is therefore not between a merely material description and a uniquely biological realm. It is between questions about material constitution and questions about which materially realised relations are required for explanatory adequacy. This also helps explain why comparable biological capacities can sometimes be realised through differing material implementations without implying unrestricted substrate neutrality.

## Mechanism and Explanatory Sufficiency

Mechanistic explanation can be extraordinarily powerful. It can reveal how molecular interactions occur, how pathways regulate activity, how components interact, and how particular physiological operations proceed. For appropriately specified explananda, such explanations may be sufficient.

APS therefore asks a simple but consequential question:

> **Powerful for explaining what?**

The question does not presume that the mechanistic answer will be incomplete. It asks instead whether the mechanism, as specified, captures what the explanandum requires. A mechanistic account may already include the relevant components, activities, interactions, and organisation. Where it does, APS has no methodological reason to demand a broader reconstruction merely because the explanation is mechanistic or relatively local.

Other targets may require additional organisational relations. Regulation can depend on constraints maintained through continuing activity. Repair can involve relations through which damaged organisation is re-established. Compensation may depend on interactions not made salient by examining a single component in isolation. Environmental conditions can alter the possibilities available to local activity. These are materially realised relations, not alternatives to mechanism.

Their inclusion must therefore be earned by the biological case. If they make an explanatory difference to the target, they belong in the explanation. If the mechanistic account already captures what the target requires, organisational redescription alone does not establish additional explanatory gain.

## Continuity, Perturbation, and Explanatory Visibility

The organisation of living systems is often easiest to overlook when functioning remains relatively stable. Components and mechanisms can then be experimentally isolated and characterised without making all of the dependencies involved in continued organisation equally visible. Perturbation provides one way of bringing some of those dependencies into view.

A change in conditions may be followed by compensation, repair, reorganisation, partial failure, recovery, or loss of viability. Such contrasts can reveal which activities, constraints, and relations make a difference to continued functioning. They can also expose the limits within which regulation succeeds and the conditions under which organisation cannot be re-established.

This gives perturbation an important diagnostic role within APS, but not a privileged one. Disruption does not create the organisational dependencies it reveals, and perturbation alone does not establish an APS explanation. Its value lies in making particular relations empirically tractable so that their contribution to the explanandum can be assessed.

[[box:perturbation-reveals-organisation]]

## Beyond Top-Down and Bottom-Up

Discussions of reductionism often move from questions about components and organisation to questions about bottom-up and top-down causation. The vocabulary is understandable. Molecular events can alter physiological activity, organismal states can change the conditions under which molecular processes occur, and organisms both modify and respond to their environments. Describing these relations as influences between levels can provide useful shorthand.

The shorthand becomes misleading when the descriptive partitions are treated as independently acting causal domains. A physiological condition does not act upon a molecular process because it occupies a “higher level,” just as a molecular event does not acquire explanatory priority merely because it is classified as “lower.” The causal work is performed through materially realised processes, constraints, interactions, boundaries, and conditions.

APS does not solve this difficulty by replacing hierarchical levels with interacting scales. Where organisation extending beyond a local boundary alters local activity, the explanatory task is to identify the material relations through which that alteration occurs. A wider spatial or temporal extent does not itself cause the local event. Conversely, a local event can alter more extensive organisation through particular causal relations without causal influence needing to be redescribed as movement upward through a hierarchy.

Scale enters at a different point in the analysis. **Scale is the analytic projection concerned with where persistence is organised across spatial and temporal extents.** It asks how far, spatially and temporally, the organisation relevant to the explanandum must be followed. It does not cause, coordinate, constrain, couple, or integrate biological activity.

This distinction leaves the required extent genuinely open. Some explanations may be adequately bounded around a relatively local mechanism. Others may require relations extending through physiological organisation, development, environmental conditions, or historical processes. What warrants the broader extent is not the existence of another scale but the explanatory difference made by the materially realised relations found there.

[[box:scale-is-not-hierarchy]]

## What APS Preserves

The result is not an argument against mechanistic biology. APS preserves molecular investigation, decomposition, mechanistic explanation, and the universal material constraints imposed by physics and chemistry. What it resists is the conversion of their scientific success into a general philosophical claim that every biological explanandum must be exhausted by decomposition.

Nor does APS reverse the privilege. Organisational description is not automatically superior because it is broader, more integrative, or expressed in APS terminology. An organisational account must identify relations relevant to the specified target and show what explanatory work they perform. Where the relevant mechanistic account already captures those relations, no additional explanatory gain follows merely from redescribing them as organised persistence.

This point extends to informational, computational, systems, and network approaches. Each can provide powerful explanations for appropriately specified targets. Where APS addresses the same target, differences in terminology, scope, or explanatory architecture are insufficient to establish superiority. Comparative explanatory gain requires a matched explanandum and a relevant comparator.

The APS position is therefore neither reductive mechanism nor anti-mechanistic holism. It is a methodological demand for explanatory adequacy: identify the materially realised organisation relevant to the question, and determine whether making that organisation explicit explains something the relevant alternative leaves unresolved.

## Organised Persistence as Biological Intelligibility

Organised persistence gives APS a characteristic explanatory focus without functioning as a universal answer. Living systems are not static collections of molecules, genes, pathways, or mechanisms to which persistence is subsequently added. Their material constituents and relations change while organisation may be maintained, re-established, or transformed.

Within APS, **life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. Organised persistence identifies the continuity of that materially realised living organisation despite change. It therefore draws attention to biological questions in which regulation, repair, resilience, perturbation, and viability become relevant to explanation.

This does not turn organised persistence into an additional causal entity. Regulation is performed through material processes and constraints. Repair depends on activities through which damaged organisation can be re-established. Environmental interactions alter material conditions. The explanatory value of organised persistence lies in making these relations available as a coherent target of investigation, not in acting upon them from outside the organisation they constitute.

The same qualification applies to biological normativity. APS does not derive normativity from an abstract principle of persistence. Normativity concerns viability-relative asymmetries within living organisation: some states, changes, and conditions differ in their consequences for continued organisation. Those differences are materially realised and empirically investigable.

Decomposition therefore neither succeeds nor fails in the abstract. It may provide everything required for one explanandum and leave relevant organisational dependencies unresolved for another. The APS question is whether identifying those dependencies adds explanatory content to what the relevant comparator already provides.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

**When does material and mechanistic decomposition adequately explain a biological phenomenon, and when do materially realised organisational relations add explanatory content that the decomposed account does not provide?**

### Architectural Role

This Clarification article distinguishes **material constitution from explanatory sufficiency**. It preserves mechanistic and decompositional explanation while rejecting the inference that material dependence entails universal explanatory reduction.

The clarification is symmetrical. APS does not make the converse inference that organisational description, broader explanatory extent, or APS terminology automatically establishes greater explanatory adequacy. The relevant question is whether the additional relations make an explanatory difference to the specified target.

### Position within APS

APS is not an anti-reductionist ontology opposed to mechanistic biology. It is a comparative explanatory methodology that asks what organisation is relevant to a specified biological explanandum and whether making that organisation explicit produces explanatory gain relative to the relevant comparator.

Within that methodology, Agency, Process, and Scale remain analytic projections of one viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation rather than independent components or causal domains. This article gives particular attention to Scale because disputes about reductionism are often expressed through levels, bottom-up and top-down causation, and explanatory extent. Scale does not replace those levels with another ontology. It identifies the spatial and temporal extent required by the explanandum.

## Conclusion

Reductionism identifies something indispensable about biology: living systems are materially constituted, and understanding their components and mechanisms is fundamental to biological science. APS accepts that material grounding completely.

What material constitution does not establish is a universal rule of explanatory sufficiency. A mechanistic account may fully explain an appropriately specified biological target. Another target may require organisational relations that are not made explanatorily salient by decomposition alone. The difference cannot be decided merely by labelling one explanation mechanistic and another organisational.

APS therefore makes viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and organised persistence explicit explanatory targets without granting them universal explanatory or ontological priority. The framework asks which materially realised relations matter to the phenomenon being explained and whether making those relations explicit adds to what the relevant comparator already explains.

Scale has an equally bounded role. It identifies the spatial and temporal extent across which the persistence relevant to the explanandum must be considered. The extent itself performs no causal work, and a broader explanation is not better merely because it is broader.

The resulting position preserves the achievements of mechanistic biology while resisting both explanatory reduction by assumption and organisational superiority by assumption. What matters is explanatory adequacy for the biological target.

## Key Point

**Material constitution does not determine explanatory sufficiency. Mechanistic explanation may be sufficient for one biological explanandum, while another may require additional organisational relations. APS asks which materially realised relations make an explanatory difference to the target and whether including them adds to what the relevant comparator already explains.**
