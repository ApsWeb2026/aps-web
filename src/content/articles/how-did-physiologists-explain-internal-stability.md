**Where This Article Fits**

*This History of Ideas article follows experimental physiology as it developed ways of explaining how living beings maintain workable internal conditions. It gives [What Is Science?](/articles/what-is-science/) a concrete companion on measurement and intervention, and leads into APS discussions of biological organisation and individuality.*

## Introduction

Take a blood sample before breakfast, another after eating, and several more over the next few hours. The resulting curve might show glucose rising and then falling towards its earlier level. The curve tells us that the amount of glucose in a given volume of blood changed. It gives us much less information about where the glucose came from, where it went, or what brought its concentration down.

Think of a bath with the tap running and the plug out. The water level can stay the same while water enters and leaves. If it falls, the tap might have been turned down, the drain might have cleared, or both might have happened. Blood glucose poses a similar problem of inference, with the additional complication that some of the processes supplying and using glucose can respond to conditions in the body. How did physiologists learn to investigate those responses? The story begins with a finding about where sugar comes from.

## Bernard’s liver

Claude Bernard’s investigations in the mid-nineteenth century helped establish that animals could produce sugar within their bodies. One experiment made the idea particularly vivid. Bernard washed an excised liver until sugar could no longer be detected in the washings. After the organ had stood for a time, sugar could again be recovered from it. The historical record distinguishes the report of this result in 1855 from Bernard’s subsequent isolation of the sugar-forming material in 1857. That material became known as glycogen (Anonymous 1857; Young 1957).

The force of the experiment lies in the sequence. Washing took away the sugar that could initially be recovered. Waiting made it possible to recover more. The return of detectable sugar showed that the tissue could generate it without a fresh supply from digestion. The experiment left open how much glucose a liver releases in an intact animal, under what conditions, and with what consequences for other organs. Those questions concern the relations among processes that the experiment had temporarily taken apart.

Bernard’s broader idea of a *milieu intérieur*, or internal environment, gave those relations a place in physiology. The cells of an animal are exposed to conditions in the fluids around them. An animal can encounter considerable change outside its body while the conditions under which its tissues work vary within a more limited range. That relative stability becomes something to explain. How do the activities of the tissues help maintain the environment on which their own activities depend? (Cooper 2008).

## Breathing and the discovery of a response

Breathing offers another way to see what an experiment can establish. We breathe faster and more deeply during exertion. That observation gives us several possibilities. The movements themselves might alter breathing, or breathing might respond to chemical changes associated with the work. An experiment that changes the composition of inspired air allows part of the problem to be investigated apart from exercise.

In 1905, J. S. Haldane and J. G. Priestley used samples of alveolar air, from the lungs’ gas-exchanging spaces, to study the regulation of breathing. Adding carbon dioxide to inspired air allowed them to examine the breathing response apart from exercise. A relatively small rise in alveolar carbon dioxide could accompany a large rise in ventilation. Measuring the two together made it possible to relate the chemical change to the size of the response (Haldane and Priestley 1905).

This episode brings out a difficulty that recurs in regulatory explanations. An increased rate of breathing is readily observable. The condition that the response helps stabilise is harder to identify. A person can breathe much more during exertion while the concentration of carbon dioxide in the relevant compartments changes comparatively little. The increase in ventilation can help explain that relative stability. Knowing what to measure, and which conditions to alter, changes what we can learn from a familiar bodily response.

Haldane’s work and Lawrence Henderson’s investigations of acid–base balance belong to the history between Bernard and Walter Cannon. They give that history substance. Physiologists were learning to investigate how breathing and the chemistry of bodily fluids were connected, as well as developing a broader idea of internal stability (Cooper 2008).

## Cannon gives homeostasis a name

In his 1929 article “Organization for Physiological Homeostasis”, Cannon brought a range of physiological findings together under a common question. How do the coordinated activities of the body maintain conditions that would otherwise be disturbed? *Homeostasis* gave physiologists a way to speak about the relative steadiness produced by those activities. The processes could be complex and distributed across several organs (Cannon 1929).

The idea changes how we read a graph. A nearly level line can conceal considerable activity. A body losing heat on a cold day may change its rate of heat production and alter heat loss. A person who has gone several hours without eating can still supply tissues with glucose. The quantity measured and the activity that helps maintain it have different trajectories. The physiological work can increase while the value we are watching changes very little.

Homeostasis also gives us a reason to take departures seriously. A rise or fall may be part of the normal course of events. A meal supplies glucose, and the resulting change in concentration helps alter the activity of tissues that release, take up, or store it. If we want to know how a system responds to a disturbance, we need measurements that reveal both the change and the activity it elicits.

## Feedback and the glucose clamp

Negative feedback gives a precise form to one part of this reasoning. If a change elicits a response whose effects oppose the change, the response can limit the deviation. A thermostat offers a familiar illustration. A room cools, the heating comes on, and the resulting warmth counteracts the fall in temperature. The analogy invites us to ask what is sensed, what changes in response, and how that response affects the original condition.

Those questions are useful in physiology, where their answers may be distributed across several tissues. After a meal, rising glucose can stimulate insulin secretion. Insulin in turn promotes glucose uptake in certain tissues and restrains glucose output from the liver. The observed trajectory also depends on the arrival of glucose from the gut and on other signals and processes. A glucose curve by itself leaves several of those contributions unresolved (Röder et al. 2016; Bich, Mossio, and Soto 2020).

A particularly revealing experiment borrows the principle of feedback and puts it to work in the apparatus. In 1979, Ralph DeFronzo, Jordan Tobin, and Reubin Andres described two forms of the glucose clamp. In one, investigators held glucose above its starting concentration and followed the insulin response. In the other, they raised insulin and adjusted the rate of glucose infusion to hold glucose near its starting concentration. The rate at which glucose had to be supplied under those conditions provided a measure of insulin sensitivity. At steady state, the infusion rate measures tissue glucose uptake when endogenous glucose production is suppressed. If the body continues releasing glucose, that supply also contributes to the balance (DeFronzo, Tobin, and Andres 1979).

The experimental arrangement makes a distinction that the breakfast curve obscures. A constant glucose measurement during the clamp can require a substantial infusion. The record of what the investigator had to supply reveals something that the concentration alone conceals. The experiment also shows why the location of control matters to an explanation. Part of the regulation in this example is supplied by the investigators and their apparatus. The intervention makes an aspect of the body’s own response measurable.

## When adjustment comes before disturbance

Feedback is easiest to recognise when we begin with a deviation. Yet physiological adjustments can also begin in advance of the demand they help meet. Consider what happens when someone prepares to run. A rise in heart rate before the first stride raises a question about the sources of that adjustment. A full explanation may need to consider what the body is preparing to do alongside the changes that occur once the muscles are working.

The idea of *allostasis* has been used to bring such anticipatory adjustments and changing demands into focus. Sterling’s account gives prediction a prominent role. Ramsay and Woods examine how far these ideas overlap with a broader understanding of homeostasis. The difference matters to an experimenter. To show anticipation, we need evidence about timing and about the cues or processes that elicit the response. To show that regulation operates around a different range, we need evidence about how the system responds to disturbances under the new conditions. A change in the average reading can prompt that investigation (Sterling 2012; Ramsay and Woods 2014).

## A leaf in drying air

A leaf has a version of the problem. The pores on its surface admit carbon dioxide and provide a route for water vapour to escape. When the air becomes drier, the pores may close. The change can limit water loss and also affect carbon uptake. What would show how the closure was brought about?

A change in the hydration of leaf tissues can affect the mechanics of the cells around a pore. Biochemical signalling can also contribute. Research by McAdam and Brodribb compared responses to drying air across vascular plants and examined the involvement of abscisic acid. The comparison makes the general question more precise. How much of a response can be accounted for by changes in hydration, and what further evidence points to a contribution from chemical signalling in the plants studied? (McAdam and Brodribb 2015).

The comparison with animals helps us see how easily a familiar outcome can suggest a familiar mechanism. A leaf that loses less water and an animal that limits a rise in blood glucose both give us reason to investigate regulation. In each case we have to establish which processes produce the response, how they are connected, and what conditions their activity helps sustain.

## Regulation and the persistence of an individual

Return to the glucose curve. An account of feedback can show how a deviation is limited. The organism also has to sustain the tissues, circulation, and signalling processes on which that response depends. Bich, Mossio, and Soto (2020) use glucose regulation to develop an organisational account that asks how the structures and processes involved depend on one another. Their account connects the correction of a deviation with the longer-term problem of maintaining the capacity to correct it.

That connection matters for biological individuality. The coordination of several organs can help explain how a living whole persists through change. We still need to establish whose continued viability the activity supports and at what scale. A response can sustain a cell, contribute to the function of a tissue, or support regulation across the organism. The presence of feedback establishes little by itself about where the individual’s boundaries lie. [Development and Biological Individuality](/articles/development-and-biological-individuality/) pursues the question over developmental timescales.

APS uses this organisational question to connect physiological regulation with biological individuality. It asks whose viability the response supports, at what scale, and how the system maintains the capacity to respond. A claim of constraint closure requires evidence that the relevant constraints depend on one another for their maintenance. [Biological Organisation](/articles/biological-organisation/) develops that argument. The breakfast curve now gives us several questions we can tell apart. What changed? What processes limited the change? And how does a living individual sustain the capacity to do that again tomorrow?
