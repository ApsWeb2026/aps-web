**Where This Article Fits**

*This Core article distinguishes biological systems in the broad sense from systems that are themselves alive. It connects the APS [definition of life](/articles/what-is-life-a-biological-question-revisited/) with the accounts of [biological agency](/articles/biological-agency-as-the-activity-of-self-maintenance/), [constraint closure](/articles/constraint-closure-what-it-does-and-does-not-do/), and [organised persistence](/articles/persistence-organised-continuity-through-time/). A bacterial cell provides a worked example of how these concepts guide the explanation of a specific physiological response.*

## Introduction

A heart, a metabolic network, a forest ecosystem, and a bacterium can all be studied as biological systems. In each case, investigators identify interacting components or processes and examine how their relationships give rise to the phenomenon of interest. That phenomenon might be the circulation of blood, the transformation of nutrients, the dynamics of a food web, or the continued functioning of a cell. The boundaries of the system matter because they determine which interactions the explanation must account for.

The bacterium also raises a more specific question. It is itself a living system, capable of maintaining the conditions on which its continued existence depends. A metabolic network can contribute to that maintenance, and an ecosystem can supply conditions that support it. The question of whether either has the organisation required to count as a living system in its own right calls for evidence about the proposed system as a whole.

The term *system* applies just as readily to a thermostat or a flame. A thermostat regulates temperature by feedback, while a flame can sustain a recognisable pattern as long as fuel and suitable conditions are available. Each illustrates a feature also found in living systems. The question pursued here is how the activities of a living system help maintain the organisation that makes those activities possible.

## From Mechanisms to Living Organisation

Mechanistic explanations identify the components, activities, and relationships that produce a phenomenon (Craver, 2007). In a bacterial cell, a mechanistic account can explain how a membrane protein transports a solute and how the resulting change in solute concentration affects water movement. The same investigation can ask how that change supports the cell’s continued functioning. The two questions have different targets and can be answered together.

APS makes the second question explicit. To explain a transport mechanism’s contribution to the cell, we need to know what the cell depends on, how a disturbance changes those conditions, and what the transport activity does under those circumstances. A process that supports continued functioning under one set of conditions may have a different effect under another. Its contribution therefore has to be established for the particular system and situation.

This distinction also clarifies the use of *robustness*. A system can be robust with respect to a particular property if it maintains that property under specified disturbances (Kitano, 2004). To assess what this means for viability, one must establish how maintaining that property affects the conditions the system needs to continue. An individual process may remain robust while other conditions required by the cell deteriorate.

## Life, Constraint Closure, and Viability

APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation** (Spencer, 2026). Constraint closure concerns the reciprocal dependence of constraints for their maintenance. Viability concerns the conditions under which the organisation of a particular living system can continue.

A constraint shapes what can occur in a process. A bacterial membrane, for example, constrains exchange between the cell and its surroundings. The membrane and its transport proteins help maintain the internal conditions in which metabolic reactions can proceed. Cellular metabolism in turn supplies materials and energy used to produce and renew membrane components. These dependencies run through multiple processes. To establish closure, one must trace how the processes shaped by constraints contribute to maintaining those constraints, directly or through other parts of the organisation (Montévil and Mossio, 2015; Moreno and Mossio, 2015).

The cell’s dependence on its surroundings is part of that account. The cell requires exchanges of matter and energy, and its organisation helps sustain the capacity to carry them out. The closure claim concerns the maintenance relations among constraints, while the conditions that support those relations can extend beyond the cell.

Viability adds a question about how changes affect the system’s ability to continue. The same change can be supportive, undermining, or restorative depending on the system’s organisation, its prior state, and its surroundings. *Organised persistence* is the continuity of that organisation through change. A cell can still persist while an ongoing disturbance degrades the conditions required for its continuation. This distinction allows us to describe deterioration and recovery within the lifetime of the same cell.

## A Bacterial Cell Under Osmotic Disturbance

Consider a bacterial cell exposed to a sudden increase in the concentration of dissolved substances outside it. Water can leave the cell, reducing turgor and increasing the concentration of material in the cytoplasm. In bacteria capable of compensating, uptake or synthesis of suitable solutes can help restore cellular hydration and turgor. The physiological details depend on the organism and the disturbance (Wood, 2011).

The cell may persist during the initial loss of water even as conditions for continued functioning worsen. The regulatory response can then help restore some of those conditions. Whether it succeeds depends on the disturbance and on which capacities the cell retains. The example thus gives *viability* and *organised persistence* different explanatory roles. One concerns the conditions being undermined or restored, while the other concerns the continuity of the cell over the course of the disturbance and response.

Osmoregulation also shows how to investigate the relation between a mechanism and the organisation that sustains it. Membrane transport and cellular metabolism help produce the response. The response in turn helps restore conditions on which cellular processes depend. This example identifies several relations relevant to closure. Establishing the wider closure of the cell would require tracing how those and other constraints are themselves maintained within the organisation.

## Biological Agency as Viability-Oriented Activity

APS calls the viability-oriented organisational activity of a living system **biological agency**. In the bacterial example, the activity is the cell’s contribution to recovering conditions for its own continued functioning. It consists in materially realised processes such as transport and synthesis, whose significance in this case comes from how they affect the cell’s conditions of viability.

Di Paolo (2005) develops the concept of *adaptivity* to explain how a system can regulate itself in relation to its conditions of viability. This provides a close point of comparison. The osmotic response illustrates the question both accounts address when a disturbance threatens continued functioning. APS uses *biological agency* for the wider organisational activity through which a living system maintains or restores the conditions of its persistence. The term thus includes routine maintenance as well as regulatory responses to disturbances.

Viability orientation describes the relation between activity and the conditions that the organisation depends on. The cell can carry out this activity without representing those conditions or anticipating an outcome. Regulation can also fail. Dormancy brings the temporal dimension into view. Some living systems persist through intervals of greatly reduced activity by retaining the organisation and capacity needed to resume active functioning under suitable conditions.

## Agency, Process, and Scale

Agency, Process, and Scale offer three ways to investigate the same cell through the same disturbance.

- **Agency** concerns what the cell does to maintain or restore conditions for continued functioning. In this case, the question is how solute uptake or synthesis contributes to recovery.
- **Process** concerns continuity through change. The cell persists as its water content, solute concentrations, and rates of activity change. This emphasis on maintained continuity through turnover and transformation connects APS with processual accounts of living systems (Dupré and Nicholson, 2018).
- **Scale** concerns the spatial and temporal extents over which the explanation must connect events. Molecular transport at the membrane alters conditions across the cell, and immediate water movement can be followed by regulatory changes over a longer interval. The surrounding medium matters because its relation to the cell shapes the disturbance and the possibilities for recovery.

Connecting these perspectives keeps the explanation tied to the physiology. Changes across the cell can alter the conditions under which particular molecular processes occur. Those processes can in turn change cellular conditions. The explanatory work lies in tracing these dependencies and showing how they bear on the continuity of the organisation.

## System Boundaries and Biological Individuality

The bacterial cell is a comparatively clear case in which a physiological system can be investigated as a living individual. Other cases require closer attention to what is being individuated. Pradeu (2016) distinguishes physiological individuality from evolutionary individuality. The first concerns the integration of a system in physiological terms. The second concerns the entity identified for an evolutionary explanation. The boundaries relevant to those questions can differ.

The distinction matters when the system under study is an organ, a symbiotic association, or an ecosystem. Each can be investigated through the relationships among its constituents. The APS question is whether the proposed whole maintains the conditions of its own continuing organisation through viability-oriented, constraint-closed activity. Answering it requires evidence about how the relevant maintenance relations are organised and which system they sustain. The result can depend on the particular case and the spatial and temporal boundaries of the inquiry.

## Conclusion

A biological system is a unit of investigation whose interacting parts or processes are studied together to explain a biological phenomenon. When the system is itself alive, APS asks how its activities maintain the conditions that allow its organisation to continue. Constraint closure identifies reciprocal maintenance relations among constraints. Viability identifies the conditions in relation to which changes can be supportive, undermining, or restorative. Biological agency concerns the activity through which living organisation helps sustain those conditions.

Bacterial osmoregulation makes these distinctions concrete. A disturbance can undermine conditions for continued functioning while the cell still persists. An explanation of recovery can then trace what the cell does, how its organisation continues through the disturbance, and how processes at the membrane and across the cell contribute over time. This is how the APS account connects a specific physiological mechanism to the wider organisation it helps sustain.
