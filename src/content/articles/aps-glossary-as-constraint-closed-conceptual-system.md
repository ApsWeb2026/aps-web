In most scientific contexts, a glossary is treated as an auxiliary tool: a list of definitions that clarifies terminology used elsewhere. It is assumed to be descriptive, expandable, and largely independent of the theoretical structure it supports. Within the APS framework, this assumption does not hold. APS is not a collection of loosely related concepts but a structured account of biological organisation. Its terms are not interchangeable labels but components of an interdependent conceptual system. As a result, the glossary cannot be treated as an open-ended list. Its size, composition, and internal relations are themselves theoretically significant. The APS glossary should therefore be understood as a constraint-closed conceptual system.

In APS, a living system is defined by [constraint closure](/glossary/constraint-closure/): a network of [constraints](/glossary/constraint/) that mutually sustain one another and thereby maintain the system’s [viability](/glossary/viability/). This idea extends to the conceptual domain. A set of concepts exhibits conceptual constraint closure when each concept is defined in relation to others within the set, no concept is fully intelligible in isolation, the system of concepts is sufficient to express the framework’s core claims, and the addition or removal of any concept alters the structure of the whole. On this view, the glossary is not merely a reference tool but the minimal conceptual structure required to articulate APS. Throughout, relations between terms are relations of conceptual articulation rather than temporal or causal sequence.

A constraint-closed conceptual system must satisfy two opposing conditions. It must be sufficient, containing enough concepts to define biological [organisation](/glossary/organisation/), articulate [normativity](/glossary/normativity/), distinguish living from non-living systems, and support explanation across [scales](/glossary/scale/). At the same time, it must be minimal, excluding redundant terms, loosely related vocabulary, and concepts that do not play a structural role. These conditions impose a strict discipline: if a concept can be removed without loss of explanatory capacity, it does not belong in the core glossary.

Within this framework, the glossary can be understood as organised into three functional groupings. The first comprises the core closure concepts, which form the irreducible backbone of APS and are mutually interdependent. The second comprises derived concepts, which depend on the core set while extending its explanatory reach. The third comprises interface concepts, which connect APS to other theoretical frameworks and disciplines and therefore introduce additional commitments.

The core closure set can now be stated explicitly. The Tier 1 glossary consists of eleven terms: [viability](/glossary/viability/), [organisation](/glossary/organisation/), [constraint](/glossary/constraint/), [constraint closure](/glossary/constraint-closure/), [process](/glossary/process/), [scale](/glossary/scale/), [biological agency](/glossary/biological-agency/), [normativity](/glossary/normativity/), [evaluation](/glossary/evaluation/), [function](/glossary/function/), and [persistence](/glossary/persistence/). These are not selected for convenience but because each satisfies strict inclusion criteria. Each term is indispensable to defining life in APS terms, each is mutually dependent with others in the set, each introduces a non-redundant conceptual distinction, and none can be derived from the others without loss of explanatory structure.

Taken together, these concepts define the system. Constraint, constraint closure, and organisation specify the structural constitution of the system. Viability and normativity specify the system-internal conditions under which its continuation is secured or threatened. Evaluation and biological agency specify the modulation and regulation of activity in relation to those conditions. Process and scale provide the ontological framing within which all of these must be understood, ensuring that the system is treated as temporally extended and inherently multiscale. Function and persistence specify the contribution of processes to viability and the continuation of the system through time. These relations are not additive but interdependent: organisation is constituted through constraint closure; viability is defined through organisation; normativity arises from viability; evaluation expresses normativity; agency realises evaluation; function contributes to viability; and persistence stabilises the system across time. Each term is required not only to define the system but to stabilise the meaning of the others, such that removal of any term alters the structure of the whole. No term stands alone, and no term is redundant.

The second grouping consists of derived concepts, including [semiosis](/glossary/semiosis/), [cognition](/glossary/cognition/), [counterfactual depth](/glossary/counterfactual-depth/), [adaptation](/glossary/adaptation/), [inheritance](/glossary/inheritance/), and [variation](/glossary/variation/). These extend the framework into specific explanatory domains but do not introduce new primitives. Each can be analysed in terms of the core set. Cognition can be understood as a structured and temporally extended form of evaluative activity; semiosis as the organisation of differences as mattering for viability; and adaptation as the transformation of viability conditions across generations. Their role is therefore to elaborate the framework, not to redefine it.

The third grouping consists of interface concepts such as information, representation, design, and [teleonomy](/glossary/teleonomy/). These terms are often historically loaded and theoretically contested. Within APS they require reinterpretation in terms of the core conceptual system. Because they connect APS to other frameworks, they introduce additional conceptual commitments and must therefore be treated cautiously. Their inclusion should be deferred until the core system is stable and clearly defined.

Maintaining a constraint-closed glossary requires active attention to conceptual integrity. Each term must be defined through other concepts in the system, must contribute to the explanation of at least one other concept, and must introduce a distinction not captured elsewhere. A concept that lacks dependency is isolated, one that lacks contribution is redundant, and one that lacks distinctiveness is duplicative. In each case, the appropriate response is removal, merging, or redefinition.

From this perspective, uncontrolled glossary expansion is not a sign of progress but a risk to coherence. Expanding the glossary to accommodate new topics or distinctions can dilute conceptual structure, increase ambiguity, and weaken explanatory clarity. The guiding principle must therefore be that the glossary grows only when the conceptual system itself requires it—when a domain cannot be expressed using existing concepts, when a genuine explanatory gap is identified, or when a new concept can be fully integrated into the existing structure.

When properly constrained, the glossary becomes more than a definitional resource. It functions as a map of the framework’s conceptual structure, a diagnostic tool for identifying inconsistencies, and a guide to explanatory priorities. In this respect, it mirrors the role of constraint closure in living systems: just as biological organisation is maintained through mutually sustaining constraints, conceptual organisation is maintained through mutually defining terms.

Treating the APS glossary as a constraint-closed conceptual system therefore imposes a discipline that is both restrictive and generative. It is restrictive in that not all useful or familiar concepts can be included and expansion must be carefully justified. It is generative in that it forces clarity, reveals hidden dependencies, and stabilises the framework as a whole. The result is a glossary that does not merely accompany the theory but constitutes its conceptual core. This structure also provides a criterion for evaluating biological explanation: accounts that cannot be expressed within this system, or that require terms external to it, remain incomplete with respect to the organisation of living systems.

Process and scale function as ontological framing conditions rather than system components.

## Colour Scheme and Conceptual Role

The colour scheme used in the diagram is not merely aesthetic but performs a substantive conceptual role. It functions as a secondary encoding layer that organises the framework into distinct modes of explanation, allowing the reader to grasp structural relations that would otherwise remain implicit. Rather than simply distinguishing individual elements, the colours map the system into functional domains, each corresponding to a different aspect of biological explanation within the APS framework. The relations indicated are relations of conceptual articulation rather than temporal or causal sequence.

The use of grey for process and scale signals their status as ontological framing conditions. These are not components of the system in the same sense as the other terms, but conditions under which the system can be described at all. The neutrality of grey reflects this role: it places these concepts in the background, indicating that they apply globally without participating directly in the flow of dependencies.

The structural constitution of the system—constraint, constraint closure, and organisation—is represented in blue. This marks the point at which the system becomes identifiable as a living organisation and emphasises that biological organisation is constituted through mutually sustaining constraints.

The relation between structure and value is encoded in amber, used for viability and normativity. This marks the inclusion of system-internal significance: the distinction between states that support or undermine persistence.

Green represents evaluation and biological agency, indicating the domain of activity in which normativity is enacted through modulation and regulation of behaviour.

Purple represents function and persistence, capturing temporal continuity and the contribution of processes to the maintenance of organisation through time.

Taken together, the colour scheme distinguishes structure, value, activity, and temporal continuity as interdependent domains of explanation. More precisely, it distinguishes what exists, what matters, what happens, and what persists, all within the framing conditions of process and scale. This organisation reflects the central claim of APS: that biological systems must be understood as integrated systems in which organisation, normativity, activity, and persistence are inseparably linked.
