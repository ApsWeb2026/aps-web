**Where This Article Fits**

*This article belongs to the APS Methodology and Explanation corpus. It provides a comparative introduction to five perspectives through which biologists investigate living systems: mechanistic explanation, evolutionary explanation, systems biology, organisational approaches, and the Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework.*

*It applies the descriptive methodology established in [Comparative Explanatory Methodology in Theoretical Biology](/articles/comparative-explanatory-methodology-theoretical-biology/), which compares research programmes through their explanatory targets, organising principles, priorities, conceptual organisation, characteristic strengths, and scope. That methodology distinguishes understanding explanatory architectures from evaluating particular claims of explanatory advance.*

*The present article extends this descriptive approach to a broader selection of biological perspectives, including established scientific fields and explanatory strategies that are not themselves organisational research programmes. It employs ten dimensions to make their commitments accessible across biological disciplines.*

*[The Explanatory Geometry of Biology](/articles/explanatory-geometry-of-biology/) provides the authoritative account of APS's own explanatory organisation. The present article does not redefine APS, establish its explanatory superiority, or demonstrate that it constitutes an independently established alternative to organisational biology. Rather, it locates APS within a plural landscape of biological explanation and identifies where its commitments overlap with, complement, or potentially differ from those of other approaches.*

*The comparison clarifies what each perspective claims without assuming that biology requires any one of them.*

## Introduction

Biological explanation is remarkably diverse. A molecular biologist may explain how a signalling pathway alters cellular activity, an evolutionary biologist may investigate how a trait arose or how its frequency is changing, a systems biologist may model the dynamics of interacting regulatory processes, and a philosopher of biology may ask what makes a collection of processes a living individual. These inquiries can concern the same organism without pursuing the same explanatory question.

The diversity is not merely a consequence of disciplinary specialisation. Biological phenomena possess histories, mechanisms, organisational relations, developmental trajectories, ecological dependencies, and functional consequences. Different explanatory practices make different aspects of these phenomena accessible to investigation. The existence of multiple perspectives therefore need not indicate a failure to discover one comprehensive biological theory.

Nevertheless, this plurality creates an intellectual problem. Explanatory approaches are often introduced through their distinctive vocabularies, methodological commitments, or preferred examples. Mechanistic accounts emphasise entities and activities; evolutionary explanations foreground evolutionary processes and historical relationships; systems biology investigates interactions and dynamics; organisational theories examine autonomy and interdependent constraints; and APS connects questions of viability-oriented activity, organised persistence, and spatial and temporal relationships.

These descriptions are informative, but they can also mislead. Mechanistic explanations are not indifferent to system dynamics, biological function, or environmental conditions. Evolutionary biology investigates contemporary causal processes as well as historical transformations. Systems biology routinely incorporates mechanistic knowledge and experimental intervention. Organisational approaches employ empirical, mathematical, and causal resources. APS depends upon established biological explanations rather than replacing them.

The resulting question is not simply which perspective is correct. It is how different perspectives should be understood in relation to one another.

This article compares five representative perspectives through a common descriptive architecture. The objective is to make their explanatory commitments explicit, identify their characteristic questions and methods, and distinguish complementarity from genuine disagreement.

The five were selected to illustrate different kinds of explanatory orientation rather than to identify the five most important theories in biology. Mechanistic explanation exemplifies a widely employed causal-explanatory strategy; evolutionary biology a broad historical and causal research field; systems biology an interdisciplinary modelling and experimental tradition; organisational approaches a family of theories concerning the constitution and maintenance of living systems; and APS a specific conceptual framework whose position within this landscape requires clarification.

Their differences in scope and intellectual status are therefore part of the comparison rather than a difficulty concealed by it.

The selection is deliberately limited. It does not exhaust biological explanation, and it does not imply that ecology, developmental biology, process philosophy, biosemiotics, enactivism, or other approaches are subordinate to those examined here. Other selections would illuminate different relationships.

The central claim is modest but important: biological perspectives can be compared systematically without being ranked, reduced to a common vocabulary, or treated as competing candidates for a single universal explanatory framework.

## Why Biological Explanation Is Plural

Biology investigates phenomena that differ substantially in causal organisation, historical development, spatial extent, temporal duration, and accessibility to experiment.

Explaining an enzyme reaction is not the same undertaking as explaining the evolutionary origin of an enzyme family. Understanding a developmental pathway differs from reconstructing the ecological conditions under which a developmental characteristic became advantageous. Explaining how an organism maintains physiological regulation differs from determining the criteria by which organisms should be distinguished from colonies, symbioses, or other biological associations.

These distinctions do not imply that biological phenomena occupy separate, self-contained domains. The enzyme may participate in developmental regulation, the developmental pathway may have an evolutionary history, and physiological activity may depend upon ecological circumstances. Rather, different questions select different explanatory relations from an interconnected biological reality.

Philosophers of biology have recognised the significance of explanatory pluralism. Mitchell (2003) argues for integrative pluralism in the study of biological complexity, while Kellert, Longino, and Waters (2006) examine the philosophical significance of multiple scientific approaches. Brigandt (2013a) emphasises the role of explanatory aims in determining whether different explanations compete or complement one another.

Such accounts do not establish that all biological explanations are equally successful. Scientific claims remain answerable to evidence, explanatory adequacy, and the phenomena under investigation. Pluralism concerns the legitimacy and relationships of different explanatory practices, not immunity from criticism.

The historical development of biology provides many examples. Darwinian evolutionary theory transformed explanations of adaptation and diversity without eliminating physiological or developmental inquiry. Molecular biology introduced powerful methods for investigating heredity and cellular mechanisms without making ecological or evolutionary explanations dispensable. Systems biology expanded the use of mathematical and computational models without abandoning experimental mechanism research.

Contemporary organisational theories investigate the constitution and maintenance of living systems while drawing upon established biological knowledge. They raise questions about autonomy, function, regulation, and biological individuality that overlap with, but are not identical to, the explanatory aims of molecular biology or evolutionary theory.

APS belongs within this plural intellectual landscape. It proposes a particular organisation of biological questions, not a replacement for the sciences from which its analyses draw.

Recognising explanatory plurality therefore provides the starting point for comparison. Before asking whether perspectives disagree, it is necessary to identify what each is attempting to explain.

## Can Different Biological Perspectives Be Compared?

### The Problem of Commensurability

Comparison ordinarily implies some shared basis upon which the objects being compared can be examined. When biological perspectives pursue different explanatory aims, however, there may be no single standard by which their contributions can be ranked.

A mechanistic explanation of stomatal closure and an evolutionary explanation of drought tolerance are not necessarily rival accounts. The former may identify processes producing a physiological response, while the latter investigates the evolutionary origins, distribution, or continuing selection of response characteristics.

Both may concern the same biological system, but they answer different questions.

This difficulty does not make comparison impossible. It requires distinguishing *descriptive comparison* from *competitive evaluation*. Perspectives can be compared according to the questions they ask, the concepts they employ, the evidence they use, and the relationships they investigate without assuming that their answers should be measured against a single criterion.

Brigandt's (2013a) analysis of explanatory aims is particularly relevant. Explanatory adequacy is partly determined by the scientific problem being addressed. Different explanations may be complementary when they contribute to different aspects of that problem, although they may compete when they make incompatible claims about the same explanandum.

The present comparison therefore begins by identifying explanatory targets before examining conceptual commitments.

### The Problem of Category Asymmetry

The five perspectives examined here are not equivalent intellectual objects.

Mechanistic explanation is a widely employed explanatory strategy. Evolutionary biology is a major scientific field encompassing several theoretical traditions. Systems biology is an interdisciplinary research enterprise involving mathematical, computational, and experimental practices. Organisational biology comprises related theoretical approaches to autonomy, constraint closure, function, and self-maintenance. APS is a particular conceptual framework developed to organise selected questions about living systems.

Treating these as five competing theories would obscure their differences in scope, historical development, institutional standing, and scientific application.

The comparison therefore uses *perspective* as a deliberately broad term. It identifies a characteristic orientation towards biological explanation without claiming that all five have the same theoretical status.

This choice has consequences. The mechanistic and evolutionary sections necessarily describe families of approaches rather than single doctrines. The organisational section focuses principally on biological autonomy and closure-of-constraints theories while acknowledging related traditions. APS, by contrast, can be reconstructed through its explicitly stated definitions and explanatory architecture.

The asymmetry is not eliminated. It is made visible so that readers can interpret the comparison appropriately.

### The Problem of Conceptual Translation

Biological perspectives often use different concepts to describe overlapping phenomena.

The language of self-maintenance, autonomy, regulation, viability, persistence, and biological organisation provides an obvious example. These terms can refer to closely related biological activities while carrying different theoretical commitments.

Organisational accounts of biological autonomy emphasise reciprocal dependencies among constraints involved in maintaining a living system (Moreno and Mossio 2015; Montévil and Mossio 2015). APS defines life through viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation and places organised persistence at the centre of a particular explanatory inquiry.

These formulations overlap substantially. Their differences cannot be established merely by comparing the words employed.

Conversely, identical terminology may conceal important distinctions. *Agency* may refer to intentional action, behavioural flexibility, organism–environment interaction, or viability-oriented organisational activity, depending upon the framework. Barandiaran, Di Paolo, and Rohde (2009) provide an influential account of agency involving individuality, normativity, and interactional asymmetry, while Moreno and Mossio (2015) distinguish constitutive biological autonomy from its interactive dimension.

A responsible comparison must reconstruct concepts within their theoretical contexts before attempting translation.

It must also recognise when translation remains incomplete. Different perspectives may organise similar empirical findings around different explanatory questions without yielding a simple one-to-one correspondence between their concepts.

### The Problem of Explanatory Pluralism

Scientific pluralism does not entail that every explanation is compatible with every other explanation.

Some perspectives address different aspects of the same phenomenon. Others offer alternative representations of overlapping causal processes. Still others make claims that cannot all be correct under the same conditions.

Disagreements may be conceptual, empirical, or both.

A conceptual disagreement can concern the criteria for attributing agency, function, or individuality. Two researchers may agree about a biological system's observed behaviour while disagreeing about whether that behaviour warrants a particular attribution.

An empirical disagreement instead concerns what occurs, which dependencies exist, or which proposed processes are causally relevant. Competing hypotheses about the mechanisms of stomatal regulation, for example, require evidence capable of discriminating between them.

Conceptual disputes may also have empirical consequences when competing definitions imply different classifications or expectations.

Such disagreements cannot be resolved merely by declaring that each perspective provides a different viewpoint. They require clarification of the claims at issue and appropriate forms of argument or evidence.

The comparison must therefore distinguish complementarity between explanations of different aspects, overlap between explanations that recover similar information, and incompatibility between claims that cannot jointly hold.

This preserves explanatory pluralism without treating it as unrestricted relativism.

### The Problem of Selection and Framing

No comparative architecture is entirely neutral.

Selecting five perspectives rather than ten determines which traditions receive attention. Choosing function, agency, and normativity as comparison dimensions foregrounds concerns important to organisational theories and APS. Selecting mathematical prediction, experimental intervention, or evolutionary reconstruction might reveal different contrasts.

Consequently, the ten dimensions used here should not be mistaken for an exhaustive or uniquely correct taxonomy of biological explanation.

They have been selected because they reveal explanatory targets, organising commitments, conceptual relations, methods, evidence, applications, and limitations. They are intended to make differences intelligible, not to measure theoretical worth.

The comparison also avoids interpreting absence as deficiency. A mechanistic account need not provide a general definition of life to explain a cellular process. An evolutionary model need not supply a theory of biological normativity to explain allele-frequency change.

A perspective's explanatory scope should be assessed in relation to its purposes rather than against the commitments of another perspective.

### Description, Evidence, and Explanatory Gain

Describing a perspective accurately does not establish that its claims are true. Demonstrating internal conceptual coherence does not establish empirical adequacy. Showing that a framework can organise existing explanations does not demonstrate that it produces additional explanatory gain.

These distinctions are particularly important for APS, whose conceptual architecture explicitly seeks to relate different explanatory domains.

The APS comparative methodology distinguishes descriptive mapping of explanatory architectures from evidential assessment of particular claims. Its strongest-comparator approach requires any claimed explanatory advance to be assessed against the strongest relevant established explanation.

The present article does not undertake that adjudicative task.

Its contribution is to clarify explanatory commitments and relationships. Where it identifies a possible difference, that difference remains subject to further philosophical or empirical examination.

## Five Perspectives on Biological Explanation

### 1. Mechanistic Explanation: How Biological Phenomena Are Produced

Mechanistic explanation occupies a central position in contemporary biology because many biological questions concern how particular phenomena are produced, maintained, or modified through organised causal activities.

Machamer, Darden, and Craver (2000) characterise mechanisms in terms of entities and activities organised so as to produce regular changes. This account gives explanatory importance not only to components but also to their activities and organisation.

The relevant entities may include molecules, cells, tissues, or other biological structures. Their activities may involve binding, transport, signalling, contraction, synthesis, degradation, or other processes. A mechanistic explanation identifies how these activities are organised to produce the phenomenon of interest.

Mechanistic inquiry often proceeds through decomposition, localisation, experimentation, and reconstruction. Bechtel and Richardson (1993) examine decomposition and localisation in scientific discovery, while Darden (2006) explores mechanistic reasoning, interfield relations, and anomaly resolution. Craver (2007) develops a philosophical account of mechanistic explanation in neuroscience.

Mechanistic explanation is not synonymous with explaining biological phenomena through isolated molecular parts. Organisation is integral to contemporary mechanistic accounts. Components may have different causal consequences depending upon their relations, conditions of operation, and participation in wider mechanisms.

Mechanisms may be dynamically organised, functionally characterised, and environmentally situated. Feedback, reciprocal influence, temporal coordination, and interactions across spatial extents can be essential to explaining their operation.

Nor does mechanistic inquiry require a fixed hierarchy of biological levels. Researchers may investigate molecular, cellular, physiological, behavioural, and environmental relations when these are relevant to the mechanism under investigation.

Mechanistic explanation is particularly powerful where experimentally accessible causal processes can be identified and their contributions assessed through intervention. It has been central to molecular biology, cell biology, physiology, developmental biology, and neuroscience.

Its scope nevertheless depends upon the explanandum. A mechanistic account of how a physiological response occurs does not necessarily explain the evolutionary history of that response. Equally, reconstructing a mechanism may not settle philosophical questions about organismal individuality or the grounds of biological function.

These are boundaries of particular explanatory tasks, not general deficiencies of mechanistic explanation.

Mechanistic explanation overlaps extensively with systems biology and organisational approaches. Mathematical models may describe mechanistic dynamics, while organisational accounts may investigate how mechanisms participate in the maintenance of living systems.

APS likewise depends upon mechanistic explanations where its questions concern materially implemented biological activities and dependencies.

The distinction is one of explanatory organisation rather than an opposition between mechanisms and biological wholes.

### 2. Evolutionary Explanation: How Biological Characteristics and Diversity Arise and Change

Evolutionary biology investigates the processes through which biological characteristics, populations, lineages, and patterns of diversity arise and change.

Darwin's account of descent with modification and natural selection transformed biological explanation by placing organisms within genealogical histories and explaining adaptation through differential reproductive success under heritable variation. Subsequent developments in population genetics, evolutionary theory, systematics, developmental biology, and ecology expanded the range of processes and evidence involved.

Evolutionary explanation cannot be reduced to natural selection alone.

Mutation, genetic drift, migration, recombination, inheritance, developmental constraints, ecological interactions, and historical contingency can all contribute to evolutionary outcomes. Their relevance depends upon the question under investigation.

Fisher (1930) developed foundational mathematical approaches to evolutionary change, while Dobzhansky (1937) helped integrate genetics with evolutionary biology. Mayr (1961) examined causal explanation in biology and drew influential distinctions between different kinds of biological questions, although subsequent discussion has qualified a strict separation of proximate and evolutionary causation.

Sober (1984) and Godfrey-Smith (2009) provide philosophical examinations of natural selection, evolutionary processes, and Darwinian populations.

The explanatory targets of evolutionary biology include adaptation, diversification, common ancestry, evolutionary transitions, trait distributions, and changes in populations and lineages through time.

Its evidential resources include population-genetic models, comparative analyses, phylogenetic reconstruction, fossil evidence, experimental evolution, ecological observation, genomic data, and developmental investigation.

Although evolutionary explanations frequently involve historical reconstruction, evolutionary biology also investigates ongoing, experimentally observable processes. Population-genetic models can generate prospective expectations, and experimental evolution can test causal hypotheses about adaptation and evolutionary change under controlled conditions.

Evolutionary biology therefore combines historical inquiry with contemporary causal and experimental research.

It also encompasses substantial debate concerning causation. Laland and colleagues (2011), for example, revisit the relationship between proximate and evolutionary causation, while niche-construction theory emphasises how organisms modify environmental conditions that can influence subsequent evolutionary processes (Odling-Smee, Laland, and Feldman 2003).

Such work complicates any representation of evolutionary biology as concerned only with selection acting upon passive organisms.

Evolutionary explanation differs from mechanistic explanation in characteristic emphasis, but the two are closely connected. The evolutionary history of a physiological trait may depend upon knowledge of its mechanisms, while mechanistic research may be informed by comparative and evolutionary evidence.

Evolutionary biology also engages directly with organism–environment relationships, developmental processes, functional interpretation, and biological individuality.

APS does not replace evolutionary explanation. Where organised persistence is the target, APS may ask how evolutionary histories and processes bear upon the maintenance or transformation of living organisation. Such an analysis remains dependent upon the historical and empirical resources of evolutionary biology.

### 3. Systems Biology: How Interacting Processes Generate Biological Dynamics

Systems biology investigates biological phenomena through interactions, dynamics, and organisation among multiple components and processes.

Although the term encompasses diverse research practices, systems biology is strongly associated with integrating experimental investigation, mathematical modelling, computational analysis, and quantitative data.

Kitano (2002a, 2002b) emphasises understanding biological systems through their structure and dynamics rather than through isolated components alone. Network organisation, feedback, robustness, and system behaviour become important explanatory concerns.

The relevant systems may include gene-regulatory networks, metabolic pathways, signalling networks, physiological regulatory systems, microbial communities, or other interacting biological processes.

Systems biology frequently investigates how particular behaviours arise from patterns of interaction. A regulatory system may display stability, oscillation, adaptation, sensitivity, or robustness depending upon the organisation and dynamics of its components.

Mathematical and computational models provide important resources for investigating these relationships.

Differential equations, stochastic models, network representations, simulations, and parameter estimation can explore the consequences of proposed interactions and generate experimentally assessable predictions.

Contemporary systems biology also includes data-driven network inference, statistical and computational integration of large biological datasets, mechanistic modelling, and investigations spanning multiple spatial and temporal extents. It should not be identified exclusively with network diagrams or with any single modelling technique.

These approaches do not displace experimentation. Model construction and evaluation commonly depend upon empirical measurements, perturbation experiments, and identification of causal mechanisms.

Kitano's (2004) discussion of biological robustness illustrates how systems biology investigates the capacity of biological systems to maintain particular functions under disturbances and changing conditions.

Systems biology also raises philosophical questions about explanation. Brigandt (2013b) examines how mechanistic and mathematical explanations can be integrated, while Brigandt, Green, and O'Malley (2017) further discuss the relationship between systems biology and mechanistic explanation.

The explanatory orientation of systems biology is therefore not simply holistic. It often depends upon detailed knowledge of components and their causal relations while investigating the dynamic consequences of their organisation.

Nor is systems biology necessarily committed to a single ontology of biological systems. Different research programmes may use systems concepts instrumentally, mechanistically, or as part of broader theoretical accounts.

This distinguishes systems biology from some organisational theories, which make stronger claims about the constitutive conditions of living organisation.

Systems biology may model how regulatory processes maintain physiological stability without thereby providing a general philosophical account of biological autonomy or normativity. Conversely, organisational accounts may draw upon systems models to investigate self-maintenance.

APS shares with systems biology an interest in organised relations and temporal change. Its particular conceptual emphasis lies in asking how viability-oriented activity contributes to organised persistence and how explanatory questions can be related through Agency, Process, and Scale.

Whether that conceptual organisation supplies information beyond a strong systems-biology explanation remains a separate question.

### 4. Organisational Approaches: How Living Systems Constitute and Maintain Themselves

Organisational approaches investigate living systems through the relations that constitute, maintain, regulate, and reproduce the conditions of biological organisation.

The term covers several related but distinct traditions. The present discussion focuses principally on biological autonomy and closure-of-constraints approaches, while recognising the relevance of autopoiesis, process philosophy, and other organisational theories.

Moreno and Mossio (2015) develop a philosophical and theoretical account of biological autonomy in which living systems are understood through the organisation of processes and constraints involved in their continued existence.

Montévil and Mossio (2015) formulate biological organisation through closure of constraints. Constraints influence processes while depending, directly or indirectly, upon other constrained processes for their maintenance.

The relevant explanatory interest is not merely that biological components interact. It concerns the organisation through which processes and constraints participate in the constitution and continued maintenance of a living system.

Closure of constraints is not simply a synonym for feedback. A feedback circuit may regulate a variable without establishing the network of constitutive dependencies characteristic of biological autonomy. Conversely, identifying a closed network of constraints requires specifying the processes, material conditions, and relationships involved.

This orientation has consequences for biological function and normativity.

Mossio, Saborido, and Moreno (2009) develop an organisational account of biological functions in which functional attribution is related to a component's contribution to maintaining an organised system that, in turn, supports the conditions of that component's existence.

Function is therefore not interpreted solely through evolutionary history or an observer's purposes. It is connected to the organisation and maintenance of the living system itself.

Organisational theories also investigate the basis of biological normativity. If the continued existence of a living system depends upon particular organisational conditions, processes may be assessed in relation to their contribution to, or disruption of, those conditions. This provides a proposed naturalistic basis for distinguishing beneficial, detrimental, and compensatory biological activities without assuming conscious intention.

These questions are closely related to viability, although organisational theories differ in how they specify autonomy, precariousness, regulation, and the conditions under which biological norms arise.

Biological regulation provides another important domain. Bich and colleagues (2016) examine how regulatory processes control aspects of living organisation from within the system, while Bich and Bechtel (2022) investigate integrated control in living organisms.

Organisational accounts also distinguish constitutive organisation from interaction with the surroundings. Moreno and Mossio (2015), for example, treat agency as an interactive dimension of biological autonomy rather than simply equating every constitutive activity with agency.

This distinction is especially important when comparing organisational theories with APS, which applies agency to viability-oriented organisational activity, including intrinsic maintenance.

The difference in terminology may affect the scope of agency attribution. Whether it also yields a substantive difference in explanation depends upon the claims made and the biological evidence available.

Organisational approaches are closely related to, but should not be equated with, autopoiesis.

Maturana and Varela (1980) develop an influential account of living systems through self-producing organisation. Later theories of biological autonomy and constraint closure address related questions while introducing different conceptual distinctions and explanatory commitments.

Process philosophy likewise emphasises temporally extended biological activity and the inadequacy of treating living systems as merely static collections of components (Nicholson and Dupré 2018).

These approaches share important concerns without forming a single unified doctrine.

Organisational theories overlap extensively with mechanistic and systems explanations. Their claims concern materially implemented biological processes, and their empirical interpretation depends upon mechanisms, dynamics, and conditions through which organisation is maintained.

However, their principal explanatory question differs from that of a local mechanistic account. They investigate what kind of organisation constitutes a living autonomous system and how that organisation grounds function, regulation, and normativity.

Organisational theories also face philosophical and empirical difficulties. Determining system boundaries, identifying constitutive constraints, specifying their temporal maintenance, and distinguishing living autonomy from other organised processes require careful argument and evidence.

The organisational approach is APS's closest comparator among the five perspectives considered here.

Both emphasise constraint closure, self-maintenance, function, viability-related conditions, and biological agency. The organisational literature already provides sophisticated accounts of many phenomena that APS discusses.

Their substantial conceptual overlap must therefore be acknowledged rather than obscured.

### 5. Agency–Process–Scale: How APS Organises Questions About Living Persistence

The Agency–Process–Scale framework proposes a conceptual organisation of biological inquiry centred on viability-oriented living organisation and its persistence through change.

APS defines life as **viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation**. Biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity, including internal maintenance and activity in relation to the surroundings where there is evidence of contribution to viability.

Organised persistence refers to the continuity of living organisation through change.

These definitions establish a particular conceptual orientation. They do not imply that living systems are intentional agents, that biological processes possess conscious purposes, or that all biological phenomena should be explained through cognition or information processing.

Within APS, organised persistence is a principal explanatory target rather than the compulsory target of biology generally.

Agency, Process, and Scale provide complementary, target-sensitive analytic projections of the same living organisation.

Agency concerns what living systems do as viability-oriented organisational activity. Process concerns how organisational continuity is maintained, transformed, or re-established through change. Scale concerns the spatial and temporal relationships relevant to the explanandum.

The three projections are not independent causes, ontological components, stages, or hierarchical levels. Nor must all three be invoked in every adequate biological explanation.

This structure is developed in Spencer (2026) and in the canonical APS article [The Explanatory Geometry of Biology](/articles/explanatory-geometry-of-biology/).

APS can use these projections to organise questions concerning regulation, repair, development, adaptation, ecological interaction, and other biological processes when their relationship to organised persistence is under investigation.

For example, physiological regulation may be considered as viability-oriented activity, developmental change as a process through which organisational continuity is transformed, and organism–environment relations as part of the spatial and temporal organisation relevant to persistence.

Such descriptions depend upon established biological explanations.

APS does not explain a signalling pathway merely by identifying it as agency, nor does it explain developmental transformation merely by describing it as organised persistence.

The framework's conceptual organisation can make selected questions and relationships explicit, but the adequacy of any resulting explanation depends upon evidence, material dependencies, and the particular explanandum.

The relationship between APS and organisational biology is especially close.

Both draw upon constraint closure and investigate biological self-maintenance, function, and normativity. APS places viability orientation explicitly within its definition of life and organises selected explanatory questions through Agency, Process, and Scale.

These differences in conceptual architecture can be described without assuming that they establish a new biological mechanism, a distinctive empirical prediction, or additional explanatory gain.

APS also overlaps with mechanistic, evolutionary, and systems approaches. Its analyses may draw upon mechanisms, historical reconstructions, mathematical models, and experimental evidence.

The framework therefore occupies a particular position within explanatory pluralism. It offers a vocabulary for relating selected biological questions while recognising the independent adequacy of explanations developed through other approaches.

Its internal conceptual coherence can be assessed through the consistency of its definitions and relationships. Its communicative usefulness can be investigated through independent comparison. Its explanatory contribution must be assessed against the strongest relevant established explanations.

These are different questions, and none should be answered by assuming the outcome of another.

## Ten Dimensions for Comparing Biological Perspectives

The preceding sections reconstruct the five perspectives through their characteristic explanatory commitments. A more systematic comparison requires a common descriptive structure.

The APS comparative methodology establishes seven analytical questions concerning explanatory target, organising principle, explanatory priorities, conceptual organisation, interpretation of biological organisation, domains of strength, and explanatory scope.

The present article retains these foundations while introducing three additional descriptive concerns useful for comparison across broad biological traditions: methods and evidence; function, normativity, and agency; and spatial, temporal, and organism–environment relationships.

The resulting ten dimensions are not a ranking instrument. They identify questions through which perspectives can be understood.

The inclusion of function, normativity, and agency reflects their importance to contemporary philosophical debates about biology, not a requirement that every perspective adopt these concepts or provide a general theory of them. Their absence from a particular explanation is not evidence of explanatory inadequacy.

### Interpreting the Comparison

The first important observation is that the perspectives do not identify the same explanatory target.

Mechanistic explanation commonly investigates how a specified phenomenon is produced. Evolutionary explanation addresses evolutionary processes and their historical and contemporary consequences. Systems biology investigates interacting dynamics. Organisational approaches examine the constitution and maintenance of living organisation. APS foregrounds organised persistence as a target within its own explanatory architecture.

These targets can intersect, but they are not interchangeable.

The second observation is that organisation is not exclusive to organisational biology or APS.

Mechanistic accounts depend upon organised entities and activities. Systems biology investigates organised interactions and dynamics. Evolutionary biology examines the emergence and transformation of organised biological characteristics.

The difference lies in the explanatory role assigned to organisation, not in whether it matters.

Third, the perspectives differ in their characteristic evidential practices.

Evolutionary reconstruction cannot simply be substituted for experimental mechanism identification. Mathematical modelling may reveal dynamic relationships without resolving every question of biological individuality. Organisational analysis may propose constitutive dependencies requiring further empirical specification.

APS similarly requires biological evidence appropriate to the relation being investigated. Its conceptual categories do not supply that evidence independently.

Finally, the comparison reveals substantial overlap between APS and existing organisational approaches.

Viability orientation, self-maintenance, constraint closure, function, and biological agency are already prominent topics within organisational and related theoretical traditions.

The APS framework's particular arrangement of these concerns is a legitimate object of descriptive comparison, but its distinctiveness must not be exaggerated.

## One Biological Phenomenon, Several Explanatory Questions: Plant Responses to Water Deficit

Plant responses to water deficit provide a useful illustration because the same biological phenomenon can be investigated through several explanatory orientations.

When soil water availability declines, plants may experience changes in water potential, hydraulic transport, cellular turgor, signalling, stomatal behaviour, carbon assimilation, and growth. The consequences depend upon species, developmental condition, environmental circumstances, and the severity and duration of disturbance.

The physiology of these responses is already a sophisticated research field.

Buckley (2019) reviews the mechanisms through which stomata respond to water status, including hydraulic relationships, epidermal mechanics, active feedback, and possible roles for abscisic acid. His account also illustrates why a simple model of guard-cell inflation and deflation cannot adequately represent stomatal responses across all plant groups.

Lawson and Vialet-Chabrand (2019) examine how the speed of stomatal responses influences photosynthesis and water-use efficiency. Their work demonstrates that conventional physiology already investigates temporal coordination, functional consequences, and the relationship between plant activity and environmental conditions.

These findings provide a strong biological basis for the comparison.

### Mechanistic Explanation

A mechanistic explanation may investigate how hydraulic conditions, signalling pathways, ion transport, and guard-cell and epidermal mechanics produce changes in stomatal aperture.

Such an explanation requires identifying relevant causal processes, their organisation, and the conditions under which they produce particular responses.

The interactions between active regulation and tissue mechanics are important because the observed response cannot necessarily be inferred from guard-cell turgor alone.

### Evolutionary Explanation

An evolutionary explanation may investigate how drought-response traits arose, how they vary among populations or lineages, and how their distribution reflects selection, inheritance, historical contingency, and ecological conditions.

Evolutionary research may also examine contemporary selection on water-use strategies, physiological traits, or developmental responses under changing environments.

The explanatory question is not simply how stomata close, but how particular response capacities evolve and influence evolutionary outcomes.

### Systems-Biology Explanation

A systems-biology explanation may model dynamic relationships among hydraulic transport, signalling, stomatal conductance, photosynthesis, metabolism, and environmental conditions.

Such a model may investigate feedback, response times, sensitivity, robustness, and the consequences of interacting processes under different conditions.

The temporal dynamics of stomatal behaviour and their consequences for carbon assimilation and water loss are already important subjects of quantitative plant physiology and systems modelling.

### Organisational Explanation

An organisational approach may ask how regulatory activities and their constraints contribute to the maintenance of living organisation.

It may investigate how water transport, metabolic activity, regulation, and structural conditions depend upon one another, and how disturbance affects the system's capacity to maintain its organisation.

This inquiry does not merely describe the presence of feedback. It seeks to identify relationships relevant to biological autonomy, self-maintenance, function, and the conditions under which the system continues to exist.

### APS Explanation

APS may ask how viability-oriented activity contributes to organised persistence under water deficit.

Its Agency projection may foreground regulatory and compensatory activities relevant to viability. Its Process projection may examine how continuity is maintained, compromised, or restored through changing conditions. Its Scale projection may identify spatial and temporal relationships relevant to the explanatory question, from cellular processes to whole-plant activity and environmental coupling.

These questions do not establish five competing explanations of one identical explanandum.

The mechanistic account may identify processes used by a systems model. Evolutionary analysis may explain the history of traits whose operation is investigated physiologically. Organisational analysis may interpret relationships through which physiological activities contribute to self-maintenance. APS may organise selected questions about those relationships in terms of viability and persistence.

A scientifically adequate analysis may employ several of these resources.

The comparison should not be interpreted as assigning the explanation of whole-plant viability exclusively to organisational biology or APS. Contemporary plant physiology and ecophysiology already investigate the coordinated effects of water relations, stomatal regulation, carbon assimilation, growth, and environmental conditions upon plant performance and survival.

The APS presentation selects organised persistence as its explanatory target and arranges relevant questions through Agency, Process, and Scale. It does not thereby identify dependencies that established physiological, ecological, or systems explanations have overlooked.

The example therefore demonstrates the descriptive usefulness of distinguishing explanatory questions. It does not establish additional explanatory gain for APS or any other perspective.

## Overlap, Complementarity, and Genuine Disagreement

Comparative analysis becomes most informative when it distinguishes several relationships among perspectives.

### Shared Explanatory Concerns

All five perspectives can investigate organised biological processes.

Mechanistic research examines how organised activities produce phenomena. Evolutionary biology investigates the origins, dynamics, and consequences of biological organisation. Systems biology models interacting processes. Organisational theories investigate self-maintenance and autonomy. APS examines organised persistence through its own conceptual architecture.

This common concern does not establish a common explanatory principle.

The word *organisation* performs different roles within the five perspectives, and its meaning must be interpreted in context.

### Complementary Explanations

Different perspectives may contribute to understanding a biological phenomenon by answering distinct questions.

Mechanistic research can identify how a regulatory response occurs. Evolutionary biology can explain the origin or changing distribution of relevant traits. Systems biology can model interacting dynamics. Organisational analysis can investigate contributions to self-maintenance.

APS may provide a further conceptual description of how these inquiries relate to organised persistence when that is the selected explanandum.

Complementarity does not require every explanation to be combined into one account. Some questions are adequately answered within a single explanatory practice.

### Overlapping Explanations

Perspectives may also recover much of the same explanatory content.

Systems biology and mechanistic explanation frequently investigate the same regulatory processes. Organisational theories and APS share substantial commitments concerning constraint closure, viability, and self-maintenance.

Where two perspectives identify the same dependencies and consequences, their differences may concern conceptual organisation rather than explanatory content.

Such differences can matter for interpretation and communication, but they should not automatically be described as explanatory advances.

### Conceptual Disagreements

Conceptual disagreements arise when perspectives adopt different criteria for the application of a concept.

The scope of biological agency provides an example. Some organisational approaches distinguish constitutive self-maintenance from agency as an interactive dimension of autonomy. APS applies agency more broadly to viability-oriented organisational activity, including internal maintenance.

The difference may change which activities receive an agency attribution. Whether it produces further explanatory consequences remains an open question.

Similar difficulties arise in debates about biological individuality, function, cognition, and normativity.

### Empirical Disagreements

Empirical disagreements concern what biological processes occur, which dependencies are present, or what consequences follow under specified conditions.

Competing explanations of stomatal regulation, for example, may make different predictions about responses to hydraulic perturbation, signalling manipulation, or environmental change.

Such disputes require appropriate empirical investigation.

Conceptual and empirical disagreements may also interact. A proposed definition may imply that certain biological systems should exhibit particular properties, making some conceptual claims empirically assessable.

The existence of multiple perspectives therefore does not eliminate scientific disagreement. It makes the identification of genuine disagreement more important.

## Unity Within Diversity

Biology already achieves a measure of conceptual unity through the practice of research. Different disciplines can investigate the same phenomenon, draw on one another’s findings, and make their explanations intelligible to one another by specifying the questions they address and the evidence on which they rely. An account of stomatal closure can explain how the response is produced, how it affects water loss and carbon uptake, or why a particular pattern of responsiveness evolved. A physiological finding about water loss can inform an evolutionary account, provided the connection between that finding and the evolutionary claim is made explicit.

The comparative matrix illustrates one way to make such connections clearer. Its dimensions give readers a common set of questions about explanatory targets, concepts, methods, evidence, and scope. A shared vocabulary could extend this practice across biology. Where researchers use different terms for closely related concepts, they can specify what those concepts share. Where the same term carries different meanings, as *function* can in physiological and evolutionary explanation, they can state which meaning is in use and how it relates to the other. The value of a common vocabulary lies partly in its ability to preserve distinctions that matter.

This is one way to pursue unity within diversity. Biology could develop a shared *explanatory grammar* that helps researchers relate questions, claims, and evidence across disciplines while preserving the specialised vocabularies and methods that give each explanation its precision. Its value could be tested by asking whether researchers from different fields can use it to identify more accurately what two explanations address, where their claims support or conflict with one another, and what further evidence would clarify how they relate. A demonstrated improvement on those tasks would be a worthwhile scientific contribution in its own right.

## Where APS Fits Within the Landscape

APS has its closest intellectual affinities with organisational accounts of biological autonomy, constraint closure, self-maintenance, and function. These accounts provide substantial precedents for its treatment of living organisation and agency. APS also shares with process philosophy an interest in continuity through change, and with systems biology an interest in the dynamics of interacting processes. Its analyses draw on mechanistic, developmental, evolutionary, physiological, and ecological explanations as the biological question requires. Its relationship to organisational approaches is examined further in [Organisational Realism in Biology](/articles/organisational-realism-in-biology/).

The comparative matrix presents APS separately so that readers can examine its particular arrangement of concepts alongside those of the other perspectives. APS defines life and biological agency in relation to viability, takes organised persistence as a recurring explanatory concern, and uses Agency, Process, and Scale to examine how living systems operate, change, and persist. These commitments give APS a recognisable profile within a landscape in which it shares substantial ground with organisational biology.

Within the approach to unity outlined above, APS offers a candidate vocabulary for connecting explanations that bear on the persistence of living systems. For instance, the concept of viability could help researchers relate a physiological account of stomatal closure to an ecological account of plant performance under drought by making explicit the conditions under which reduced water loss contributes to continued functioning. Whether APS terminology helps researchers from different fields identify such connections more accurately remains open to comparative assessment.

The framework can be assessed on several grounds. Its definitions and conceptual relationships can be examined for internal coherence. Their concision may make the framework easier to state, while judging its overall conceptual economy requires examining what assumptions and distinctions an application actually needs. Its communicative value depends on how well it helps researchers connect explanations across fields. Any claim of additional explanatory gain depends on what an APS analysis can explain beyond the strongest relevant established account. Each of these questions can be investigated in its own right.

## Conclusion

Biology does not possess a single explanatory question, method, or conceptual vocabulary.

Mechanistic, evolutionary, systems, organisational, and APS perspectives investigate living systems through different but overlapping explanatory orientations. Their relationships include complementarity, shared explanatory resources, conceptual overlap, and substantive disagreement.

Comparing these perspectives is philosophically difficult because they are not equivalent intellectual enterprises, do not always share explanatory targets, and cannot necessarily be evaluated through one common measure of success.

These difficulties do not make comparison unproductive.

A disciplined descriptive methodology can identify explanatory targets, organising principles, conceptual commitments, methods, evidence, applications, and scope without requiring a theoretical ranking.

The comparison presented here places APS within that wider landscape. Its account of viability-oriented organisation, organised persistence, and Agency, Process, and Scale can be made explicit without claiming that these concepts are necessary for every biological explanation or that their integration has established additional explanatory gain.

The purpose of comparison is therefore not to discover which perspective should replace the others.

It is to understand what each perspective asks, what it claims, how its claims are supported, and where different explanations may contribute to a richer understanding of living systems.
