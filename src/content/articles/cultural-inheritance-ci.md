**Where This Article Fits**

*Cultural Inheritance* *is a Core article within the Social Organisation topic of APS.*

*Its central question is how practices, skills, meanings, expectations, knowledge, and forms of coordination are transmitted, reconstructed, and transformed across individuals, populations, and generations.**

*The article builds upon **Cultural Organisation**, which examines culture as a socially distributed form of organised persistence. The distinction is important. Cultural organisation concerns the relationships through which coordinated practices and meanings are sustained. Cultural inheritance concerns the processes through which such organisation becomes available to successive participants and can persist or change through historical time.*

*The article also connects the APS account of social organisation with the evolutionary literature on cultural transmission, dual inheritance, cumulative cultural evolution, and gene–culture coevolution.*

I*ts purpose is not to replace established cultural evolutionary theory. Rather, it examines what an organisational interpretation contributes to understanding cultural continuity and transformation, while recognising that questions concerning transmission mechanisms, population dynamics, selection, drift, and evolutionary outcomes require their own empirical and theoretical explanations.*

## Introduction

Culture persists through time, but it does not persist automatically.

Practices may survive the individuals who perform them. Knowledge acquired by one generation may become available to another. Languages, traditions, technologies, institutions, and systems of meaning can continue despite continual changes in their participants. Such continuity is one of the most remarkable features of human social life, although socially transmitted traditions are not confined to humans.

This persistence requires explanation. Cultural practices are not simply preserved as static possessions. They must be learned, performed, communicated, interpreted, and often reconstructed within changing social and environmental circumstances. Their continuation depends upon activities through which later participants acquire or recreate aspects of the organisation established by earlier participants.

These activities constitute cultural inheritance.

Within APS, cultural inheritance is understood as the transmission, reconstruction, and transformation of practices, skills, meanings, expectations, symbolic systems, and forms of knowledge through social learning and participation in cultural organisation.

Cultural inheritance allows organised social activity to extend beyond the lives of particular individuals. It makes previously established resources available to successors, while also creating opportunities for modification, innovation, and loss.

The central APS interpretation is:

> Cultural inheritance comprises the processes through which socially distributed cultural organisation is reproduced or transformed across individuals and generations.

This formulation distinguishes the organisation of culture from the processes through which it is inherited.

Culture provides historically established patterns of coordinated activity, meaning, and practice. Cultural inheritance identifies the processes through which those patterns may be acquired, reproduced, modified, or abandoned.

Together, they contribute to the historical continuity and transformation of social organisation.

However, the existence of cultural inheritance does not guarantee that particular traditions will persist. Cultural practices may disappear, spread without improving biological viability, or change so extensively that their relationship to earlier forms becomes difficult to identify.

APS must therefore explain cultural continuity without treating continuity as inevitable, and cultural evolution without assuming that evolutionary change necessarily constitutes progress.

## Why Cultural Inheritance Exists

Social organisation depends upon activities performed by individuals whose participation is necessarily limited in time.

People enter communities already possessing languages, practices, expectations, technologies, and institutional arrangements. They acquire capacities that were developed before their birth, frequently without understanding the historical processes through which those capacities emerged.

Without mechanisms of cultural transmission, many such resources would disappear when their possessors died or ceased participating in social life. Skills would need to be rediscovered, conventions renegotiated, and accumulated knowledge repeatedly reconstructed without reliable access to previous achievements.

Cultural inheritance makes a different historical arrangement possible.

Through observation, teaching, imitation, participation, communication, and the use of artefacts, individuals can acquire practices and knowledge developed by others. They can reproduce established activities without independently discovering all the conditions necessary for their performance.

This greatly expands the possibilities for social continuity.

A craft may be transmitted through apprenticeship. A language may be acquired through everyday participation. Scientific techniques may be taught through educational institutions. Agricultural practices may be maintained through families and communities. Records may preserve information that would otherwise disappear from living memory.

In each case, continuity depends upon processes linking earlier and later participants.

Yet these processes differ in reliability, accessibility, and consequence. Some practices are transmitted with considerable fidelity, while others undergo extensive reconstruction. Some are preserved through formal instruction, while others spread through observation or informal participation. Some traditions persist for centuries; others disappear within a generation.

The relevant explanatory problem is therefore not simply why culture exists, but how particular cultural resources become available to successors, under what conditions they are reproduced, and why some persist while others change or disappear.

APS interprets these processes in relation to organised persistence. Cultural inheritance can maintain resources supporting social organisation, but the particular mechanisms and historical outcomes require independent investigation.

## Cultural Inheritance Beyond Biological Inheritance

Cultural inheritance and biological inheritance are related but distinguishable phenomena.

Genetic inheritance involves the transmission of genetic material through reproductive processes. Biological inheritance more broadly involves the developmental reconstitution of living systems through genetic, cellular, physiological, ecological, and other contributing resources.

Cultural inheritance operates through socially mediated processes of learning, communication, participation, and reconstruction. It can occur between parents and offspring, among peers, across unrelated individuals, and through institutions or artefacts that connect participants separated by considerable distances or historical periods.

The distinction is therefore not simply between biological material and cultural information.

Both forms of inheritance depend upon materially realised processes. Cultural knowledge requires organisms capable of learning and acting, as well as the social and environmental conditions through which practices become accessible. Biological inheritance likewise depends upon developmental processes operating within organised contexts.

The APS article **Inheritance and Continuity in APS** develops the biological account of how viable organisation is reconstituted across generations. The present article addresses a different explanatory target: how socially established practices and resources become available to successive participants.

These distinctions do not imply evolutionary independence.

Cultural practices may alter the conditions under which biological evolution occurs. Dietary practices, agricultural systems, settlement patterns, technological developments, and socially organised environments can modify ecological conditions and selective pressures. Biological and developmental capacities, in turn, influence the possibilities for cultural learning, communication, and participation.

Dual-inheritance theory and gene–culture coevolution investigate such relationships systematically. The quantitative foundations developed by Cavalli-Sforza and Feldman (1981), and Boyd and Richerson (1985), established important approaches to modelling cultural transmission and its interaction with biological evolution.

APS is compatible with the investigation of these interactions, but it does not replace the population-level models through which their evolutionary consequences are analysed.

Its organisational concern is how genetic, developmental, ecological, and cultural processes participate in historically continuing systems, and which relationships are relevant to the particular phenomenon requiring explanation.

It is a socially mediated process grounded in biological capacities, material practices, and historically established relationships.

## Social Learning as a Mechanism of Cultural Inheritance

Social learning is one of the principal mechanisms through which cultural inheritance occurs.

Individuals acquire practices, skills, expectations, and knowledge through their relationships with others. Observation, imitation, teaching, apprenticeship, participation, and communication can make previously established activities available to learners without requiring independent discovery.

The importance of social learning is well established in cultural evolutionary research. It allows individuals to draw upon the experience of others, and it can sustain traditions that extend beyond the capacities or lifetimes of particular participants.

However, social learning is not a single uniform mechanism.

A learner may imitate an observed action, reproduce its apparent outcome through different means, follow explicit instruction, infer an underlying rule, or acquire a practice through extended participation in a community.

These processes need not produce identical results.

A transmitted practice may be modified because the learner misunderstands an instruction, adapts an activity to local circumstances, combines it with existing knowledge, or reconstructs a result without reproducing the original procedure.

Cultural continuity therefore does not always require faithful replication.

A tradition may remain recognisable because successive participants repeatedly reconstruct similar practices under similar social and environmental conditions. In other circumstances, relatively accurate copying may be essential to preserving a complex technique or convention.

The distinction matters for the relationship between cultural inheritance and theories of replication.

### Memetics and the problem of cultural replication

Memetics, historically associated with Richard Dawkins and subsequently developed by Daniel Dennett and others, proposed that cultural evolution could be understood through the differential replication of cultural units called memes.

The analogy with genetic evolution drew attention to the possibility that ideas, practices, and representations might spread through populations through processes resembling variation, transmission, and differential persistence.

The proposal remains historically important, but the identification of discrete cultural replicators presents difficulties.

Cultural practices do not always possess clear boundaries. Their transmission frequently involves interpretation, reconstruction, and transformation. Similar practices may arise through different learning pathways, while apparently identical cultural expressions may have different meanings or functions in different settings.

Consequently, cultural evolutionary explanation does not generally require the assumption that traditions consist of faithfully copied, gene-like units.

Approaches associated with cultural epidemiology and cultural attraction emphasise the reconstruction and transformation of representations during transmission. Population-based cultural evolutionary approaches investigate the changing distribution of cultural variants without necessarily requiring a strong replicator interpretation.

These alternatives illuminate different aspects of cultural change and should not be treated as interchangeable.

APS does not need to adopt memetics as its account of cultural inheritance. Nor does its organisational perspective, by itself, resolve the debates concerning cultural units, replication, and reconstruction.

Its contribution is to ask how the activities involved in transmission sustain or transform the organisation of cultural practices and the social relationships through which they operate.

### Transmission biases and population processes

Cultural inheritance also depends upon who learns from whom, and under what circumstances.

Individuals may preferentially acquire common practices, follow prestigious or successful models, learn from familiar groups, or adopt behaviours perceived as effective. Such learning biases can influence the distribution of cultural variants across populations.

Cultural change can also result from innovation, error, demographic change, migration, and drift.

A practice may become widespread because it is advantageous in a particular environment. It may also spread because its practitioners are influential, because it is frequently encountered, or because chance events alter the composition of a population.

Similarly, a useful practice may disappear when the individuals possessing it are lost, when transmission opportunities decline, or when social conditions change.

These mechanisms are central to the quantitative approaches developed by Cavalli-Sforza and Feldman (1981), and Boyd and Richerson (1985).

They demonstrate why cultural evolution cannot be explained solely through the preservation of successful organisational arrangements.

APS must distinguish the processes responsible for the transmission and distribution of cultural variants from its broader interpretation of the organisation within which those processes occur.

## Cultural Inheritance and the Preservation of Coordination

Cultural inheritance contributes to social continuity by making established forms of coordination available to successive participants.

Communication, norms, symbolic conventions, and practical routines enable individuals to organise activities in relation to shared expectations. Such coordination may be reproduced when new participants acquire the relevant practices.

A language, for example, depends upon continuing participation in conventions that make communication possible. Its speakers do not reproduce every expression or meaning identically, but their activities sustain sufficient continuity for communication to remain effective.

Similarly, agricultural practices, craft traditions, educational procedures, and institutional routines depend upon the transmission of skills and expectations through which coordinated activity can continue.

What persists is not necessarily a fixed cultural object.

It may be a way of performing an activity, interpreting a situation, responding to others, or participating within an established social arrangement.

Cultural inheritance can therefore preserve organisation through the repeated performance and reconstruction of coordinated practices.

However, the same processes can produce transformation.

Practices may change in response to new technologies, environmental conditions, demographic changes, or competing expectations. Shared conventions may become contested. Traditions may fragment or disappear.

The organisational outcome depends upon the relationships through which transmission occurs and the circumstances in which inherited practices are performed.

**Cultural Organisation** develops the wider APS account of these socially distributed relationships. The present article concentrates upon the transmission processes through which they are reproduced or transformed.

## Cultural Inheritance Across Scales

Cultural inheritance operates across multiple spatial, temporal, and social scales.

At relatively local scales, individuals may acquire skills and practices through families, peers, apprenticeships, and communities. Repeated interaction provides opportunities for observation, correction, instruction, and participation.

At larger scales, cultural transmission may connect geographically dispersed populations through trade, migration, educational institutions, written records, and communication technologies.

Across historical time, cultural resources may remain accessible through institutions, artefacts, and practices that survive the individuals who originally developed them.

These differences of scale are important because transmission mechanisms and their consequences can change with the extent of the relationships involved.

Face-to-face apprenticeship may preserve practical skills through repeated correction and demonstration. Written instructions may extend access to knowledge while separating learners from the circumstances in which that knowledge was originally produced. Digital technologies can rapidly distribute cultural material while altering its interpretation, selection, and reproduction.

Population structure also matters.

The frequency of contact among groups, the distribution of learning opportunities, demographic change, and migration can influence which cultural variants spread, remain local, or disappear.

Scale therefore does not simply measure the longevity or geographical extent of cultural organisation.

It identifies differences in the material and social relationships through which transmission operates.

APS treats scale as an explanatory question: which spatial, temporal, and organisational relationships must be included to understand the transmission and historical consequences of a particular cultural practice?

The answer depends upon the phenomenon under investigation rather than upon a predetermined hierarchy of cultural levels.

## Cultural Inheritance and Collective Memory

Cultural inheritance plays an important role in the maintenance of collective memory.

Communities can preserve access to earlier experience through narratives, rituals, records, educational practices, symbolic systems, and repeated forms of participation.

These resources make aspects of the past available to people who did not experience the events or activities through which they originated.

Collective memory is therefore not simply the storage of information.

Remembered material must be interpreted, communicated, and made meaningful within present circumstances. Records require readers, practices require participants, and traditions require continuing activities through which their significance is reproduced.

In this respect, collective memory is an active process.

However, remembering is also selective.

Communities may preserve certain interpretations while neglecting others. Historical narratives may be contested, modified, or reconstructed. Institutional records may endure while the practices that originally gave them significance disappear.

Cultural inheritance can therefore contribute both to the continuity and transformation of collective memory.

APS interprets collective memory as an organisational phenomenon supported by relationships among people, practices, institutions, and artefacts.

The particular concern of cultural inheritance is how those relationships allow remembered material to become available across participants and generations, and why some forms of memory remain durable while others are transformed or lost.

## Cultural Inheritance and Cultural Evolution

Cultural inheritance makes historical continuity possible, but cultural evolution involves more than the preservation of inherited practices.

Cultural variants can change in frequency, distribution, form, and function through time. New practices emerge, established traditions are modified, and some disappear.

These transformations may result from several distinguishable processes.

Cultural selection can produce differences in the persistence or spread of variants. Drift can alter their distribution through stochastic processes. Learning biases can influence which practices individuals acquire. Migration and contact can introduce variants into new populations. Innovation and reconstruction can generate forms that differ from those previously transmitted.

These mechanisms may interact, but they are not equivalent.

Their relative importance must be established through the investigation of particular cultural phenomena.

Theories of cultural evolution provide mathematical, comparative, historical, and empirical approaches for examining these processes. APS should recognise their explanatory achievements rather than assume that an organisational interpretation supersedes them.

### Cumulative cultural evolution

One important consequence of cultural inheritance is the possibility of cumulative cultural evolution.

When innovations are retained and made available to successors, later participants may modify or combine them to produce capacities that would have been difficult to develop independently.

Complex technologies, scientific practices, agricultural systems, and specialised skills can exhibit this cumulative character.

Tomasello (1999), Sterelny (2012), and Henrich (2016) provide influential accounts of the developmental, social, and evolutionary conditions through which cultural learning and accumulation become possible.

However, accumulation is not an inevitable outcome of cultural transmission.

Knowledge can be lost. Technical capacities can decline. Traditions may disappear through demographic change, environmental disruption, or the breakdown of transmission pathways.

Nor does increasing cultural complexity necessarily imply biological adaptation or progress.

Some culturally transmitted practices impose substantial costs. Others spread because of social influence or historical circumstances rather than because they improve individual or collective viability.

A tradition may remain stable even when its original function has disappeared.

Cultural evolution therefore includes persistence, accumulation, simplification, divergence, and loss.

APS should interpret these outcomes without treating continuity as synonymous with improvement.

### Gene–culture coevolution and inherited environments

Cultural evolution can also influence biological evolutionary processes.

Culturally established practices may modify diets, reproductive patterns, settlement arrangements, disease environments, and ecological relationships.

These changes can alter the conditions under which genetic variants are transmitted and selected.

Biological capacities may, in turn, affect the opportunities for cultural learning, communication, and social participation.

Dual-inheritance theory and gene–culture coevolution examine such reciprocal interactions.

Niche-construction approaches additionally investigate how organisms modify environments that subsequently influence development and evolution.

Cultural practices can contribute to such environmental modification, creating historically persistent conditions encountered by later generations.

These approaches already recognise relationships among inheritance, development, ecology, and evolutionary transformation.

APS should therefore avoid presenting their integration as an exclusively APS discovery.

Its organisational interpretation may help identify how these processes contribute to the persistence or transformation of particular biological and social arrangements, but that possibility requires demonstration in a specified explanatory context.

### The explanatory contribution of APS

APS approaches cultural evolution through the question of organised persistence.

It asks what relationships allow a cultural practice, institution, or coordinated activity to remain recognisable through time, how those relationships are materially sustained, and what changes when transmission processes alter them.

This question directs attention to the organisation of cultural continuity rather than treating inherited cultural variants solely as independently identifiable contents.

However, organisational interpretations of cultural evolution are not unique to APS. Developmental, ecological, processual, and cultural evolutionary approaches already investigate many of the relationships relevant to cultural persistence and transformation.

The potential value of APS therefore lies in the clarity and usefulness of its explanatory distinctions, not in a presumed priority over these traditions.

Whether an APS analysis improves upon an established cultural evolutionary explanation must be determined through comparison with the strongest relevant alternative.

An organisational redescription alone is not sufficient to establish explanatory advance.

## Cultural Inheritance, Institutions, and Technology

Institutions and technologies play important roles in cultural inheritance because they can stabilise, extend, and transform the conditions of transmission.

Institutions establish recurring practices, expectations, roles, and procedures through which participants acquire cultural capacities.

Educational institutions organise teaching and assessment. Legal institutions reproduce conventions and procedures. Scientific institutions transmit methods, standards, and bodies of knowledge through training, publication, and collective investigation.

These arrangements can support continuity despite changes in membership.

Technologies also alter the possibilities for cultural transmission.

Writing, printing, recording, measurement, communication networks, and digital infrastructures can preserve and distribute resources that would otherwise depend upon direct interpersonal interaction.

Such technologies can extend the temporal and geographical reach of inherited knowledge.

However, they do not guarantee faithful preservation.

New media can change which practices are encountered, how information is interpreted, and which cultural variants become influential. Technologies may preserve certain traditions while contributing to the displacement of others.

Institutions can likewise support both continuity and transformation. Established procedures may stabilise knowledge, but they may also constrain innovation, preserve ineffective practices, or resist changing circumstances.

The explanatory significance of institutions and technologies therefore depends upon their particular roles in transmission.

APS interprets these relationships as part of the materially realised organisation through which cultural inheritance occurs.

Their wider significance for social coordination and collective persistence is developed in **Cultural Organisation**, **Institutions**, and **Technology**.

## Why Cultural Inheritance Matters

Cultural inheritance is one of the principal processes through which social organisation acquires historical continuity.

It makes practices, skills, knowledge, expectations, and symbolic resources available beyond the lives of their original participants.

Through cultural inheritance, individuals can enter already established systems of activity and participate in forms of coordination developed through earlier historical experience.

This creates possibilities for the preservation and accumulation of cultural resources, but also for their modification, redistribution, and loss.

Cultural inheritance is therefore not simply the transfer of traditions from one generation to another.

It is a collection of socially mediated processes through which cultural organisation is repeatedly reproduced and transformed.

The distinction between cultural organisation and cultural inheritance is central to the APS interpretation.

Cultural organisation concerns the relationships through which coordinated practices and meanings are sustained. Cultural inheritance concerns the transmission and reconstruction processes through which those relationships may continue or change.

This interpretation connects cultural phenomena with the broader APS concern for organised persistence, while preserving the explanatory importance of established cultural evolutionary theory.

The scientific value of the interpretation depends upon its ability to clarify particular phenomena and, where explanatory improvement is claimed, to demonstrate that improvement against adequate alternatives.

Cultural inheritance matters because it makes historically extended social organisation possible without requiring that culture remain unchanged.

It is one of the processes through which the past remains active in the present while becoming available for transformation in the future.

### Central Question

How are cultural practices, skills, meanings, expectations, and forms of knowledge transmitted, reconstructed, and transformed across individuals and generations, and under what conditions do these processes sustain or alter socially distributed organisation?

### Architectural Role

This article is a Core article within the Social Organisation topic.

It develops cultural inheritance as a distinguishable set of transmission and reconstruction processes contributing to the continuity and transformation of cultural organisation.

Its purpose is to explain the relationship between cultural transmission and organised persistence without reducing cultural evolution to faithful replication, cumulative improvement, or biological adaptation.

### Builds On

The article builds upon the APS accounts of social organisation, communication, coordination, normativity, symbolic activity, organised persistence, and explanatory scale.

It also draws upon established research in cultural transmission, social learning, dual-inheritance theory, and cultural evolution.

### Relationship to Biological Inheritance and Evolution

**Inheritance and Continuity in APS** addresses the reconstitution of viable biological organisation across generations.

The present article addresses socially mediated cultural inheritance.

The distinction concerns their explanatory targets and mechanisms rather than a complete separation between biological and cultural processes.

**Evolution as the Historical Transformation of Organised Persistence** provides the broader APS evolutionary architecture. Cultural evolutionary processes may interact with biological evolution, but their particular mechanisms and consequences must be established independently.

### Explanatory Boundaries

Cultural inheritance does not necessarily produce continuity, accumulation, adaptation, or increasing complexity.

Cultural evolution may involve selection, drift, learning biases, innovation, migration, reconstruction, and loss.

APS does not replace established cultural evolutionary models or establish explanatory superiority through organisational terminology alone.

Claims of explanatory improvement require comparison with the strongest relevant alternatives in a specified empirical or theoretical case.

### Corpus Significance

Within APS, cultural inheritance connects social organisation with historical continuity and transformation.

It identifies how socially established practices and resources can persist beyond particular participants while remaining open to modification and loss.

Its principal contribution to the APS corpus is to distinguish the processes of cultural transmission from the organisation they reproduce or transform, and to situate that distinction within established biological and cultural evolutionary explanation.
