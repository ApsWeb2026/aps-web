>> **Where this article fits:** This is a Life-demarcation clarification. It asks whether genetic replication or a gene-centred description should be identified with life itself. It does not adjudicate the comparative adequacy of gene-centred evolutionary theories as a class. Broader comparison belongs to *Gene-Centric Biology and APS* and to the APS comparative-explanation programme.

## A Familiar Idea — and Its Problem

“Life is DNA’s way of making more DNA” is a memorable expression of the importance of heredity and replication. It captures a genuine feature of biological continuity: genetic material is transmitted across generations, heritable differences contribute to evolutionary change, and DNA plays a central role in familiar terrestrial life. The difficulty arises only when this explanatory importance is converted into an identity claim about life itself.

Read modestly, the slogan draws attention to the evolutionary significance of genetic replication, a role APS has no reason to deny. Read more strongly, however, it suggests that living systems are fundamentally vehicles whose biological significance is exhausted by the replication of genetic material. It is this stronger identification, rather than the explanatory importance of genes, that APS rejects.

The distinction matters because an account can explain an important biological process without thereby defining the organisation in which that process occurs. Genetic replication, inheritance, and evolutionary change are central features of familiar life, but their explanatory importance does not establish that life itself should be identified with the replication of genetic material.

## What Genetic Explanations Explain

Gene-centred, population-genetic, molecular, and other evolutionary approaches provide substantive explanations of biological phenomena. They illuminate inheritance and genetic variation, differential transmission, evolutionary change, the consequences of selection, and the persistence of heritable differences across generations. These are genuine explanatory achievements and should not be reduced to mere description simply because APS organises its account of life around a different explanatory target.

The question addressed here is therefore deliberately narrower: **does the explanatory importance of genetic replication establish that life itself should be identified with genetic replication?** The answer is no. An explanation of how heritable material is transmitted, how genetic variants change in frequency, or how selection acts upon heritable differences need not also constitute a definition of living organisation.

Keeping these questions separate is methodologically important. If genetic and organisational explanations address different explananda, their difference does not establish that one has displaced the other. Only where they address a genuinely matched biological target does comparative explanatory adequacy become the relevant question.

## Replication Is Not a Definition of Life

DNA replication is a biological process occurring within organised material systems. In familiar cellular life, replication depends upon molecular machinery, energy supply, regulation, membrane organisation, appropriate chemical conditions, and other processes through which cells maintain the conditions under which genetic activity can occur. DNA therefore does not replicate biologically in isolation from organised cellular activity.

This is a material dependency claim, but its implications must be kept bounded. The dependence of genetic replication upon organised cellular conditions does not by itself establish that APS provides a superior explanation of genetics, heredity, or evolution, nor does it imply that every explanatory question concerning genes should be reformulated in APS terminology. A molecular or population-genetic explanation may be entirely adequate for the explanandum it addresses.

What the dependency does establish is more limited. The existence and replication of DNA within living systems should not be confused with a definition of the living organisation in which those processes occur. Genetic replication can be indispensable to the continuity of familiar biological lineages without being identical with the organisation that makes those lineages living systems.

## Life in APS

APS defines life as:

> **Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation.**

This organisation consists in the ongoing modulation of constraints through which a system actively maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence. Life, on this account, is not identified with a particular component or isolated process but with the organisation through which biological continuity is actively maintained despite material and environmental change.

Within that organisation, **biological agency is viability-oriented organisational activity**. Agency is therefore not an additional component alongside DNA, metabolism, regulation, membranes, or other biological processes. It names the activity through which viability-oriented living organisation is enacted and sustained.

Constraint closure contributes to specifying this organisation by describing relations in which relevant constraints are maintained through the activity of the system whose organisation they help make possible. Genetic processes can participate materially in these relations and can be indispensable to their continuation across generations. Their participation, however, does not make genetic replication identical with the organisation as a whole.

The distinction is therefore not between genes and organisation as competing biological realities. Genes are part of living organisation, and their activities contribute to it. The question is whether one important class of biological processes should be identified with life itself. APS answers that question negatively.

## Genes Within Living Organisation

Recognising this distinction does not diminish the biological importance of genes. Genetic processes contribute to the production and regulation of biological components, developmental continuity, inheritance, phenotypic variation, and evolutionary change. Their effects are extensive, and in many explanatory contexts a genetic account may be sufficient or preferable.

At the same time, genetic activity is materially embedded in wider biological organisation. Genes are expressed, replicated, repaired, regulated, and transmitted through cellular and organismal processes involving metabolic activity, molecular interactions, developmental organisation, and environmental conditions. Which of these relations matters explanatorily depends upon the biological question being asked.

APS therefore does not propose that organismal organisation should replace molecular or genetic explanation. Different explananda require different explanatory resources. For some questions, the relevant dependencies may be predominantly genetic. For others, the organisation through which genetic, metabolic, regulatory, developmental, and environmental processes are coordinated may itself become the explanatory target.

It is in relation to this latter problem that APS asks how living organisation persists through change. The question is not whether genes matter, but what must be explained when the continuing organisation of a living system, rather than the behaviour of a particular genetic component, is the explanandum.

## Evolution and Organised Persistence

The same distinction applies to evolutionary explanation. Evolutionary biology explains historical change through well-established resources including inheritance, variation, differential reproduction, selection, drift, developmental processes, and population structure. APS does not replace these explanations, nor does describing evolution in organisational terms make established evolutionary mechanisms explanatorily dispensable.

APS introduces an additional question concerning what happens to living organisation across evolutionary time: **what is being historically transformed when evolving lineages remain biologically organised through continuing change?** APS describes this target as the historical transformation of viability-oriented organisation. The emphasis is not merely on the persistence of particular components, but on transformations in the organisation through which living systems continue to maintain viable relations with changing conditions.

This formulation does not imply that genes cease to matter, that gene-centred explanations are invalid, or that selection should be translated into APS vocabulary. Genetic inheritance remains central to many evolutionary processes, and established evolutionary explanations retain their explanatory force for the targets they address. Organised persistence instead identifies a possible additional explanandum: the continuity and transformation of living organisation across generations and evolutionary change.

Whether that additional framing produces explanatory gain cannot be settled by conceptual architecture alone. Where APS and an evolutionary account genuinely address the same target, their comparative contribution must be assessed through the dependencies each specifies and the evidence supporting them. A difference in vocabulary, scale of description, or conceptual organisation is not sufficient to establish explanatory superiority.

## What the Distinction Establishes

The Life-demarcation result can now be stated precisely. DNA replication is not identical with life, and genetic inheritance is not by itself a sufficient definition of living organisation. Genes function materially within organised biological systems, and their importance to inheritance and evolution does not require treating organisms merely as instruments through which genetic material reproduces itself.

These conclusions do not entail that gene-centred explanations are biologically illegitimate, that genetic explanations are merely descriptive, or that APS is explanatorily prior to evolutionary genetics. Nor do they imply that organismal explanation always supersedes genetic explanation or that APS provides greater explanatory gain for every evolutionary explanandum. Such claims would require separate target-matched comparison and cannot be inferred from the Life-demarcation result.

This distinction is important because questions of identity and questions of explanatory adequacy are easily conflated. Showing that life is not identical with genetic replication establishes a conceptual and biological boundary; it does not establish which explanatory approach will be best for every phenomenon occurring within that boundary.

## Conclusion

Genes are central to familiar biological inheritance and evolution. Genetic material contributes to continuity across generations, heritable variation makes evolutionary transformation possible, and genetic processes participate materially in the development and regulation of living systems. None of this is diminished by refusing to identify life itself with genetic replication.

APS defines life as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Genetic processes operate within that organisation and contribute to its continuity across generations, but the organisation of living systems cannot simply be equated with the replication of one class of biological components. The distinction is therefore between an important biological process and the organisation within which that process occurs, not between a mistaken genetics and a superior APS explanation.

Life is not, in APS, DNA’s way of making more DNA. Yet the non-identity of life and genetic replication does not establish the explanatory inferiority of genetic or evolutionary biology. It establishes a more limited and useful conclusion: genetic continuity and living organisation are related but distinguishable explanatory targets, and their relationship must be investigated without allowing the explanatory success of one to become a definition of the other.

> **Key Point:** Genetic replication is a central biological process without being the APS definition of life. APS distinguishes genetic continuity from viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation while leaving the comparative adequacy of genetic and organisational explanations to target-matched assessment.

## Explanatory Architecture

### Central Question

The central question is whether life itself should be identified with genetic replication or gene-centred evolutionary description, or whether genetic processes should instead be distinguished from the living organisation within which they occur. APS adopts the latter position while preserving the explanatory importance of genes, inheritance, and established evolutionary processes.

### Architectural Role

This article establishes a Life-demarcation claim. It distinguishes the APS definition of life from genetic replication without claiming that gene-centred, molecular, population-genetic, or evolutionary explanations are therefore inferior. Its task is to prevent the explanatory importance of genetic processes from becoming an identity claim about living organisation.

The broader comparison between APS and gene-centred evolutionary biology is consequently not adjudicated here. That comparison requires a specified shared explanandum and an assessment of the explanatory dependencies and evidence supplied by each approach.

### Preceding Explanatory Dependencies

Within the APS corpus, this clarification draws upon *What Is Life?*, *Biological Agency*, *Constraint Closure*, *Organised Persistence*, *Evolution*, and *APS and Contemporary Explanatory Approaches*. These are relations within the APS explanatory architecture rather than claims that genetic explanation is causally, constitutively, or explanatorily subordinate to APS.

The concepts supplied by these preceding discussions make it possible to distinguish genetic continuity from the organisation through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their persistence. They do not determine in advance which explanatory resources will be most appropriate for any particular genetic or evolutionary explanandum.

### Subsequent Explanatory Developments

The distinction developed here provides a basis for more specific investigation in *Gene-Centric Biology and APS*, comparative work on evolutionary explanation, research on organised persistence across generations, biological individuality, and broader questions concerning inheritance and development.

Those subsequent investigations can ask whether genetic and organisational explanations address genuinely matched targets and, where they do, whether their differences produce explanatory gain, complementarity, redundancy, or comparator advantage. They need not reopen the narrower Life-demarcation result established here.

### Related Explanatory Questions

The clarification therefore opens several further questions without attempting to settle them. These include when genetic and organisational explanations address the same explanandum, which biological processes materially depend upon wider cellular or organismal organisation, when a genetic explanation is sufficient for a particular biological target, and what additional explanatory work, if any, is performed by treating organised persistence as an evolutionary explanandum.

A further methodological question concerns how to distinguish substantive explanatory disagreement from differences in vocabulary or explanatory architecture. Where two approaches describe different dependencies or different targets, their contrast may be biologically consequential. Where they merely redescribe the same dependencies in different conceptual terms, the difference does not by itself constitute explanatory gain.

### Position Within APS

This article belongs to the Life-demarcation family. Its function is to prevent the explanatory importance of DNA, genetic replication, and heredity from being converted into an identity claim about life while preserving their legitimate explanatory roles within genetics and evolutionary biology.

The conclusion is therefore deliberately bounded. Life and genetic replication are not identical, but comparative explanatory superiority remains open.
