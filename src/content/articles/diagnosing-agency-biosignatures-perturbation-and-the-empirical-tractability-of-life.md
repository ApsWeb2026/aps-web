## Why Agency Must Be Diagnosable

Within APS, agency is the defining activity of life. Living systems are not merely collections of components or mechanisms but viability-oriented organisations that actively maintain the conditions of their own continued persistence. If this claim is to function as a scientific principle rather than a philosophical proposal, agency must be empirically tractable. The central challenge is therefore not simply to define agency, but to determine how agency can be identified, investigated, and evaluated in real systems.

This challenge arises across multiple domains of inquiry. Biologists seek criteria for distinguishing living from non-living systems. Astrobiologists seek methods for detecting life beyond Earth. Synthetic biologists seek to determine whether artificial systems exhibit genuinely life-like organisation. In each case, the underlying question is the same: how can agency be diagnosed?

APS approaches this problem by treating agency as an observable organisational phenomenon rather than an invisible internal property. Agency becomes tractable because viability-oriented activity leaves detectable traces and generates characteristic responses to changing conditions. The task of diagnosis is therefore to identify and interpret these forms of evidence.

## Two Paths to Empirical Access

APS identifies two complementary routes through which agency becomes empirically accessible.

The first involves the detection of organisational signatures. These are relatively stable patterns of organisation that indicate the presence of viability-oriented activity. Examples include boundary regulation, self-maintenance, adaptive plasticity, self-repair, and the coordination of processes across multiple scales of organisation. Such signatures provide evidence that a system actively contributes to its own persistence.

The second involves the analysis of perturbation responses. Rather than examining organisational patterns alone, this approach investigates how systems respond when conditions relevant to viability are altered. Perturbations reveal the regulatory dynamics through which living systems preserve themselves, reorganise activity, and recover from disruption.

These approaches are sometimes treated as competing strategies. APS instead treats them as complementary perspectives on the same underlying phenomenon. Organisational signatures reveal the relatively stable structures produced by agency, while perturbation responses reveal the active processes through which agency operates.

The distinction is therefore not between two different kinds of life, but between two different forms of evidence for the same viability-oriented organisation.

## Organisational Signatures of Agency

Every persistent living system exhibits organisational features that reflect its viability-oriented activity. These features are not arbitrary traits but recurring patterns generated by the ongoing maintenance of living organisation.

Boundary regulation provides one example. Living systems selectively manage exchanges between themselves and their surroundings, maintaining organisational integrity while remaining coupled to environmental conditions. Homeostatic and homeorhetic processes provide another example. Living systems actively stabilise the conditions required for continued viability despite continual internal and external change.

Constraint closure offers a deeper organisational signature. The activities of living systems contribute to the maintenance of the very constraints that make those activities possible. Organisational persistence therefore becomes visible through the reciprocal relations that sustain viability over time.

Cross-scale coordination provides further evidence. Molecular, cellular, physiological, behavioural, and ecological processes do not operate independently but become integrated within a broader organisational structure oriented toward persistence. Such integration is difficult to explain without recognising the viability-oriented organisation that produces it.

These signatures do not constitute agency themselves. They are evidence of agency. They function as organisational footprints left by the ongoing activity of systems engaged in maintaining their own viability. Diagnostically, they identify candidate systems whose organisation warrants further investigation.

## Perturbation as a Diagnostic Tool

Organisational signatures provide important evidence of agency, but they do not reveal how a system responds when viability is challenged. For this reason, APS places particular emphasis on perturbation-based investigation. Perturbations expose the regulatory dynamics through which living systems actively maintain themselves and therefore provide direct access to agency in operation.

A perturbation occurs when conditions relevant to viability are altered. These alterations may be internal or external, minor or severe, temporary or persistent. What matters diagnostically is not the perturbation itself but the system's response. Living systems characteristically reorganise their activity in ways that preserve viability, restore organisational integrity, or compensate for changing conditions.

This capacity for reorganisation distinguishes viability-oriented systems from many forms of non-living self-organisation. A crystal may maintain a stable structure under suitable conditions, but it does not actively respond to threats to its continued existence. A living system, by contrast, often modifies its behaviour, physiology, development, or ecological interactions in ways that contribute to its own persistence.

Perturbation therefore reveals something that organisational signatures alone cannot fully disclose. It identifies which conditions matter to a system, which processes are mobilised when viability is threatened, and how organisational continuity is actively maintained. In this sense, perturbation does not merely test organisation. It reveals the evaluative structure through which viability-oriented organisation operates.

APS consequently treats perturbation as one of the most powerful diagnostic tools available for investigating agency. When a system reorganises activity in response to viability-relevant disturbance, agency becomes directly observable through the system's own regulatory behaviour.

## Viability Gradients and Viability Landscapes

Perturbation becomes particularly informative when viewed in relation to viability itself. APS proposes that living systems occupy regions of possibility within which continued persistence remains achievable. Outside these regions, viability deteriorates and organisational continuity becomes increasingly difficult to maintain.

This perspective allows diagnostic investigation to move beyond simple binary distinctions between life and non-life. Instead of asking only whether a system is viable, APS asks how viability changes under different conditions and how systems respond to those changes. The resulting patterns can be understood as viability gradients.

A viability gradient describes how changes in internal or external conditions alter a system's capacity to maintain organised persistence. Some changes may have little effect, while others may progressively reduce the system's ability to sustain itself. Still others may trigger compensatory responses that restore viability despite disruption.

When these gradients are considered collectively, they form a broader viability landscape. This landscape represents the range of conditions within which a system can continue to persist, the regions in which viability becomes increasingly difficult to maintain, and the thresholds beyond which organisational continuity breaks down.

Viability landscapes provide a common framework through which organisational signatures and perturbation responses can be interpreted together. Organisational signatures indicate the presence of viability-oriented organisation within a particular region of the landscape, while perturbation responses reveal how that organisation navigates changing conditions. Together they provide a richer understanding of the relationship between agency and persistence.

## Why Signatures and Perturbations Must Be Integrated

Neither organisational signatures nor perturbation responses are sufficient on their own to diagnose agency comprehensively. Each provides access to a different aspect of viability-oriented organisation, and each becomes more informative when interpreted alongside the other.

Signatures identify candidate systems. They reveal organisational patterns that suggest the presence of viability-oriented activity. Homeostatic regulation, adaptive plasticity, self-repair, and constraint closure all provide evidence that a system actively contributes to its own persistence. Yet signatures alone can sometimes be difficult to interpret. Certain forms of self-organisation may exhibit superficially similar patterns without possessing the regulatory dynamics characteristic of living systems.

Perturbations provide a different form of evidence. They reveal how systems respond when viability becomes threatened. However, perturbation responses are most meaningful when interpreted within an organisational context. A response can only be understood as viability-oriented if the organisational conditions that make viability possible are already known.

APS therefore treats signatures and perturbations as complementary forms of evidence. Signatures identify the organisational structures associated with agency, while perturbations reveal the activity through which those structures are maintained. One provides evidence of organisational continuity; the other reveals the dynamics of organisational maintenance.

Agency becomes diagnostically tractable when both forms of evidence converge. A system exhibiting organisational signatures consistent with viability-oriented activity and demonstrating regulatory responses to viability-relevant perturbations provides substantially stronger evidence of agency than either form of evidence alone.

The central APS insight is therefore not that diagnosis requires signatures or perturbations, but that both are observational windows onto the same underlying phenomenon: viability-oriented agency.

## Diagnosing Marginal Cases

The integration of organisational signatures and perturbation responses becomes particularly valuable when investigating marginal or controversial cases. Many systems occupy regions near the boundaries of conventional biological classifications, making diagnosis difficult when based solely on component lists or traditional definitions of life.

Viruses provide a familiar example. Viral systems possess continuity, heredity, and evolutionary capacity, yet their dependence upon host organisms raises questions about whether they constitute autonomous living systems. Organisational signatures may reveal some aspects of viability-oriented organisation, while perturbation-based investigation can help determine the extent to which regulatory activity is genuinely attributable to the virus itself rather than to the host system within which it operates.

Protocells, synthetic biological constructs, and other experimentally created systems present similar challenges. Such systems may exhibit some signatures associated with living organisation while lacking others. Perturbation analysis allows investigators to examine whether these systems actively preserve viability under changing conditions or merely display transient forms of self-organisation.

APS therefore replaces binary classification with a more nuanced diagnostic framework. Rather than asking whether a system possesses a fixed set of defining properties, APS investigates the degree to which organisational signatures and perturbation responses jointly support the presence of viability-oriented agency. Marginal cases become scientifically informative precisely because they illuminate the conditions under which agency emerges, persists, and breaks down.

## Life Detection Beyond Earth

The same diagnostic framework extends naturally to astrobiology and the search for life beyond Earth. One of the central challenges of life detection is that direct observation of agency may be impossible. Investigators often have access only to traces, residues, environmental modifications, or other indirect forms of evidence.

In such contexts, organisational signatures become especially important. Persistent chemical disequilibria, complex boundary structures, self-maintaining environmental patterns, and other forms of organised continuity may provide evidence that viability-oriented activity has occurred. These signatures function as indicators of agency even when the underlying system cannot be directly observed.

Yet APS also recognises the limitations of signatures alone. Many apparent biosignatures may have non-biological explanations. For this reason, APS interprets life detection as a process of evaluating converging lines of evidence rather than identifying a single decisive marker. The stronger the integration among organisational signatures, the stronger the inference that viability-oriented activity may be present.

Where perturbation-based investigation becomes possible, diagnostic confidence can increase substantially. Experimental manipulation, environmental monitoring, or longitudinal observation may reveal regulatory responses consistent with agency. In this way, APS provides a framework capable of integrating indirect and direct forms of evidence within a unified theory of life detection.

The significance of this approach extends beyond astrobiology. It demonstrates that agency can remain diagnostically accessible even when conventional biological assumptions concerning chemistry, morphology, or evolutionary history are unavailable.

## Toward an Empirical Programme of Agency

The broader significance of APS lies in its transformation of agency from a philosophical concept into an empirical research programme. If agency is the defining activity of life, then scientific investigation requires methods capable of identifying, evaluating, and comparing agency across diverse systems and contexts.

Organisational signatures provide one route to empirical access. They reveal the stable organisational patterns produced by viability-oriented activity and allow investigators to identify candidate living systems. Perturbation responses provide another route. They reveal the regulatory dynamics through which living systems preserve viability and expose the evaluative structure that guides their activity.

Together these approaches establish a unified framework for diagnosis. Signatures reveal the organisational footprints of agency, while perturbations reveal agency in operation. Neither replaces the other. Instead, both contribute complementary evidence concerning the same underlying phenomenon.

This integration offers a common framework for biological diagnosis, life detection, synthetic biology, origins-of-life research, and the investigation of marginal cases. More fundamentally, it demonstrates that viability-oriented agency is not merely a theoretical abstraction but an empirically tractable feature of living systems.

APS therefore reframes diagnosis itself. The central question is no longer whether a system possesses a predefined list of biological traits. The question becomes whether organisational evidence and regulatory evidence converge upon the existence of a system actively engaged in maintaining the conditions of its own persistence.
