## Introduction

What are living systems doing when they remain alive?

Biology answers this question indirectly. Organisms regulate internal conditions, acquire resources, repair damage, coordinate development, respond to environmental change, and reproduce. These activities are investigated through physiology, ecology, development, evolution, and behaviour. Beneath this diversity, however, lies a more fundamental question: what unifies these activities as expressions of life?

This article answers this question through the concept of biological agency.

Agency is not a specialised capacity possessed only by animals, nor is it synonymous with behaviour, cognition, intelligence, or conscious intention. Biological agency is the viability-oriented activity through which living systems continually maintain and re-establish the conditions required for their own organised persistence. Organisms remain alive because they continuously participate in maintaining themselves.

Within APS, this understanding gives agency a foundational explanatory role. Living systems do not merely persist. They actively contribute to maintaining the organisation that makes persistence possible. A bacterium regulating its internal chemistry, a plant reorganising growth in response to changing conditions, and an animal repairing damaged tissue each participate in agency because each contributes to sustaining viability. Their mechanisms differ profoundly, but the underlying organisational principle remains the same.

Within APS, agency is understood as the defining activity through which life is continually sustained. Agency maintains viability. Through viability, living systems sustain organised persistence. Organised persistence provides the explanatory target through which biology seeks to understand living systems. Agency is therefore not an additional characteristic that appears once life already exists. It is the ongoing activity through which living organisational activity sustains the conditions of its own persistence.

Understanding agency in this way clarifies why living systems possess functions, why biological processes can succeed or fail, why normativity arises within biology, and why biological explanation differs from explanations of non-living systems. Agency provides the organisational foundation through which these phenomena become conceptually connected.

Whereas Agency as the Defining Activity of Life establishes the scientific basis for treating agency as the defining activity of life, the present article develops the APS account of biological agency through self-maintenance, viability, organised persistence, function, normativity, and biological explanation. Its purpose is not to defend the existence of agency but to clarify how agency functions within the explanatory architecture of APS.

## Why Agency Requires Clarification

Biology has achieved extraordinary explanatory success in revealing how living systems function. It explains metabolism, development, physiology, evolution, ecology, behaviour, and countless other aspects of biological organisation with increasing precision. Yet the success of these disciplines does not by itself resolve a more fundamental conceptual question: what unifies these diverse activities as the characteristic activity of life?

Biologists routinely describe organisms as regulating, repairing, adapting, developing, reproducing, and interacting with their environments. These descriptions identify important biological processes, but they do not by themselves make explicit the organisational activity through which these processes collectively sustain a living system. The concept of biological agency remains widely employed across the life sciences, yet its meaning often shifts between behaviour, autonomy, control, goal-directedness, cognition, or decision-making according to disciplinary context. The result is not conceptual failure but conceptual plurality.

 This plurality reflects an underlying explanatory question that has not always been explicitly distinguished from the many biological phenomena through which it is expressed. The issue is not whether organisms act, but what kind of activity their actions collectively instantiate. Clarifying biological agency therefore requires identifying the organisational activity common to the diverse processes through which living systems maintain themselves.

Within APS, biological agency is not introduced as an additional biological mechanism, a competing theory, or a replacement for existing explanations. Rather, it provides a comparative explanatory concept through which the activities already investigated across biology can be understood as complementary expressions of viability-oriented living organisation. Agency identifies the ongoing activity through which organisms continually sustain the conditions required for their own organised persistence.

Clarifying agency in this way also clarifies its relationship to neighbouring biological concepts. Function describes the present contribution of traits and processes to viability. Development investigates the continuing reorganisation of living organisation across an individual life history. Evolution explains the historical transformation of biological organisation across generations. Ecology examines how organisms maintain viability within changing environmental relations. These remain distinct explanatory domains, yet each investigates different aspects of the same continuing organisational activity.

The purpose of this article is therefore not to argue that biology has overlooked agency, nor to replace existing biological explanations with a single organising principle. It is to make explicit the concept of biological agency that enables these diverse explanations to be understood as complementary investigations of the activity through which living systems continually sustain themselves. This clarification provides the foundation for the sections that follow, beginning with a more precise account of what biological agency is and how it functions as the activity of self-maintenance.

## Agency as Activity Rather Than Property

Biological agency is often described as though it were a property that living systems possess. Organisms are said to have agency in much the same way that they have mass, shape, or genetic material. Within APS, however, agency is understood differently. Agency is not a static attribute added to an already living system. It is the continuing activity through which living organisation is enacted and sustained.

This distinction is fundamental because living systems do not remain alive simply by possessing particular structures or components. Every organism continually regulates, repairs, reorganises, exchanges matter and energy with its surroundings, and responds to changing conditions. These activities are not secondary consequences of life; together they constitute the ongoing organisational activity through which living systems maintain themselves. Agency therefore exists only in and through the continuous organisational activity of living systems.

Understanding agency as activity rather than property also explains why it cannot be identified with particular biological capacities such as movement, nervous systems, cognition, intelligence, or conscious intention. A bacterium regulating its internal chemistry, a plant reorganising its growth in response to environmental conditions, and an animal repairing damaged tissue all exhibit biological agency despite profound differences in their mechanisms. What unites them is not a shared behavioural repertoire but a common organisational activity directed towards maintaining viability.

Agency is therefore understood as something organisms continually enact rather than something they simply possess. Agency is realised through the ongoing modulation of the organisational constraints that maintain the conditions required for continued living. It is inseparable from the continuous activity through which organisms sustain themselves despite continual internal and external change.

Viewing agency in this way also clarifies its relationship to the organisation of life itself. If agency were merely a property of living systems, it would remain unclear how that property contributes to biological continuity. Under the APS framework, by contrast, agency is the present-tense activity through which living organisation continually maintains and re-establishes the conditions of its own persistence. The explanatory focus therefore shifts from identifying a characteristic possessed by organisms to understanding the activity through which biological organisation is continuously realised.

This distinction prepares the way for the sections that follow. Having established that agency is an ongoing organisational activity rather than a static property, the next section examines how this activity is expressed through the continual self-maintenance of living systems.

## Self-Maintenance and Viability

If biological agency is an ongoing organisational activity rather than a property possessed by living systems, then that activity must itself be identified. Within APS, the defining activity of biological agency is self-maintenance organised relative to viability. Living systems continually maintain and re-establish the conditions required for their own continued organisation despite persistent internal change and environmental perturbation.

Self-maintenance is not simply the preservation of existing structures. Living organisms continuously replace molecular components, reorganise physiological processes, regulate exchanges with their surroundings, repair damage, and modify their organisation in response to changing conditions. The persistence of a living system therefore consists not in static continuity but in the continual renewal and reorganisation through which its viability is maintained.

Viability provides the normative reference for this activity. Biological organisation is not evaluated according to an external purpose or predetermined design but according to whether it contributes to maintaining the conditions under which the organism can continue to exist as a living system. Activities that sustain those conditions contribute positively to viability; activities that undermine them contribute negatively. In this sense, viability establishes the biological asymmetry through which the organisation of living systems becomes intelligible.

Agency is therefore expressed through the continual organisational activity of self-maintenance. Organisms do not first exist as complete entities and subsequently engage in self-maintenance. Rather, their continuing existence depends upon the uninterrupted organisational activity through which viability is sustained. Agency is realised in the ongoing regulation of the relationships, processes, and constraints that enable living organisation to continue through change.

Understanding self-maintenance in this way also clarifies why biological agency differs fundamentally from the behaviour of non-living systems. A flame may persist while fuel remains available, and a river may maintain a recognisable course despite continual material change, but neither actively maintains the conditions of its own continued organisation. Living systems, by contrast, continually generate and regulate the organisational conditions upon which their own viability depends. Self-maintenance is therefore not merely persistence through time; it is the continuing biological activity through which living organisation actively maintains itself.

This account prepares the transition to the next stage of the argument. Self-maintenance explains the present activity through which agency is enacted. The following section shows how this continuing activity constitutes organised persistence and why organised persistence becomes the primary explanatory target of biological explanation within APS.

## Agency and Organised Persistence

Self-maintenance explains the ongoing activity through which biological agency is enacted. Organised persistence explains what that activity continuously achieves. These concepts are inseparable within living systems yet remain explanatorily distinct. Agency identifies the viability-oriented organisational activity through which living systems continually maintain themselves; organised persistence identifies the continuing organisation sustained through that activity.

Organised persistence should not be understood as a static condition or an enduring state. Living systems persist only because they continuously renew the organisational conditions required for their own continued existence. Molecules are replaced, cells divide, tissues remodel, physiological processes fluctuate, and environmental conditions continually change. Despite this continual transformation, the organism remains organised as the same living system because its agency continually sustains the organisation upon which its viability depends.

This distinction clarifies why persistence is itself a biological achievement rather than a passive consequence of existence. A living organism does not simply continue through time because it possesses particular structures or components. It continues because its ongoing agency continually regenerates, regulates, and reorganises the organisational conditions required for viability. Organised persistence is therefore neither a fixed structure nor an independently operating process. It is the continuing organisation constituted and sustained through the activity of biological agency.

Understanding organised persistence in this way also clarifies its explanatory significance. Biological disciplines investigate many different aspects of living systems, including metabolism, development, physiology, ecology, evolution, behaviour, and cognition. Each of these domains contributes to explaining how living systems maintain themselves under particular conditions. Organised persistence provides the comparative explanatory target through which these diverse investigations can be understood as complementary explanations of the continuing organisation of life rather than as isolated biological phenomena.

Agency and organised persistence therefore stand in a relationship of mutual explanatory dependence without being conceptually identical. Agency explains the present-tense organisational activity through which living systems continually maintain themselves. Organised persistence explains the continuing organisation realised through that activity across time. Neither concept can be fully understood in isolation. Agency without organised persistence would lack an enduring organisational outcome, while organised persistence without agency would become an unexplained condition rather than a continuing biological achievement.

This relationship is central to the explanatory architecture of APS. Biological explanation is directed not merely towards identifying mechanisms or describing biological processes, but towards understanding how diverse biological activities collectively contribute to the continuing organisation through which living systems maintain themselves. Agency therefore provides the activity of life, while organised persistence provides the continuing organisational phenomenon that biological explanation seeks to understand.

## Agency Across Biological Scale

Biological agency is always realised through the activities of living systems acting in the present. Every act of regulation, repair, growth, development, or environmental interaction occurs through local biological processes. Yet the organisational conditions that make these activities viable are not confined to a single spatial or temporal extent. Living organisation is continually constituted across multiple extents whose coordinated relationships enable organisms to maintain themselves through change.

Within APS, this organisational distribution is examined through the explanatory perspective of Scale. Scale does not divide biological reality into independent hierarchical levels or separate forms of agency. Rather, it asks where the organisation required for organised persistence is established, maintained, and transformed across the spatial and temporal extents that constitute living systems. Scale therefore identifies an explanatory perspective on biological organisation rather than a classification of biological entities.

This perspective clarifies why no single organisational extent is sufficient to explain the persistence of living systems. Cellular processes depend upon tissue organisation, tissues function within organisms, organisms maintain themselves within ecological relations, and evolutionary history explains how present forms of organisation have been transformed across generations. These organisational relations remain biologically distinct, yet together they contribute to the continuing conditions under which agency maintains viability. Understanding biological organisation therefore requires attention both to local activities and to the wider organisational contexts within which those activities are continually sustained.

APS does not treat wider organisational extents as exerting independent causal control over local biological processes. Local causation remains the means through which biological activity is realised. Wider organisational relations instead modify the conditions under which local causal interactions occur by establishing, maintaining, or transforming the organisational contexts that make particular biological activities possible. Scale therefore complements rather than replaces mechanistic explanation by situating local biological activity within the broader organisation through which living systems persist.

Viewing biological organisation through Scale also clarifies the relationship between present activity and historical continuity. Agency is always enacted in the present, but the organisational conditions that sustain present agency have themselves been established across developmental, ecological, and evolutionary time. Present biological activity therefore expresses an organisation whose continuity extends beyond any individual event while remaining continually dependent upon ongoing agency. Scale provides the explanatory perspective through which this continuity becomes intelligible.

Agency and Scale are therefore complementary projections of the same biological organisation. Agency asks what living systems do to maintain themselves. Scale asks where that organisation is distributed across spatial and temporal extents. Neither perspective supersedes the other. Together they reveal how the continuing activity of biological agency is organised across the full extent of living systems while preserving the primacy of local biological causation.

Understanding agency across Scale prepares the transition to the next section. Once the organisation of biological agency has been situated across spatial and temporal extents, it becomes possible to examine how particular biological activities acquire functional significance relative to viability. The following section therefore considers the relationship between agency, function, and biological normativity.

## Agency, Function, and Normativity

Understanding biological agency as viability-oriented organisational activity clarifies the meaning of biological function. Within APS, functions are not defined simply by the structures that perform them, nor solely by the evolutionary histories through which those structures arose. Rather, a biological function is the present-tense organisational contribution that a trait, process, or activity makes to maintaining the viability of the living system in which it participates.

This perspective follows directly from the conception of agency developed in the preceding sections. If living systems continually maintain themselves through viability-oriented organisational activity, then the significance of any biological activity depends upon its contribution to that continuing organisation. Function therefore expresses how particular biological activities participate in sustaining the conditions required for organised persistence. Evolutionary history may explain how a function emerged, but it does not by itself constitute the function the activity presently performs.

Viewing function in this way also clarifies the origin of biological normativity. Living systems are organised around an intrinsic distinction between activities that contribute to viability and those that undermine it. This distinction is neither imposed from outside the organism nor derived from conscious judgement. It arises from the organisation of living systems themselves. Activities that contribute to maintaining viability are biologically successful in the present organisational context; activities that compromise those conditions are biologically unsuccessful. Normativity therefore reflects the viability-relative asymmetry inherent in living organisation.

Within APS, this organisational asymmetry should not be confused with morality, conscious evaluation, or intentional purpose. Biological normativity concerns what matters for the continued organisation of a living system, not what ought to occur in an ethical or social sense. Nor does it require deliberation, representation, or cognition. Even the simplest organisms distinguish, through their continuing organisation, between conditions that support viability and conditions that threaten it. Biological normativity therefore precedes cognition and provides one of the organisational foundations upon which more complex evaluative capacities subsequently develop.

Agency, function, and normativity are thus inseparable aspects of the same living organisation. Agency identifies the continuing viability-oriented activity through which organisms maintain themselves. Function identifies the present organisational contribution of particular activities to that continuing organisation. Normativity expresses the viability-relative distinction through which those contributions acquire biological significance. These concepts are analytically distinguishable, yet each derives its explanatory role from the same continuing organisation of living systems.

This understanding also clarifies why APS treats purpose as an organisational rather than an intentional concept. Living systems exhibit purposive organisation because their activities are organised towards maintaining viability, not because they consciously anticipate future states or act under external design. Purpose therefore describes the directionality intrinsic to viability-oriented organisation without attributing intention, foresight, or representation to the organism. In this sense, purposiveness is an organisational characteristic of life itself rather than a psychological capacity.

The next section completes this account by examining how viability-oriented organisation is continually maintained through the modulation of organisational constraints. This introduces the APS concept of constraint-closure and explains how agency, function, and normativity are stabilised within the continuing organisation of living systems.

## Agency, Constraint-Closure, and Organisational Maintenance

The continuing activity of biological agency depends upon an organisation capable of maintaining the conditions through which that activity remains possible. Within APS, this organisational architecture is described through the concept of constraint-closure. Constraint-closure explains how living systems continually generate, maintain, and reorganise the constraints that sustain their own viability-oriented organisation. Agency identifies the ongoing activity through which this organisation is enacted; constraint-closure identifies the organisational structure that enables that activity to continue.

Biological constraints should not be understood as external restrictions imposed upon otherwise independent processes. Rather, they are organisational conditions that channel biological activity in ways that contribute to the maintenance of the living system. Enzymatic regulation, membrane organisation, developmental control, physiological regulation, and countless other biological processes operate through networks of mutually supporting constraints. These constraints are continually established, modified, and renewed through the activity of the organism itself. Living organisation therefore persists not because its constraints remain unchanged, but because they are continually maintained and reorganised through ongoing biological activity.

Constraint-closure describes the organisation through which these mutually supporting constraints collectively maintain the conditions required for their own continued operation. This organisation is neither static nor self-sustaining in the absence of agency. The constraints that characterise living systems exist only insofar as they are continually enacted, repaired, and reorganised through viability-oriented organisational activity. Constraint-closure therefore explains the organisational continuity of living systems without reducing that continuity to material permanence or structural invariance.

This relationship clarifies the complementary roles of agency and constraint-closure within APS. Agency explains what living systems do: they continually sustain the conditions required for their own viability. Constraint-closure explains how the organisation required for that activity remains continuously possible. These are not competing explanations but complementary analytical projections of the same living organisation. Agency emphasises the continuing activity of life; constraint-closure emphasises the organisation through which that activity is sustained.

Understanding constraint-closure in this way also clarifies its relationship to organised persistence. Organised persistence is not produced by constraint-closure alone, nor by agency considered independently. Rather, organised persistence is continually constituted through the ongoing interaction between viability-oriented organisational activity and the organisation of constraints that enables that activity to continue. Constraint-closure therefore provides the organisational continuity through which agency can continually sustain the living system despite continual material turnover and environmental change.

This account also distinguishes APS from interpretations that treat constraint-closure as a complete explanation of life. Constraint-closure identifies an indispensable organisational characteristic of living systems, but it does not by itself explain the continuing activity through which living organisation maintains and re-establishes itself. Agency is therefore understood as something organisms continually enact rather than something they simply possess. Neither concept is reducible to the other, and neither is sufficient in isolation.

The next section draws together the arguments developed throughout the article by examining why agency should be regarded as fundamental to understanding life. Having established the relationships among agency, self-maintenance, organised persistence, Scale, function, normativity, and constraint-closure, the discussion turns to the broader explanatory significance of biological agency within APS.

## Why Agency Is Fundamental to Life

The preceding sections have developed a progressively integrated account of biological agency. Agency has been shown to be the viability-oriented organisational activity through which living systems continually maintain themselves. Self-maintenance explains how this activity is expressed, organised persistence explains what it continually achieves, Scale identifies where this organisation is distributed across spatial and temporal extents, function and normativity explain its viability-relative significance, and constraint-closure explains how the organisation required for this activity is continually maintained. Together, these concepts reveal why agency occupies a foundational explanatory position within APS.

Agency is fundamental not because it replaces existing biological explanations, but because it identifies the continuing activity through which those explanations become intelligible as complementary investigations of living organisation. Biological disciplines examine metabolism, physiology, development, evolution, ecology, behaviour, and cognition from different explanatory perspectives. Each contributes essential knowledge about living systems. This article argues that these diverse explanations acquire greater conceptual coherence when understood in relation to t.the ongoing organisational activity through which organisms sustain the conditions of their own viability.

This perspective also clarifies why agency should not be regarded as one biological topic among many. Agency is not confined to behaviour, movement, cognition, or decision-making. Nor is it an additional process operating alongside metabolism, development, or evolution. Rather, it identifies the continual activity through which living organisation is enacted across all of these domains. Agency therefore provides the common explanatory concept through which otherwise diverse biological explanations can be understood as addressing different aspects of the same continuing living organisation.

Understanding agency in this way does not diminish the explanatory achievements of contemporary biology. Mechanistic, developmental, ecological, evolutionary, and physiological explanations remain indispensable. APS does not seek to replace these explanatory traditions or reduce them to a single principle. Instead, it asks how their distinctive explanatory achievements relate through the organisation required for living systems to maintain themselves. Agency therefore functions as the central explanatory concept through which the organisational coherence underlying biology's conceptual diversity becomes explicit.

The explanatory importance of agency also follows from the definition of life adopted throughout APS. If life is understood as viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation, then agency identifies the continuing activity through which that organisation is realised. Without agency there is no ongoing self-maintenance, no organised persistence, no viability-relative function or normativity, and no continual modulation of the constraints that sustain living organisation. Agency is therefore not an additional property possessed by living systems; it is the activity through which living organisation continually exists.

For this reason, APS proposes biological agency as the defining activity of life. This proposal does not alter the empirical content of biological science, nor does it introduce a new biological mechanism. Rather, it provides a comparative explanatory framework through which the diverse explanatory practices of biology can be understood as complementary investigations of the ongoing activity of living organisation. In doing so, APS offers a unified organisational perspective while preserving the explanatory autonomy of the biological disciplines it brings into relation.

This synthesis provides the basis for the final stage of the article. Having established why agency occupies a foundational explanatory position within APS, the concluding section considers the broader implications of this perspective for biological explanation and for the continuing development of theoretical biology.

## Implications for Biological Explanation

The account of biological agency developed throughout this article has implications that extend beyond the definition of a single biological concept. If agency is understood as the ongoing viability-oriented organisational activity through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence, then it also provides a comparative explanatory methodology for understanding how the diverse explanatory practices of biology relate to one another. The significance of APS therefore lies not in proposing a new empirical biology but in clarifying the organisation of biological explanation itself.

Modern biology encompasses a wide range of explanatory traditions. Molecular biology investigates the organisation of biochemical processes, physiology examines the regulation of organismal activity, developmental biology explains the reorganisation of living systems across individual life histories, evolutionary biology accounts for historical transformation across generations, and ecology investigates the relations through which organisms maintain viability within changing environments. Each of these disciplines addresses a distinct aspect of living organisation and retains its own explanatory methods, concepts, and standards of evidence.

APS does not seek to replace this explanatory plurality with a single theory or organising principle. Rather, it proposes that these diverse explanatory achievements become more conceptually coherent when understood as complementary investigations of the continual activity through which living systems maintain viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation. Agency provides the central explanatory concept through which these different explanatory perspectives can be related without diminishing their individual explanatory autonomy.

APS applies this same comparative methodology to contemporary theoretical biology. Frameworks such as the Free Energy Principle investigate important aspects of biological organisation through different explanatory priorities and formal methods. Rather than asking which framework is correct, APS asks what explanatory contribution each makes to understanding viability-oriented organised persistence. This comparative methodology is developed in *APS and Contemporary Theories* and illustrated in *APS and the Free Energy Principle: Complementary Explanatory Frameworks in Theoretical Biology*, where APS compares its account of biological agency with the active inference framework.

This perspective also clarifies the distinction between the organisation of life and the organisation of explanation. Living systems exhibit a unified organisation through the continual activity of agency, yet biological explanations remain appropriately diverse because different explanatory questions require different methods, concepts, and forms of evidence. APS therefore does not seek explanatory uniformity. Instead, it provides an explanatory grammar through which the conceptual relationships among biological explanations become more explicit while preserving their legitimate methodological diversity.

Understanding biological explanation in this way also clarifies the role of theoretical biology. Theoretical biology does not merely develop additional models or hypotheses concerning particular biological phenomena. It also investigates the conceptual organisation through which biological knowledge becomes intelligible as a coherent scientific enterprise. The APS framework contributes to this endeavour by asking how biology's central concepts relate through the continuing activity of living organisation rather than by proposing alternative explanations for the empirical phenomena those concepts describe.

The methodological contribution of APS therefore lies in its comparative explanatory orientation. It asks not whether existing biological explanations are correct, but how they relate as explanations of the continuing activity through which living systems sustain their own viability. In doing so, APS seeks to make the conceptual architecture of biology more explicit while preserving the empirical achievements, explanatory diversity, and methodological pluralism that characterise contemporary biological science.

These implications complete the central argument of the article. The concluding section summarises the principal claims and restates the contribution of biological agency to the explanatory architecture of APS before the article closes with its Explanatory Architecture.

## Conclusion

Biological agency has often been treated as a specialised concept associated primarily with behaviour, cognition, or intentional action. This article develops a different interpretation. Biological agency is not a capacity possessed only by certain organisms, nor an additional property of living systems. It is the continuing viability-oriented organisational activity through which living systems maintain and re-establish the conditions of their own persistence.

Understanding agency in this way clarifies why living systems differ from merely persistent physical structures. Organisms do not simply continue to exist through the persistence of their material components. They continually maintain, regulate, repair, and reorganise the conditions required for their own viability. Self-maintenance is therefore not an additional feature of life but the operational expression of biological agency.

This perspective also brings greater conceptual coherence to many of biology's central explanatory concepts. Organised persistence, Scale, function, normativity, and constraint-closure each illuminate different aspects of the same continuing biological organisation. Rather than replacing existing biological explanations, understanding agency in this way shows how these complementary explanatory perspectives relate through the viability-oriented activity that characterises living systems.

The significance of biological agency therefore extends beyond the definition of a single biological concept. It provides a comparative explanatory methodology through which the diverse explanatory achievements of biology can be understood as complementary investigations of the continuing activity of living organisation. In this way, APS seeks not to replace biology's existing explanatory traditions but to clarify the organisational relationships that connect them.

Life is not merely organised persistence.

Life is viability-oriented, constraint-closed organisation enacted through the continuing activity of biological agency.
