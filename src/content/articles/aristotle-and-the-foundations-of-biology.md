**Where This Article Fits**

*This article belongs to the History of Ideas series, which traces changes in the concepts, evidence and methods through which living nature has been understood. It examines Aristotle's contribution to biological inquiry through observation, comparison, causal explanation and the study of development. It provides a historical point of departure for later articles on the emergence of cell theory, evolutionary explanation and modern biology.*

*Aristotle's attention to living organisation has affinities with questions discussed by the Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework, but his philosophy is considered here on its own terms. Thematic affinities neither make Aristotle a precursor of APS nor establish theoretical equivalence.*

## Introduction

On the shores of the Aegean, the variety of marine life offered Aristotle an extraordinary field of inquiry. Fish, octopuses, cuttlefish, crustaceans and other animals differed in their structures, movements and reproductive habits. They also presented a common intellectual challenge: how could so many forms of life be described systematically, compared and explained? The surviving biological writings of Aristotle (384–322 BCE) show an investigator confronting that challenge with an ambition unusual in the ancient world.

Aristotle is often introduced as a philosopher of logic, ethics and metaphysics. Yet a substantial part of his surviving work concerns animals. *Historia Animalium* (usually rendered *History of Animals*) assembles descriptions of their parts, behaviour and reproduction; *Parts of Animals* asks why their structures are as they are; and *Generation of Animals* examines how new organisms come into being. Together with *On the Soul* (*De Anima*), these works offer a sustained attempt to investigate living nature as a subject with its own explanatory questions.

Aristotle did not invent observation of nature. Physicians, farmers, hunters and earlier natural philosophers possessed considerable knowledge of living things. Nor did he establish biology as a modern institutional discipline. His distinctive achievement was to bring diverse observations into an ambitious programme that connected description with causal explanation. An animal's body was not merely an inventory of parts: its structures had to be understood in relation to its characteristic activities, development and way of life.

The achievement came with profound limitations. Aristotle lacked microscopy, cell theory, genetics and evolutionary theory; some of his anatomical and reproductive claims were wrong. The historical question is therefore not whether his conclusions remain true, but what kind of inquiry his biological writings made possible. To answer it, we must look closely at his observations, his account of causes and the ways in which his followers extended the study of living nature.

## Aristotle's Central Biological Insight

For Aristotle, a living body is more than a quantity of matter arranged into a recognisable shape. Its parts and capacities belong to a particular kind of living thing. A fish's gills, for example, cannot be adequately understood by listing their materials alone; their significance lies partly in the animal's characteristic activities and conditions of life. This way of asking questions connects anatomy with physiology, behaviour and the wider form of life of the organism.

In *On the Soul*, Aristotle uses *psychē*, conventionally translated as 'soul', to identify the principle by which a living body is alive. The term should not be read automatically in the modern sense of an immaterial personal spirit. Aristotle's account is part of his theory of form and matter: the soul is the actuality or form of a natural body capable of life. Different living beings exhibit different capacities. Nutrition and growth belong to plants as well as animals; perception and locomotion characterise many animals; reasoning is distinctive, in his account, of human beings (*De Anima* II).

This framework supplied a way to investigate both diversity and commonality. Organisms could be compared by their parts and capacities without treating them as interchangeable machines. It also generated a demanding explanatory question: not simply *what is present?*, but *why does this kind of living being have these parts and powers?* The answer required empirical investigation as well as philosophical analysis.

## Aristotle's Empirical Programme

Aristotle's zoological writings contain descriptions of marine and terrestrial animals, accounts of reproduction and behaviour, and comparisons of bodily structures. Their information came from several sources, including observations attributed to Aristotle himself and reports from people whose occupations brought them into close contact with animals. The precise division between firsthand investigation and testimony is often difficult to reconstruct; the texts should not be mistaken for modern laboratory notebooks (Balme 1991; Lennox 2001).

Marine animals are particularly prominent. Aristotle distinguishes forms of cephalopods, describes their bodily features and reproductive habits, and attends to differences among fish and other aquatic animals (*History of Animals* I–IX). Some observations are impressively detailed, although their accuracy varies. Leroi (2014) has vividly reconstructed the possible importance of the lagoon near Mytilene to this work; aspects of that reconstruction remain historical inference rather than direct documentary evidence.

The classifications are revealing. Aristotle frequently contrasts 'blooded' with 'bloodless' animals, a division that roughly resembles, but is not identical with, the modern vertebrate–invertebrate distinction. He also groups animals by features such as their modes of reproduction and the presence or absence of particular structures. These are neither evolutionary genealogies nor a modern taxonomic hierarchy. They are attempts to discover order in anatomical and reproductive variation (Pellegrin 1986).

A notable feature of this research is the movement from description towards explanation. *History of Animals* supplies observations and distinctions that can be brought to bear on the questions pursued in *Parts of Animals*. The separation is not absolute, but it reveals a recognisable scientific practice: establishing what happens or what is present before asking why it is so. Aristotle's discussions of dissection and internal anatomy are part of this endeavour, although the precise methods and frequency of his dissections cannot always be established.

Comparison was especially productive. Gills and lungs, different kinds of limbs, and variations in teeth or digestive structures invited questions about the relation between an animal's bodily equipment and its activities. Aristotle sometimes recognised similarities of role across anatomically different structures without possessing a theory of common descent. His comparisons consequently illuminate both the power and the historical limits of functional anatomy.

There was also a social dimension to the enterprise. Aristotle taught and worked in an intellectual community associated with the Lyceum, and Theophrastus continued related investigations. Claims about the scale and organisation of a coordinated research institution should nevertheless be made cautiously: the surviving evidence is less complete than a modern account of a scientific establishment might suggest (Lloyd 1968; Lennox 2001).

The lasting methodological contribution is not that every observation was reliable. It is the deliberate connection of accumulated evidence, systematic comparison and the search for causes. Aristotle made living things objects of sustained explanatory investigation rather than merely sources of practical information or philosophical illustration.

## Teleology and Biological Explanation

Aristotle's account of causation is essential to understanding his biology. The traditional phrase 'four causes' can be misleading to modern readers, because the Greek *aitia* concerns explanatory factors or answers to different kinds of 'why' question. Aristotle distinguishes what something is made of (material cause), what it is or the form defining it (formal cause), the source of its coming-to-be or change (efficient cause), and that for the sake of which it develops or acts (final cause). These are not necessarily four separate events acting upon an object.

A living plant offers a useful, though simplified, example. Its tissues and constituents are relevant to material explanation. Its characteristic organisation and capacities are relevant to formal explanation. Its generation and development require an account of the processes and sources through which it comes into being. Its mature activities and characteristic ends enter into a final-causal account. This schematic example is an interpretation of Aristotle's explanatory framework, not a substitute for the distinctions and qualifications in his texts.

The final cause is especially important in *Parts of Animals*. Aristotle asks, for example, why an animal possesses a structure suited to a characteristic activity. The answer is not necessarily that anyone planned or consciously desired the structure. A natural end belongs to his account of the living being itself. Nor does final causation remove the need to explain the materials and processes through which a part develops. His biological arguments frequently combine several kinds of explanation.

This does not make Aristotle's teleology equivalent to contemporary accounts of biological function. Modern explanations may invoke natural selection, developmental mechanisms and evolutionary history, none of which forms part of Aristotle's theory. Moreover, scholars disagree about the scope and precise interpretation of his natural teleology. Gotthelf (1987), Johnson (2005), Lennox (2001) and Leunissen (2010) illuminate different aspects of its relationship to form, necessity and biological investigation.

A further distinction matters. Aristotle recognised that some features arise from material necessity or as consequences of other arrangements rather than directly 'for the sake of' an end. His explanatory practice was therefore more discriminating than the caricature in which every natural detail is assigned a purpose. The tension between material constraints, developmental processes and functional explanation makes his biology philosophically interesting even where its particular answers have been superseded.

## The Organism as an Integrated Whole

Aristotle's account of the organism follows from his conception of form and function, but it raises a distinct issue: what makes a bodily part the part that it is? A hand is not defined simply by having a certain outline or being made of flesh and bone. In his discussions of form and the living body, Aristotle uses the example of a severed or lifeless hand to argue that something which cannot perform the characteristic work of a hand is a hand only in a qualified, homonymous sense. The example expresses a relation between a part's identity and its place in a functioning whole.

This is not the claim that an organism exists before its parts in chronological time. It concerns explanatory priority. We may need to know what an animal does, and how it lives, to understand why its structures have their particular character. An isolated inventory of bones, muscles and tissues cannot answer every question about locomotion, feeding or perception.

Aristotle's treatment of animal movement provides a concrete illustration. In *Movement of Animals* and *Progression of Animals*, he examines bodily arrangements involved in locomotion and employs mechanical comparisons, including those involving joints and leverage. These discussions show that attention to the whole organism did not require him to ignore local physical interactions. The question was how such interactions participated in the activities of the animal.

The historical importance of this approach should not be overstated. Aristotle did not possess a modern concept of physiological regulation, nor did he formulate systems biology in advance. His form–matter framework, species assumptions and accounts of causation differ substantially from contemporary science. What survives as a productive question is the problem of relating parts, processes and organism-level activities without assuming that any one description is sufficient by itself.

## Embryology, Generation, and Development

Aristotle's inquiry did not stop with the fully formed animal. *Generation of Animals* asks how a new living being develops and why its parts appear in a particular sequence. This brought a temporal dimension to the investigation of bodily organisation.

His discussion of the developing chick is a celebrated example. Aristotle describes examining eggs at different stages of incubation and reports early visible signs associated with the heart and circulation. The observations are not a modern embryological chronology, and their interpretation was shaped by his physiological theories. Nevertheless, the method of examining successive developmental stages made an important question accessible to investigation: how does an animal's structure become differentiated over time?

Aristotle favoured an account in which bodily parts arise progressively rather than being present in their completed form from the beginning. It would be misleading, however, to portray this simply as a rejection of the much later early-modern doctrine of preformation, in which a miniature organism was supposed to exist already formed. Ancient disputes about generation had their own terms and intellectual setting. Aristotle's account is better described in relation to those disputes and to his understanding of form, matter and the causes of development.

His theory also contained serious errors. In particular, his explanation of male and female contributions to generation assigned them sharply unequal causal roles and did not recognise the mechanisms of heredity established by modern biology. His claim that the heart was the first organ to form cannot be transferred uncritically into contemporary embryology. The observational programme and the theoretical interpretation must therefore be assessed separately.

Development, for Aristotle, was directed towards the characteristic mature form of the organism. That account linked embryology to teleology, but it did not provide a modern explanation of cellular differentiation or gene regulation. Its significance lies in bringing the origin of bodily structure within the scope of natural inquiry. The embryo was not merely an incomplete adult; it was a developing living being whose changing features could be observed, compared and explained.

## Theophrastus and the Birth of Botany

Theophrastus (c. 371–c. 287 BCE), Aristotle's associate and successor at the Lyceum, extended systematic natural inquiry to plants. His *Enquiry into Plants* and *Causes of Plants* are major surviving sources for ancient botanical knowledge. They deserve attention in their own right, rather than simply as botanical applications of Aristotle's zoology.

The *Enquiry* distinguishes trees, shrubs, undershrubs and herbs, while examining plant parts, modes of reproduction, habitats and useful products. Theophrastus discusses variation within familiar kinds of plants and observes how cultivated and wild forms may differ. The *Causes* addresses processes such as growth, fruiting and propagation, together with the influence of soil, water, climate and cultivation. The distinction between describing plant diversity and explaining plant performance is not rigid, but it helps to reveal the range of his interests.

Botany posed problems that zoological analogies could not resolve easily. Plants differ from animals in mobility, the visibility of their reproductive processes, and their dependence on local growing conditions. Theophrastus therefore had to attend closely to seasonality, environmental variation and practical knowledge of cultivation. His accounts are historically valuable partly because they record a world in which natural history and agriculture informed one another.

Theophrastus was not a modern experimental plant physiologist, and the surviving treatises contain mistakes and inherited assumptions. Even so, his work broadened the empirical reach of ancient natural science. It made the study of plant diversity and plant–environment relationships an extensive subject of inquiry rather than a brief appendix to animal anatomy.

## Limits, Errors, and Historical Transformation

Aristotle's biology must be understood within its intellectual and material conditions. He lacked the instruments and concepts needed to investigate cells, microorganisms, chromosomes or molecular processes. His reproductive physiology was mistaken in important respects, and some observations were inaccurate or based on unreliable testimony. His natural philosophy also did not contain a theory of species transformation by common descent and natural selection.

The familiar claim that Aristotle believed species were simply 'fixed' needs qualification. His biological explanations commonly appeal to the reproduction and persistence of characteristic forms, and he did not advance a Darwinian account of evolutionary descent. But the complexity of his discussions of variation, generation and kinds of animals should not be reduced to a single modern slogan about species fixity.

His subsequent reception was equally complex. Aristotelian writings were transmitted, translated, commented upon and adapted in different intellectual traditions. Medieval and early-modern scholars did not all interpret them in the same way. Teleological explanations could be incorporated into theological accounts, but Aristotle's own natural ends should not be confused with every later doctrine that drew upon his language. Equally, the rise of mechanical philosophy did not simply eliminate questions about function from the study of organisms.

Modern biology changed the terms of explanation through microscopy, cell theory, evolutionary theory, experimental physiology, genetics and molecular biology. Natural selection supplied a historical explanation for many adaptations; embryology revealed cellular processes unavailable to ancient investigators; and comparative biology acquired methods for reconstructing evolutionary relationships. These were not merely refinements of Aristotle's programme. They transformed what counted as evidence, how biological causes were identified and what could be inferred from similarities among organisms.

Yet historical discontinuity need not imply that every earlier question disappears. How should anatomical structures be compared? What counts as an adequate explanation of development? How do bodily parts participate in the activities of a living animal? Aristotle formulated versions of such questions within a framework very different from ours. His importance lies partly in the durability of the questions and partly in the contrast between his answers and those made possible by later science.

## Conclusion: Why Aristotle Still Matters

Aristotle's biological writings offer a rare opportunity to observe an early and sustained attempt to make living nature scientifically intelligible. They bring together detailed animal descriptions, comparisons of structure and behaviour, arguments about causes, and observations of development. The resulting body of work is neither a modern biology in embryo nor a collection of merely speculative doctrines.

Its most instructive feature is the relationship between empirical ambition and philosophical commitment. Aristotle's observations sometimes challenged easy generalisations, while his assumptions about form, function and generation also shaped what he thought he saw. Recognising both sides gives a more historically useful account than either celebrating him as a precursor of modern science or dismissing him because many of his conclusions were wrong.

Theophrastus's botanical investigations show how this style of inquiry could be extended and transformed. Later traditions would preserve, reinterpret and sometimes reject Aristotle's explanatory categories. The history that followed was not a straight line of progress from ancient insight to modern confirmation; it involved new instruments, evidence, concepts and disputes.

Aristotle matters because he made the living world the subject of sustained questions about observation, comparison, causation and development. The answers have changed profoundly. The intellectual challenge of explaining living beings has not.
