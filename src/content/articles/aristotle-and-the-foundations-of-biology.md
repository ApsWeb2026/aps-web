**Where This Article Fits**

*This article belongs to the History of Ideas series, which traces the changing concepts, evidence and methods through which living nature has been understood. It examines Aristotle's foundational contribution to biological inquiry: the systematic study of organisms through observation, comparison, function, development and causal explanation.*

*The article provides a historical starting point for later accounts of the emergence of cell theory, evolutionary explanation and modern biological science. Although Aristotle's emphasis on organised living wholes has affinities with questions considered by the Agency–Process–Scale (APS) framework, his philosophy is examined here on its own historical terms. Such affinities do not imply that Aristotle anticipated APS or that the two approaches are theoretically equivalent.*


## Introduction

Aristotle’s contribution to biology is frequently acknowledged yet insufficiently appreciated. While he is often remembered primarily as a metaphysician or logician, Aristotle devoted an extraordinary portion of his intellectual energy to the systematic investigation of living beings. His biological writings—most notably *Historia Animalium*, *Parts of Animals*, and *Generation of Animals*—constitute the earliest surviving corpus of sustained, organised inquiry into animal life. In scope, methodological ambition, and explanatory depth, these works establish biology as a distinct domain of scientific investigation.

Charles Darwin, in a well-known remark, referred to Aristotle as the “father of biology.” The comment is striking not because Aristotle anticipated modern evolutionary theory—he did not—but because Darwin recognised in Aristotle something structurally foundational: the attempt to understand living beings through systematic observation, comparative analysis, and explanatory reasoning appropriate to their distinctive mode of organisation. Aristotle did not merely catalogue animals; he sought to explain why they possess the structures they do, how their parts relate to one another, and how development unfolds over time.

In the fourth century BCE, such a project was unprecedented. Pre-Socratic natural philosophers had investigated cosmology and elemental processes; physicians had studied human anatomy and disease. Aristotle, however, treated animals—and, through his student Theophrastus, plants—as objects of disciplined inquiry in their own right. He assembled reports from fishermen, hunters, and travellers; he conducted dissections; he compared anatomical structures across species; and he sought regularities underlying biological diversity. His biological works are marked by an empirical attentiveness that sits uneasily with later caricatures of Aristotle as primarily deductive or speculative.

Yet Aristotle’s significance does not lie in empirical accumulation alone. It lies equally in his articulation of a distinctive form of explanation appropriate to living beings. In the biological treatises, Aristotle deploys the doctrine of the four causes—material, formal, efficient, and final—not as abstract metaphysical categories, but as tools for understanding organised life. In particular, his use of final causation in biology does not invoke external design; rather, it refers to the functional organisation of parts within the living whole. Organs are explained “for the sake of” the organism—not in the sense of conscious intention, but in virtue of their contribution to the organism’s characteristic mode of life. In this way, Aristotle established functional explanation as central to biological reasoning.

At the same time, Aristotle was not immune to error. His embryological theories lack the resources of genetics and cell theory; his rejection of evolutionary transformation reflects the intellectual horizon of his era; and some anatomical claims are demonstrably incorrect. The significance of Aristotle’s biology, therefore, cannot be measured by empirical accuracy alone. Its enduring importance lies in the conceptual architecture it established: the recognition that living beings require forms of explanation that attend to organisation, development, and function, and that these explanations are neither reducible to physics nor detachable from empirical investigation.

This essay examines Aristotle’s biological corpus in order to clarify how he founded biology as a scientific discipline. It will consider his empirical methods, his conception of teleology, his treatment of the organism as an integrated whole, his embryological investigations, and the extension of his programme through Theophrastus’ botanical work. The aim is not to portray Aristotle as proto-modern, nor to overlook his limitations, but to show how the basic structure of biological explanation—as concerned with organised, living wholes—was first systematically articulated in his work.

## Aristotle's Central Biological Insight

Aristotle's great insight was that life is not simply a collection of materials or mechanisms, but a distinctive form of organised activity in which the parts of an organism contribute to the functioning of the whole. He understood living beings as natural unities whose characteristic capacities—including nutrition, growth, reproduction, perception and, in humans, reasoning—express different forms of life. In *De Anima*, he described the soul (*psychē*) not as a separate supernatural entity, but as the form or organising principle of a living body: that by virtue of which it is alive.

His biological investigations combined extensive observation, comparison and classification with explanations concerned not only with material composition and efficient causes, but also with organisation, development and purpose (*telos*). Although aspects of his teleology are incompatible with modern biological understanding, Aristotle recognised that understanding an organism requires asking both how its processes operate and what its structures and activities contribute to its characteristic way of life.

Within the wider scientific enterprise, Aristotle consequently gave biology a distinctive intellectual standing. Living nature required systematic empirical investigation and explanations appropriate to its characteristic organisation, rather than being understood solely through principles developed for inanimate matter. His enduring contribution was to establish the study of life as a coherent field of natural inquiry in which organisation, function, development and the unity of the organism are central explanatory concerns.

## Aristotle's Empirical Programme

Aristotle’s biological writings are remarkable not only for their scope but for the empirical orientation that animates them. Far from constructing a purely speculative system, Aristotle undertook what can reasonably be described as the first sustained programme of biological research. The *Historia Animalium* in particular reads as an extensive compendium of observational reports, anatomical descriptions, behavioural accounts, and comparative notes drawn from a wide range of sources. These include his own dissections, reports from fishermen and hunters, travellers’ accounts, and local knowledge gathered from the Mediterranean world (Balme 1991; Lennox 2001).

The sheer breadth of the *Historia Animalium* is striking. Aristotle catalogues marine species with particular care, describing cephalopods, crustaceans, fish, and other aquatic animals in detail (HA I–IX). His accounts of the octopus, cuttlefish, and shark are often based on direct observation, and modern commentators have noted the accuracy of many of these descriptions (Leroi 2014). Aristotle distinguishes species by structural features, modes of reproduction, habitat, and behaviour. He classifies animals into broad groupings such as “blooded” (roughly vertebrates) and “bloodless” (invertebrates), not on arbitrary grounds, but on perceived anatomical and physiological regularities (HA I.6, 490b–491a). While this classification does not correspond to modern phylogenetics, it reflects a systematic attempt to identify stable structural differences across forms of life.

What distinguishes Aristotle’s empirical work is not merely the accumulation of data but its integration into explanatory inquiry. The *Historia Animalium* is not a random collection of curiosities; it functions as a foundation for the more analytically structured works *Parts of Animals* and *Generation of Animals*. As Lennox (2001) has argued, Aristotle’s biological method proceeds from the collection of phenomena (*phainomena*) to the search for explanatory causes appropriate to living beings. The descriptive work of the *Historia Animalium* thus prepares the ground for causal analysis.

Aristotle’s commitment to direct investigation is evident in his willingness to perform dissections and to examine internal structures. In *Parts of Animals*, he frequently refers to anatomical features that can only have been identified through internal examination (PA II–IV). Although his tools were rudimentary and his access to specimens uneven, the methodological significance of dissection is profound: living beings are to be understood through careful inspection of their organisation. This represents a departure from purely theoretical natural philosophy and signals the emergence of a discipline grounded in observation.

At the same time, Aristotle did not confine himself to isolated anatomical facts. He sought patterns across species. The comparative method is central to his biology. For example, he observes regular correspondences between different structures—gills in fish and lungs in terrestrial animals, scales and feathers, horns and other defensive structures (HA I–III). Such comparisons aim to identify functional analogies and structural regularities. The diversity of living forms is not chaotic; it is structured and intelligible.

Importantly, Aristotle’s classification is not merely taxonomic but explanatory. He attempts to account for why particular groups of animals possess certain structures. In *Parts of Animals*, he famously criticises explanations that appeal only to material necessity, insisting that one must also explain “for the sake of what” a structure exists (PA I.1, 639b12–640a5). This insistence emerges from empirical engagement: once one has examined the recurrent association of certain structures with particular modes of life, explanation must address their functional role within the organism. Thus empirical observation and causal reasoning are tightly intertwined.

Aristotle’s empirical programme also extends to behaviour and life history. He describes migratory patterns, reproductive cycles, and habitat preferences. In *Generation of Animals*, he investigates embryological development through observation of chick embryos at successive stages (GA II–III). His account of progressive differentiation—whereby the heart is formed first, followed by other organs—reflects direct examination, even if interpreted through theoretical assumptions now superseded. The methodological point remains significant: development is a structured process that can be studied through sequential observation.

Modern scholars have emphasised the extent to which Aristotle’s biological research may have been institutionally organised. The Lyceum appears to have functioned as a research community, gathering specimens and reports systematically (Lennox 2001; Lloyd 1968). This collective dimension reinforces the view that Aristotle’s biology was not incidental but programmatic. It sought comprehensive understanding of living nature.

Armand Leroi (2014), in a vivid reconstruction of Aristotle’s time at the lagoon near Mytilene, underscores the importance of marine research in shaping Aristotle’s thought. The diversity and accessibility of aquatic life provided a natural laboratory for comparative study. Whether or not every detail of Leroi’s narrative can be verified, the central insight remains: Aristotle’s biology emerged from sustained engagement with concrete organisms in their environments.

What ultimately distinguishes Aristotle’s empirical programme is its integration of observation, comparison, and explanation within a coherent framework. He did not merely record what animals look like; he asked why they are organised as they are. He did not treat living beings as accidental aggregations of matter; he regarded them as structured wholes whose parts are coordinated. Nor did he subsume biology entirely under physics. Instead, he recognised that living beings display patterns of organisation that require explanatory tools adapted to their specific mode of being.

In this sense, Aristotle established more than a body of biological knowledge. He established a mode of inquiry. The study of living beings became a systematic, empirically grounded, and explanatorily ambitious enterprise. Later developments in anatomy, physiology, embryology, and evolutionary theory would transform biological understanding beyond recognition. Yet the structural commitment—to observe carefully, to compare across species, and to seek causes appropriate to organised life—can be traced to Aristotle’s biological corpus. It is in this disciplined empirical programme that biology first takes shape as a science.

## Teleology and Biological Explanation

Aristotle’s teleology has often been misunderstood as theological or metaphysically dogmatic. In the biological works, however, teleological explanation is neither an appeal to external design nor a substitute for causal analysis. When Aristotle states that organs exist “for the sake of” particular activities (*Parts of Animals* I.1), he is identifying structured functional relationships internal to the organism. The eye exists for seeing, not because of divine planning, but because vision is constitutive of the animal’s mode of life. Teleology, in this context, expresses organised coordination rather than supernatural intention.

Modern scholarship has clarified this point. Gotthelf (1987) argues that Aristotle’s final causation in biology is immanent to the organism and grounded in its characteristic activities. Johnson (2005) further emphasises that Aristotelian teleology concerns natural function rather than theological purpose. Lennox (2001) situates this teleological framework within Aristotle’s empirical research programme, showing how functional explanation emerges from comparative anatomical investigation. Building on these accounts, Leunissen (2010) demonstrates that teleology in Aristotle’s science of nature operates as an explanatory principle embedded in scientific method itself. She distinguishes between primary teleological explanations, which concern features necessary for the life-form, and secondary teleological considerations relating to the organism’s way of living, thereby revealing the structural role teleology plays in both discovery and justification within Aristotelian natural science.

Teleological explanation in Aristotle therefore complements rather than replaces material and efficient causes. Material necessity constrains what is possible, but it does not explain why a structure has the specific configuration it does. The explanatory task is to understand how material organisation enables characteristic activity. Teleology, properly understood, articulates this internal organisation. Far from being speculative, it reflects Aristotle’s effort to describe living systems as integrated wholes whose parts are coordinated in relation to their life-functions.

## The Organism as Integrated Whole

Aristotle’s biological explanations rest on a conception of the organism as an integrated whole whose parts derive their identity and intelligibility from their role within that whole. This orientation distinguishes his approach from purely material accounts of living beings and underlies his repeated insistence that biological explanation must proceed with reference to organisation rather than aggregation. The organism is not a heap of elements but a structured unity.

One of Aristotle’s most frequently cited claims in the biological works is that “the whole is prior to the part” in the order of explanation (PA I.1). This does not mean that the whole exists temporally before the part; rather, it means that the explanatory identity of a part depends on its function within the organism. A detached hand is no longer properly a hand except “in name” (homonymously), because its defining activity has ceased. The part’s nature is specified by its contribution to the life of the whole.

This position reflects Aristotle’s broader commitment to form (*eidos*) as the organising principle of living beings. In the biological treatises, form is not merely geometric shape but the structured arrangement that enables characteristic activities. To understand an organ, one must understand how its material constituents are organised so as to perform a function appropriate to the organism’s mode of life. Thus the heart, the lungs, and the limbs are not explained simply by the materials of which they are composed, but by the roles they play within a coordinated system.

Aristotle’s insistence on integrated organisation is evident in his treatment of anatomical variation. Differences in structure across species are consistently linked to differences in way of life. Carnivorous animals possess particular dentition; birds exhibit modifications of limbs into wings; aquatic animals display structures suited to respiration in water. These correlations are not treated as accidental. They reflect systematic coordination between structure and activity. The organism is intelligible only when viewed as a dynamic configuration of interdependent parts.

Importantly, this conception resists reduction without rejecting material causation. Aristotle does not deny that bodily structures are composed of matter subject to physical processes. Rather, he argues that material explanation alone is insufficient. The arrangement of parts must be understood in relation to the activities that define the organism. In *Parts of Animals*, he criticises those who explain biological phenomena exclusively through necessity, without accounting for function. Material necessity may constrain what is possible, but it does not determine why a structure has the particular configuration it does.

The organism’s unity is also reflected in Aristotle’s account of nutrition and growth. In *On the Soul* and related biological discussions, he describes the nutritive capacity as fundamental to living beings. Nutrition is not merely intake of matter; it is the maintenance of organised structure. Growth and development occur in accordance with the form of the organism. Matter is assimilated into a pre-existing pattern, rather than randomly accumulated. This reinforces the view that living beings are defined by structured organisation sustained over time.

Aristotle’s analysis of animal motion further illustrates this integration. In *Movement of Animals* and *Progression of Animals*, he examines how bodily structures cooperate to produce coordinated locomotion. Limbs, joints, and muscles operate not independently but in relation to the organism’s overall organisation. Motion is not reducible to isolated mechanical interactions; it reflects the coordinated functioning of a structured whole. Even when Aristotle invokes mechanical analogies, such as levers or joints, these serve to illuminate how parts contribute to unified activity.

This emphasis on integration extends beyond anatomy to reproduction and generation. In *Generation of Animals*, Aristotle treats reproduction as the transmission of form, not merely matter. The developing embryo acquires structure progressively in accordance with a guiding principle intrinsic to the organism. Although his embryological theories lack modern genetic understanding, the conceptual commitment remains consistent: the organism is a self-organising unity whose parts emerge and differentiate in ordered sequence.

Scholars have observed that Aristotle’s organism-centred perspective anticipates later debates about reductionism in biology (Lennox 2001). While modern biological science employs molecular and genetic analysis unavailable to Aristotle, the question of how to relate parts to wholes remains central. Aristotle’s approach does not deny the importance of material constituents; rather, it situates them within an explanatory framework oriented toward organised life.

It is essential, however, not to conflate Aristotle’s conception of the organism with modern systems biology. His framework presupposes fixed species forms and lacks evolutionary dynamics. Moreover, his account of form is embedded within a metaphysical structure that differs from contemporary scientific ontology. Nevertheless, the structural insight—that biological explanation must treat living beings as organised wholes whose parts are functionally interdependent—remains foundational.

In establishing this organism-centred perspective, Aristotle effectively distinguished biology from both pure physics and abstract metaphysics. The study of life required attention to the coordination of parts within structured unities. By insisting that explanation proceed from the whole to the parts, and by grounding functional analysis in observed organisation, Aristotle provided a conceptual model that would shape biological thought for centuries. Even where his specific doctrines have been superseded, the commitment to understanding organisms as integrated systems continues to inform biological inquiry.

## Embryology, Generation, and Development

Among Aristotle’s most remarkable contributions to biology is his sustained investigation of generation and development. In *Generation of Animals*, he turns from the description of mature organisms to the processes by which they come into being. This shift from structure to development reflects a deepening of his commitment to organised explanation: if living beings are structured wholes, their coming-to-be must exhibit corresponding order.

Aristotle’s embryological investigations were grounded in observation, most notably his examination of chick embryos at successive stages of incubation (GA II–III). By breaking open fertilised eggs at different intervals, he sought to determine the sequence in which structures appear. He reports that the heart becomes visible before other organs and that differentiation proceeds gradually rather than instantaneously. Although many details of his account are inaccurate by modern standards, the methodological significance of sequential observation is substantial. Development is treated as a process that unfolds in time according to identifiable stages.

In opposition to theories that posited preformed miniature organisms fully present from conception, Aristotle argued for a model of progressive formation. Structures emerge in ordered succession, guided by the internal principles of the organism. Matter does not spontaneously assemble into a fully formed animal; rather, organisation arises through structured development. This emphasis on gradual differentiation reflects a dynamic conception of life. The organism is not a static configuration but an unfolding pattern of activity.

Aristotle’s account of reproduction centres on the transmission of form. While his understanding of the respective roles of male and female contributions reflects the biological assumptions of his era, the broader conceptual point is clear: generation is not merely the aggregation of material substances but the realisation of organised structure. The developing embryo acquires its defining characteristics in accordance with the species’ form. Even in error, Aristotle maintains consistency with his organism-centred framework: reproduction preserves organised life.

Development also reinforces Aristotle’s commitment to teleological explanation. Embryogenesis proceeds “for the sake of” the mature organism. Organs arise in the order required for the organism’s eventual mode of life. Although this teleological orientation lacks evolutionary grounding, it expresses the view that developmental processes are not random but structured toward characteristic activities. The form of the organism is not imposed from without; it is realised through internal processes.

Importantly, Aristotle does not treat development as purely deterministic in a mechanical sense. He recognises variability and acknowledges cases of abnormal formation. Yet deviations are interpreted against the backdrop of an organised norm. This further illustrates his conviction that biological phenomena are intelligible in relation to structured wholes. Even irregularities presuppose an underlying pattern.

Modern scholars have emphasised the sophistication of Aristotle’s embryological reflections (Lennox 2001). Although lacking microscopic knowledge, he correctly rejected simplistic accounts of spontaneous generation of complex form and instead posited a regulated process of differentiation. His attention to sequence, functional integration, and species continuity situates development at the heart of biological explanation.

At the same time, Aristotle’s developmental framework is constrained by his broader metaphysical commitments. He does not conceive of species as historically transformable; development unfolds within fixed species boundaries. Nor does he possess a concept of genetic inheritance in the modern sense. The explanatory resources available to him were limited by the intellectual and technological conditions of antiquity. Yet within those limits, he articulated a vision of life as organised process rather than static structure.

The integration of development into biological explanation further solidifies Aristotle’s role in founding the discipline. Biology, for him, is not confined to adult morphology. It encompasses the entire trajectory of living beings—from generation to maturation. This longitudinal perspective complements his comparative and anatomical investigations. Together, they present life as dynamic, structured, and intelligible across time.

In emphasising organised development, Aristotle anticipated a recurrent theme in biological thought: the need to understand how complex form arises and is maintained. Later advances in embryology, cell theory, and genetics would transform this inquiry, yet the fundamental question remains recognisably Aristotelian. How does organised life emerge from prior living systems? How are parts coordinated in growth? What governs the sequence of differentiation? Aristotle did not answer these questions in modern terms, but he framed them with clarity and methodological seriousness.

By incorporating development into his explanatory framework, Aristotle ensured that biology would concern not only what living beings are, but how they come to be. In doing so, he extended the study of life beyond static description to dynamic process—a move that would prove indispensable for the future of the discipline.

## Theophrastus and the Birth of Botany

The scope of Aristotle’s biological enterprise extended beyond his own writings. Through the work of his student and successor Theophrastus, the Aristotelian programme expanded to encompass the systematic study of plants. While Aristotle himself focused primarily on animals, Theophrastus’ *Enquiry into Plants* and *Causes of Plants* constitute the earliest comprehensive botanical treatises in Western antiquity. Together, they demonstrate that the biological framework established in the Lyceum was not confined to zoology but applied to living nature more broadly.

Theophrastus adopted and adapted Aristotle’s methodological commitments. Like his teacher, he emphasised careful observation, classification, and causal explanation. In the *Enquiry into Plants*, he catalogues plant species, distinguishing them by structural features such as roots, stems, leaves, and reproductive organs. Classification is not arbitrary; it seeks stable morphological and functional patterns. In the *Causes of Plants*, he investigates growth, reproduction, and environmental influences, exploring how soil, climate, and cultivation affect plant development.

This attention to environmental context marks a significant extension of Aristotelian biology. While Aristotle had considered habitat in his accounts of animals, Theophrastus systematically examined how external conditions interact with internal organisation. Plants are not merely static entities; they are living systems whose development reflects both inherent structure and environmental factors. Such analysis anticipates later concerns in ecology and plant physiology, even though articulated within an ancient conceptual framework.

Importantly, Theophrastus retained the organism-centred and functional orientation characteristic of Aristotle. Plant structures are explained in relation to their roles within the life of the plant as a whole. Roots, leaves, and reproductive structures are not isolated components but coordinated parts of an organised system. The emphasis remains on understanding living beings through structured unity rather than through elemental composition alone.

The continuation of biological research under Theophrastus underscores that Aristotle’s work was not an isolated intellectual achievement but the foundation of an institutionalised scientific programme. The Lyceum functioned as a community of inquiry, gathering observations and pursuing systematic study of living organisms. Through Theophrastus’ botanical investigations, the Aristotelian approach to biology achieved greater breadth, reinforcing the conception of biology as a distinct and comprehensive field of knowledge.

## Limits, Errors, and Historical Transformation

No historically responsible account of Aristotle’s biology can ignore its limitations. His anatomical knowledge was constrained by the absence of systematic human dissection, microscopic instrumentation, and experimental physiology. His embryological theories, though methodologically ambitious, reflect mistaken assumptions about the respective contributions of male and female in reproduction. Most significantly, Aristotle did not conceive of species as historically transformable; his biological framework lacks anything resembling evolutionary theory. These omissions and errors are not peripheral but structurally important. They delimit the scope of his achievement.

Aristotle’s teleology, too, later became entangled in metaphysical and theological interpretations far removed from its biological context. Medieval scholasticism integrated Aristotelian causation into broader theological systems, sometimes obscuring the empirical orientation of the original biological treatises. In early modern science, reaction against such scholasticism contributed to a suspicion of teleological language in natural philosophy. As mechanistic explanations gained prominence, final causation was frequently rejected as unscientific. In this process, Aristotle’s biological project was often caricatured as speculative or pre-critical.

Yet these historical transformations should not obscure the distinctive character of Aristotle’s own work. His teleology in biology does not depend on supernatural intervention, nor does it rest on dogmatic authority. It is grounded in observed regularities of structure and function. Likewise, his conception of form, though embedded within a broader metaphysical system, serves in the biological context to articulate organised unity rather than mystical essence.

Modern biology has radically transformed the empirical and theoretical landscape. The advent of evolutionary theory, cell biology, genetics, and molecular mechanisms has reconfigured explanatory priorities. Species are now understood as historically evolving populations; development is analysed through gene regulation; physiological processes are described at biochemical levels. These advances represent genuine departures from Aristotle’s framework.

Nevertheless, the transformation of biological knowledge did not eliminate the need to understand organisms as integrated systems, nor did it abolish functional explanation. The historical trajectory of biology involves both rupture and continuity. Aristotle’s specific doctrines have often been superseded, but the structural orientation he established—the treatment of living beings as organised wholes requiring domain-specific explanation—remains recognisable. Appreciating this dual reality of limitation and endurance allows Aristotle’s contribution to be assessed without either uncritical celebration or dismissive anachronism.

## Conclusion: Why Aristotle Still Matters

Aristotle did not found biology by discovering evolution, identifying cells, or formulating experimental physiology. His achievement lies elsewhere. He established that living beings constitute a distinct domain of inquiry requiring explanatory principles adapted to their organised character. Through systematic observation, comparative analysis, and causal investigation, he treated animals—and, through Theophrastus, plants—as intelligible objects of disciplined study. In doing so, he gave biology a conceptual and methodological foundation.

Central to this foundation was the recognition that living beings are structured wholes whose parts are coordinated in relation to characteristic activities. Anatomical features are not arbitrary; they are integrated into functional systems. Development unfolds in ordered sequence; reproduction preserves organised life; and explanation must attend not only to material processes but to the organisation they sustain. Aristotle’s teleology, properly understood, articulated this internal coordination rather than invoking external design. His biology thereby established functional explanation as indispensable to the understanding of life.

Subsequent centuries transformed biological knowledge beyond anything Aristotle could have imagined. Evolutionary theory reinterpreted functional explanation in historical terms; cell theory and genetics revealed the mechanisms underlying development; molecular biology illuminated processes invisible to ancient investigators. Yet even within this transformed landscape, the structural questions Aristotle posed remain familiar. How are parts integrated into living wholes? How does development proceed in organised fashion? What distinguishes the study of living beings from the study of inanimate matter? These questions continue to animate biological research.

To recognise Aristotle as foundational is not to claim that he anticipated modern science in detail. It is to acknowledge that he first articulated, in systematic form, the conceptual grammar of biological explanation. He distinguished biology from physics without denying physical processes; he grounded explanation in observation without reducing it to description; and he insisted that living beings are intelligible only through their organised unity. In this sense, Aristotle’s biological writings mark the origin of biology as a scientific discipline.

Understanding this achievement clarifies not only the history of science but the enduring character of biological inquiry. Modern biology rests upon empirical discoveries and theoretical frameworks far removed from the fourth century BCE. Yet the commitment to explaining life as organised, structured, and functionally integrated can be traced to Aristotle’s foundational work. It is in this disciplined articulation of living organisation that his legacy endures.
