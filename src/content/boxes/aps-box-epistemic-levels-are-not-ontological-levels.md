Biologists frequently describe living systems using levels such as molecular, cellular, organismal, and ecological. These distinctions are often useful because scientific investigation requires complex systems to be divided into manageable domains of analysis. Different explanatory levels allow researchers to focus on particular aspects of biological organisation without attempting to explain the entire system at once.

APS accepts the practical value of such levels but rejects the inference that their explanatory usefulness establishes a universal hierarchy of biological reality. Many level distinctions are **epistemic**: they arise from the organisation of inquiry and help structure investigation, communication, modelling, and theory construction. But this does not mean that every level description is merely an epistemic construction.

Some level descriptions can track materially real biological organisation, including structures, boundaries, containment, composition, and part–whole relations. Cells can form parts of tissues, tissues can contribute to organs, and materially significant boundaries can distinguish one organised domain from another. The existence of such relations does not, however, establish that living systems as a whole are organised into discrete tiers possessing fixed causal or explanatory authority.

This distinction is important because explanatory abstractions can be mistaken for ontological structures, while materially real relations can conversely be overlooked if every level distinction is treated as merely conventional. The fact that biology employs molecular, physiological, developmental, ecological, or evolutionary explanations does not by itself establish corresponding levels of existence. What matters is whether a particular distinction tracks materially relevant organisation and what explanatory purpose it serves.

APS therefore distinguishes levels as tools of explanation from claims about the structure of biological reality. A level description may be scientifically useful and may sometimes track materially real biological relations without thereby establishing a universal hierarchy of discrete causal or explanatory levels.

**Key Point.** Epistemic levels organise biological explanation. Some level descriptions may also track materially real structures, boundaries, containment, or part–whole relations. Neither their explanatory usefulness nor their correspondence to such relations establishes a universal hierarchy of discrete causal or explanatory levels.
