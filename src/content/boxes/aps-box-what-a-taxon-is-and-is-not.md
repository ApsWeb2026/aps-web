A taxon in APS is not a static natural unit or a biological process. It is a **classificatory designation used to recognise and communicate historically identifiable patterns of biological continuity and transformation**.

A taxon is not:

- A fixed natural category with essential boundaries
- A biological individual
- A lineage or historical process itself
- A form of constraint-closed organisation
- A collection defined simply by similarity

A taxon may classify biological patterns involving:

- inherited historical continuity
- viability-oriented organisation
- evolutionary divergence and transformation
- relative organisational coherence across time

The distinction is important. The biological systems, lineages, and historical patterns being classified may persist, reorganise, diverge, and transform. The taxon is the **classificatory designation applied to those patterns**, not the biological organisation or process itself.

Traits, genes, phylogenetic relations, and other biological evidence remain important for recognising and distinguishing such patterns. They provide evidence relevant to classification without turning the classificatory designation into an independently existing biological entity.

Taxonomic boundaries may therefore reflect the complexity and continuity of evolutionary history rather than fixed essential divisions in nature. APS uses taxa to describe and communicate historically recognisable patterns without treating the categories themselves as the processes that generated those patterns.

## **Key Point**

A taxon is not a fixed natural unit or biological process. It is a **classificatory designation used to identify and communicate historically recognisable patterns of biological continuity and transformation**.
