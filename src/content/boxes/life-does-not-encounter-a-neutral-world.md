For living systems, the world is never neutral.

Because viability can succeed or fail, some conditions matter more than others. Nutrients differ from toxins. Opportunities differ from threats. Supportive environments differ from hostile ones.

As a consequence, organisms do not encounter merely physical differences. They encounter differences that possess biological significance.

Some conditions support the maintenance and re-establishment of living organisation; others impair or undermine it. The organism's relation to its world is therefore structured by viability-relative differences rather than by physical variation alone.

This organisation is pre-cognitive. Biological significance does not by itself establish cognition. It provides one of the organisational conditions from which cognition may arise when significance becomes integrated across time in ways that contribute additional explanatory work beyond immediate or fixed regulation.

Living systems therefore inhabit relations structured by biological significance before cognitive attribution is warranted.
