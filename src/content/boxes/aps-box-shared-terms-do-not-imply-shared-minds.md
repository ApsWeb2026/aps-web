Biologists often use the same cognitive terms across a wide range of living systems. Organisms as different as bacteria, plants, animals, and humans may all be described as exhibiting memory, learning, communication, anticipation, or decision-making.

APS recognises that such shared terminology can be informative, but it does not imply that these systems possess identical psychological capacities. Similar terms may refer to processes that differ substantially in their mechanisms, complexity, organisation, and experiential character.

What justifies the use of a common term is not the presence of a common mind but the presence of a comparable organisational role. A plant and a human may both exhibit forms of memory because both retain information that influences future activity, yet this does not imply conscious recollection, subjective awareness, or equivalent cognitive architecture.

APS therefore treats terminological continuity as evidence of functional continuity rather than psychological equivalence. Shared vocabulary identifies comparable patterns of organisation without erasing important differences among living systems.

Shared terms should not be mistaken for shared minds.
