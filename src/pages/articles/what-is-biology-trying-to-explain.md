## Introduction - What Is Biology Trying to Explain?

Biology is one of humanity's greatest scientific achievements.

It explains how organisms develop, how traits are inherited, how populations evolve, how cells function, how ecosystems are organised, and how living systems interact with one another and with their environments. Across more than a century of extraordinary scientific progress, biology has transformed our understanding of the living world and continues to reshape medicine, agriculture, conservation, biotechnology, and countless other fields.

Yet the success of biology invites a surprisingly simple question.

**What are all of these explanations ultimately explanations of?**

Biologists investigate genes, cells, organisms, populations, species, ecosystems, behaviour, physiology, development, evolution, and many other aspects of living systems. Each of these fields has developed its own concepts, methods, and explanatory frameworks. Together they form one of the richest and most successful sciences ever created.

But this richness also raises a deeper question.

Are these disciplines simply studying different biological subjects, or are they exploring different aspects of a common phenomenon?

## A Question That Has Never Disappeared

The question is not new.

Since the beginnings of biology as a scientific discipline, researchers and philosophers have debated what should be regarded as its central explanatory focus.

Different traditions have proposed different answers. Some have emphasised organisms, others evolution, genes, mechanisms, functions, information, development, process, autonomy, or organisation. Each has revealed something important about living systems, and each has contributed significantly to biological understanding.

The continuing discussion is therefore not evidence of failure. Rather, it reflects the richness and complexity of life itself. Living systems can be investigated from many complementary perspectives, and each perspective reveals features that others may overlook.

The challenge is to understand how these many perspectives relate to one another.

## The Search for a Common Explanatory Target

Most sciences possess a relatively clear understanding of the kinds of phenomena they seek to explain.

Physics investigates physical systems and their interactions.

Chemistry investigates substances and their transformations.

Geology investigates the Earth and the processes that shape it.

Biology investigates living systems.

But this immediately raises another question.

**What makes living systems the distinctive subject matter of biology?**

The question is not whether biology explains living systems successfully. It plainly does. The question is whether the many explanations developed throughout biology ultimately converge upon a common explanatory target.

If they do, understanding that target may help explain why genetics, physiology, ecology, development, evolution, behaviour, and cognition all belong to a single scientific discipline.

## A Different Way of Asking the Question

The APS Framework begins not by proposing a new biological mechanism or competing with existing biological disciplines, but by asking whether the question itself can be approached differently.

Rather than asking which biological discipline is most fundamental, APS asks whether biology's many successful explanations can be understood as complementary perspectives on one underlying form of living organisation.

From this perspective, biological explanation is concerned not simply with isolated mechanisms or individual processes, but with understanding the organised activity through which living systems develop, maintain themselves, respond to changing conditions, reproduce, and evolve.

Different biological disciplines then become different windows onto the same broader phenomenon. Each contributes to understanding living systems from its own perspective while remaining connected to a common explanatory enterprise.

APS therefore does not seek to replace existing biology. Instead, it asks how biology's many successful explanations can be understood as parts of a coherent account of living systems.

*Every scientific discipline begins by asking what it seeks to understand. This collection begins with the same question for biology.*

## Questions That Follow

Approaching biology in this way naturally leads to further questions.

- What is life?
- What makes an organism an individual?
- What is biological agency?
- How does cognition emerge?
- How should biological function be understood?
- Can purpose exist without external design?

These are among biology's oldest questions. They continue to shape contemporary research across the biological sciences and the philosophy of biology.

The articles in **Biology's Big Questions** explore these questions in greater depth. Together they investigate not only the nature of living systems but also the organisation of biological explanation itself.
